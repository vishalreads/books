## Executive Architectural Summary

Published by Princeton University Press as part of **The James Madison Library in American Politics**, *Richard Nixon: Speeches, Writings, Documents*—edited and incisively introduced by acclaimed historian **Rick Perlstein** (author of *Nixonland* and *Before the Storm*)—provides an unvarnished, forensic primary-source documentary history of the most complex, consequential, and polarizing figure in modern American political history.

Richard Milhous Nixon (1913–1994) was the architect of modern American political reality. From his entrance into Congress in 1946 through his resignation in the shadow of impeachment in 1974, Nixon reshaped the ideological landscape of the United States. He invented the modern political television broadcast with the **"Checkers Speech" (1952)**, confronted Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev in the **"Kitchen Debate" (1959)**, articulated the resentment of the American middle class in the **"Silent Majority" address (1969)**, executed one of the greatest diplomatic masterstrokes in world history with the **Opening to China and Détente with Moscow (1972)**, dismantled the global Bretton Woods monetary system with the **Nixon Shock (1971)**, and ultimately precipitated the gravest constitutional crisis since the Civil War with the **Watergate scandal**.

Perlstein’s critical apparatus demonstrates that Nixon was not an ideological ideologue, but the supreme practitioner of **American Realpolitik**. At home, he weaponized the cultural resentments of the working class against liberal intellectual elites; abroad, he and National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger cast aside moralistic crusades to build a multi-polar, balance-of-power global order modeled on 19th-century European statecraft. Yet the same dark, paranoid suspicions that fueled his political comebacks ultimately trapped him within the walls of the Oval Office, culminating in the fatal taping system that documented his destruction.

This Master Codex synthesizes the 30 primary documentary texts across 10 structured propositional units, presenting a rigorous analysis of power, statecraft, rhetoric, and constitutional limits.

## Unit 1: The Anatomy of Resentment: The Alger Hiss Case & Cold War Populism

### 1.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
How did a quiet lawyer from a poor grocery store in California become a national political giant? In 1948, young Congressman Richard Nixon uncovered that Alger Hiss, a wealthy, Ivy League-educated government official, was secretly a Soviet spy. By exposing Hiss, Nixon didn't just fight communism; he discovered a powerful political formula: showing everyday working-class Americans that wealthy elites in Washington and Harvard were looking down on them and betraying their country.

### 1.2 The HUAC Investigation and the Pumpkin Papers
In August 1948, Whittaker Chambers, a former communist courier turned senior editor at *Time* magazine, testified before the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC):
- Chambers accused **Alger Hiss**—a golden boy of the New Deal, former State Department adviser at the Yalta Conference, and current president of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace—of being a member of an underground communist cell.
- Most committee members, intimidated by Hiss’s suave demeanor, aristocratic pedigree, and elite character witnesses (including Dean Acheson and Felix Frankfurter), wanted to drop the investigation.
- The 35-year-old freshman congressman from California, **Richard Nixon**, refused to back down:
  - Sensing that Hiss’s denials were too legalistic and precise ("I have never known a man named *Whittaker Chambers*"), Nixon arranged a secret confrontation at the Commodore Hotel in New York.
  - In December 1948, Chambers led investigators to his Maryland farm and extracted microfilms of classified State Department documents hidden inside a hollowed-out pumpkin (the **"Pumpkin Papers"**).
  - Hiss was indicted and eventually convicted of perjury in January 1950.

### 1.3 The Rhetorical Discovery of the "Establishment"
Nixon’s January 26, 1950 speech to the House of Representatives, *"The Hiss Case—A Lesson for the American People"*, marked a turning point in modern conservative rhetoric:
- Nixon did not merely attack communism as an external military threat; he framed the Hiss case as **a moral betrayal by an arrogant, cosmopolitan elite**:
  - Hiss was educated at Johns Hopkins and Harvard Law; he moved in the highest social circles; he looked down upon Middle America with condescension.
- Nixon established a powerful dichotomy that would define his entire career:
  - On one side: the hardworking, patriotic, churchgoing, ordinary Americans who pay taxes, fight in wars, and love their country.
  - On the other side: the smug, intellectual, privileged establishment who sneered at traditional values and tolerated treason.
- Perlstein notes that Nixon effectively invented **the cultural politics of resentment**—a populist weapon that revolutionized the Republican Party.

### 1.4 The "Pink Sheet" Campaign (1950)
In his 1950 race for the U.S. Senate against Democratic Representative Helen Gahagan Douglas:
- Nixon's campaign distributed 500,000 copies of a flyer printed on bright pink paper (the infamous **"Pink Sheet"**).
- The flyer compared Douglas's voting record with that of pro-Soviet congressman Vito Marcantonio, implying that she was "soft on communism" (giving her the moniker "The Pink Lady," while Douglas labeled Nixon "Tricky Dick").
- Nixon won in a landslide, cementing his national reputation as the fiercest anti-communist prosecutor in American politics.

## Unit 2: The Television Revolution & The Rhetoric of Vulnerability: The Checkers Speech (1952)

### 2.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
In 1952, Richard Nixon was running for Vice President when newspapers reported that he had a "secret slush fund" paid for by wealthy donors. General Dwight Eisenhower’s team wanted to kick him off the ticket. Instead of quitting, Nixon bought 30 minutes of live television, spoke directly into the camera to 60 million people, laid bare his private bank accounts, and talked about his family’s little dog, Checkers. It was the moment television became the supreme weapon of modern politics.

### 2.2 The Secret Fund Crisis
In September 1952, while campaigning as the vice-presidential running mate of war hero Dwight D. Eisenhower, disaster struck:
- The *New York Post* published a screaming headline: *"Secret Rich Men's Trust Fund Keeps Dick Nixon in Style Far Beyond His Salary."*
- Seventy-six wealthy California donors had contributed $18,000 to reimburse Nixon for political travel and mailing expenses. While legal, it looked like corruption to an electorate weary of political scandals.
- Prominent newspapers and Eisenhower’s inner circle urged Ike to dump Nixon from the ticket to protect his pristine crusader image.

### 2.3 The Television Broadcast of September 23, 1952
Rejecting the advice of party managers, Nixon raised $75,000 to buy thirty minutes of prime-time television immediately following the popular comedy show *The Milton Berle Show*:
- Sitting at a desk at the El Capitan Theatre in Hollywood, Nixon looked directly into the camera lens:
  - He did not deliver a bombastic political speech; he spoke in a calm, earnest, conversational tone.
  - He performed an unprecedented, radical act of **public financial transparency**: listing every asset, debt, and mortgage he owned, from his $4,000 life insurance policy to the $3,000 he owed his parents.
  - He pointed out that his wife, Pat, did not own expensive mink coats like the wives of Truman administration officials: *"Pat doesn't have a mink coat. But she does have a respectable Republican cloth coat, and I always tell her she'd look good in anything!"*

### 2.4 Checkers the Cocker Spaniel
The masterstroke of emotional rhetoric came when Nixon addressed campaign gifts:
- He confessed that one gift was sent all the way from Texas after the nomination:
  > *"A man down in Texas heard Pat on the radio mention that our two youngsters would like to have a dog. And believe it or not, the day before we left on this campaign trip we got a message from Union Station in Baltimore saying they had a package for us. We went down to get it. You know what it was? It was a little cocker spaniel dog in a crate that he’d sent all the way from Texas. Black and white spotted. And our little girl—Tricia, the six-year-old—named it Checkers. And you know, the kids, like all kids, love the dog, and I just want to say this right now, that regardless of what they say about it, we’re gonna keep him!"*

### 2.5 The Revolution in Democratic Power
The impact was seismic:
- Over 60 million Americans—half the adult population—watched the speech. More than four million telegrams, phone calls, and letters flooded the Republican National Committee demanding that Nixon stay on the ticket.
- Eisenhower embraced Nixon publicly: *"You're my boy!"*
- **Perlstein's Insight**: The Checkers Speech revolutionized political communication. Nixon demonstrated that an individual politician could use television to bypass party bosses, bypass the hostile press corps, and appeal directly to the emotional sympathies of the mass public.

## Unit 3: Cold War Dialectics on the Global Stage: The Kitchen Debate with Khrushchev (1959)

### 3.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
In 1959, Nixon traveled behind the Iron Curtain to open an American exhibition in Moscow. Standing inside a model suburban American kitchen, he got into a fiery, unscripted shouting match with Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev. While Khrushchev bragged about Soviet rockets and steel mills, Nixon pointed to dishwashers and color TVs, arguing that true freedom means giving ordinary families the freedom to choose what they want.

### 3.2 The Moscow Exhibition
In July 1959, as Vice President, Nixon traveled to Moscow to open the **American National Exhibition** in Sokolniki Park, an exchange designed to promote cultural understanding:
- Rather than displaying military weaponry, aircraft, or heavy industrial turbines, the American exhibit showcased the consumer fruits of democratic capitalism: suburban ranch houses, hi-fi stereo systems, refrigerators, lawn mowers, Pepsi-Cola, and cosmetic makeup.
- Soviet Premier **Nikita Khrushchev**, touring the exhibit with Nixon, became visibly combative, mocking the American gadgets as useless trivialities.

### 3.3 The Clash at the Model Kitchen
Standing before a yellow General Electric model kitchen cut in half so crowds could view it, the two leaders launched into an extraordinary, impromptu ideological debate:
- **Khrushchev’s Thrust**:
  - *"You Americans think that the Russian people will be astonished to see these things. The fact is, all our newly built houses have this equipment... Your capitalist system is old and dying. We will pass you by, wave to you, and say: 'Follow us!' In another seven years, we will be on the same level as America!"*
- **Nixon’s Counter-Thrust**:
  - Nixon jabbed his finger directly at Khrushchev's chest:
    > *"To us, diversity, the right to choose, the fact that we have 1,000 builders building 1,000 different houses is the most important thing. We don't have one decision made at the top by one government official that everyone must have the same house. That is the difference."*
  - Nixon turned consumer appliances into a philosophical argument for human liberty: the point of a washing machine was not mere comfort, but liberating women from backbreaking toil so they could cultivate their minds.

### 3.4 The Television Victory
Nixon insisted that the entire debate be recorded on new color videotape and broadcast uncensored on both American and Soviet television networks:
- For the American public, watching their Vice President stand toe-to-toe with the volatile Soviet dictator, never backing down while maintaining diplomatic poise, was an electrifying sight.
- The Kitchen Debate catapulted Nixon to the undisputed leadership of the Republican Party, setting the stage for his 1960 presidential nomination.

## Unit 4: Defeat, Wilderness & The Psychology of Grievance: "You Won't Have Nixon to Kick Around Anymore"

### 4.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
After losing the presidency to John F. Kennedy in 1960, Nixon ran for Governor of California in 1962 and lost again. Exhausted and humiliated, he walked into a press conference and told the reporters: *"You won't have Nixon to kick around anymore, because, gentlemen, this is my last press conference!"* Everyone assumed his career was dead. But Nixon spent the next six years traveling the country, writing essays, helping local candidates, and staging the greatest political comeback in American history.

### 4.2 The 1960 Heartbreak and the Television Trap
The 1960 presidential campaign against John F. Kennedy was a psychological trauma for Nixon:
- In the first televised presidential debate on September 26, 1960, Nixon—recovering from a knee infection, underweight, refusing television makeup, and sweating under the hot studio lights—looked haggard and defensive beside the bronzed, glamorous Kennedy.
- Radio listeners thought Nixon won; television viewers thought Kennedy won.
- Nixon lost the election by just 112,000 votes out of 68 million cast (0.17%), amid allegations of voter fraud in Chicago and Texas. Despite private fury, Nixon chose not to contest the results, fearing a constitutional crisis during the Cold War.

### 4.3 The 1962 Meltdown: "My Last Press Conference"
Seeking to rebuild his political base, Nixon ran for Governor of California in 1962 against incumbent Democrat Pat Brown and was decisively beaten:
- On the morning of November 7, 1962, sleep-deprived and furious, Nixon walked into the press room at the Beverly Hilton Hotel:
  > *"Now that all the members of the press are so delighted that I have lost, I'd like to make a statement of my own... As I leave you I want you to know—just think how much you're going to be missing. You won't have Nixon to kick around anymore, because, gentlemen, this is my last press conference."*
- Pundits across the nation wrote his political obituary. ABC News even broadcast a television special titled: *"The Political Obituary of Richard M. Nixon."*

### 4.4 The Wilderness Years (1962–1968)
Instead of retiring, Nixon moved to a Wall Street law firm in New York City and executed a brilliant, painstaking comeback strategy:
- He worked tirelessly for Republican candidates in the disastrous 1964 Barry Goldwater blowout, earning debts of loyalty from county chairmen across the nation.
- He studied international relations, wrote foreign policy essays for elite journals, and traveled extensively across Asia, Europe, and the Middle East, preparing a grand strategic platform.

### 4.5 The 1968 Law-and-Order Platform
By 1968, America was tearing itself apart: the Vietnam War, urban riots burning American cities, the assassinations of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy, and student anti-war strikes:
- In his August 8, 1968 acceptance speech at the Republican National Convention in Miami:
  > *"When we look at the state of America today, we see cities enveloped in smoke and flame. We hear sirens in the night... I pledge to you: the first civil right of every American is to be free from domestic violence."*
- Nixon positioned himself as the steady hand of order and reconciliation, winning the presidency in November 1968.

## Unit 5: The Grand Strategic Vision: "Asia After Viet Nam" (1967) & The Nixon Doctrine

### 5.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
While the rest of Washington was hopelessly obsessed with the jungle war in Vietnam, Nixon was already looking ten years ahead. In 1967, he published a famous essay in an elite journal saying: *"We cannot leave a billion Chinese people locked out of the world forever."* Once he became president, he created the **Nixon Doctrine**: America will give weapons and aid to its allies, but Asian nations must fight their own ground wars. America would no longer send half a million soldiers to fight in Asian rice paddies.

### 5.2 The 1967 *Foreign Affairs* Manifesto
In October 1967, Nixon published a revolutionary article in the prestigious foreign policy journal *Foreign Affairs*, titled *"Asia After Viet Nam"*:
- While most conservative politicians were demanding an escalation of the Vietnam War and preaching endless anti-communist crusades, Nixon demonstrated breathtaking strategic vision:
  > *"Taking the long view, we simply cannot afford to leave China forever outside the family of nations, there to nurture its fantasies, cherish its hates and threaten its neighbors. There is no place on this small planet for a billion of its potentially most able people to live in angry isolation."*
- Nixon understood that Communist China and the Soviet Union were not a monolithic bloc; their ideological and border tensions (the Sino-Soviet split) were deepening. An astute American statesman could exploit this rivalry.

### 5.3 The Nixon Doctrine (Guam, July 1969)
Speaking to reporters on the island of Guam on July 25, 1969 (formalized in his February 18, 1970 Foreign Policy Report):
- Nixon articulated a profound recalibration of American global military commitments:
  1. The United States will keep all of its treaty commitments.
  2. The United States will provide a nuclear shield if a nuclear power threatens an ally.
  3. **In cases involving other types of aggression, the threatened nation must assume the primary responsibility of providing the manpower for its own defense.**
- The United States would provide economic and military assistance, air support, and naval power, but **no more American ground troops would be committed to fight endless counterinsurgency wars on the Asian mainland**. This was the philosophical basis for "Vietnamization"—withdrawing 500,000 American troops while training South Vietnamese forces.

### 5.4 Realpolitik Over Ideology
Nixon, along with Henry Kissinger, replaced the moralistic, Wilsonian idealism that had guided American foreign policy since World War I with classical European **Realpolitik**:
- Foreign policy should not be driven by sentimental crusades to spread democracy or crusade against abstract evil.
- It must be guided by **the balance of power, national self-interest, and geopolitical equilibrium**.

## Unit 6: The Invention of the Cultural Majority: "The Great Silent Majority" & The Southern Strategy

### 6.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
In 1969, anti-war protests were rocking Washington, and television news showed demonstrators burning flags. On November 3, 1969, Nixon gave the most successful speech of his life. He looked into the camera and asked for the support of **"The Great Silent Majority"**—the quiet, decent Americans who don't hold protests, don't scream at police, pay their taxes, and love their country. With that single phrase, he united the working class and rebuilt American politics.

### 6.2 The November 3, 1969 Silent Majority Address
In the autumn of 1969, over 500,000 anti-war protesters marched on the White House in the massive Moratorium protests:
- Editorial boards and television commentators predicted Nixon would be forced into an immediate, humiliating withdrawal from Vietnam.
- Instead, on November 3, 1969, Nixon delivered a masterclass in political mobilization:
  - He explained why a sudden, precipitous withdrawal would cause bloodbaths in Southeast Asia and destroy American international credibility.
  - He concluded with his legendary appeal:
    > *"And so tonight—to you, the great silent majority of my fellow Americans—I ask for your support. I pledged in my campaign for the presidency to end the war in a way that we could win the peace. I have initiated a plan of action which will enable us to achieve that goal. The more support I can have from the American people, the faster that pledge can be redeemed; for the more divided we are at home, the less likely the enemy is to negotiate at Paris. Let us be united for peace. Let us also be united against defeat. Because let us understand: North Vietnam cannot defeat or humiliate the United States. Only Americans can do that."*

### 6.3 The Phenomenon of the "Silent Majority"
The response was an overwhelming political triumph:
- Over 80,000 telegrams and letters flooded the White House supporting the President; Nixon’s approval rating soared to 68%.
- Nixon gave a name and an identity to tens of millions of working-class and suburban Americans who felt ignored, ridiculed, and disenfranchised by university radicals, television anchors, and cultural elites.
- The "Silent Majority" became the durable political base that would win Nixon a 49-state landslide in 1972 and anchor conservative presidential victories for decades.

### 6.4 The Southern Strategy
Working with strategists Kevin Phillips and John Mitchell, Nixon dismantled the Democratic Party's century-old "Solid South":
- By taking a moderate, non-punitive approach to school desegregation, opposing forced busing of schoolchildren, and appointing conservative judges to the Supreme Court, Nixon severed working-class white Southerners from the New Deal coalition.
- He transformed the American South into a solid Republican bastion, fundamentally altering the electoral college map of the United States.

## Unit 7: Triangulation & Realpolitik: The Opening to China, Détente & The Shanghai Communiqué (1972)

### 7.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
For twenty-five years, the United States and Communist China didn't even talk to each other; they were bitter enemies. In 1972, Nixon shocked the world by getting on Air Force One and flying straight to Beijing to shake hands with Chairman Mao. By making friends with China, Nixon terrified the Soviet Union, forcing the Soviets to sign historic treaties reducing nuclear missiles. It was the greatest diplomatic chess move of the 20th century.

### 7.2 The Secret Journey of Henry Kissinger
Beginning in 1969, Nixon used backchannels (the Pakistani and Romanian embassies) to send secret messages to Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai:
- In July 1971, National Security Advisor **Henry Kissinger**, on a trip to Pakistan, feigned stomach illness, was secretly driven to the airport, and flew on a clandestine flight to Beijing to meet Zhou Enlai.
- On July 15, 1971, Nixon stunned the world by announcing live on television that he had been invited to visit the People's Republic of China.

### 7.3 The Beijing Summit: "The Week That Changed the World"
In February 1972, Nixon touched down in Beijing:
- The images of the fierce anti-communist American president shaking hands with **Mao Zedong** and **Zhou Enlai**, banquet toasts in the Great Hall of the People, and Pat Nixon touring Chinese kitchens were beamed live by satellite to a mesmerized global audience.
- Only an unimpeachable anti-communist conservative like Nixon could have made this move; a liberal Democrat would have been destroyed by accusations of being a communist appeaser ("Only Nixon could go to China").

### 7.4 The Shanghai Communiqué (February 27, 1972)
The summit produced one of the most brilliant diplomatic documents in modern history:
- Rather than papering over deep disagreements with empty platitudes, the communiqué frankly listed the opposing positions of both nations on Vietnam, Korea, and international policy.
- On the explosive question of **Taiwan**, it achieved a masterstroke of constructive ambiguity:
  > *"The United States acknowledges that all Chinese on either side of the Taiwan Strait maintain there is but one China and that Taiwan is a part of China. The United States Government does not challenge that position."*
- This formula allowed the U.S. and China to normalize relations, open trade, and isolate the Soviet Union, preserving regional peace for half a century.

### 7.5 Détente with Moscow and the ABM Treaty
Nixon’s triangulation paid immediate dividends:
- Terrified of a Sino-American alliance on their eastern border, Soviet General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev immediately invited Nixon to Moscow in May 1972.
- Nixon became the first American president to visit Moscow, signing the **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I)** and the **Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty**, inaugurating the era of **Détente** (the easing of Cold War tensions).

## Unit 8: Domestic Statecraft & Economic Rupture: The EPA, Family Assistance Plan & Camp David (1971)

### 8.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
Most people remember Nixon only for foreign policy and Watergate. But in domestic policy, he did things that would shock modern politicians: he created the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), passed the Clean Air Act, and tried to pass a law giving every poor family in America a guaranteed cash income! And in 1971, he severed the dollar from gold, creating the modern financial world of floating paper currencies.

### 8.2 The Surprising Progressive Domestic Record
Viewed through modern ideological lenses, Nixon’s domestic presidency was remarkably progressive:
- **Environmental Revolution**: Recognizing the growing public demand for environmental protection following the first Earth Day (1970), Nixon created the **Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)** by executive order and signed the **Clean Air Act of 1970**, the **Clean Water Act**, and the **Endangered Species Act**.
- **Worker Safety**: He signed the legislation creating the **Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)**.
- **Quiet Desegregation**: Under Labor Secretary George Shultz, Nixon implemented the "Philadelphia Plan," establishing affirmative action hiring goals for federal construction contractors, and peacefully desegregated over 70% of southern dual school systems.

### 8.3 The Family Assistance Plan (FAP)
In an August 8, 1969 televised address, Nixon proposed replacing the bureaucratic, broken welfare system with the **Family Assistance Plan**:
- Drafted by his urban affairs adviser **Daniel Patrick Moynihan**, FAP was essentially a **Negative Income Tax / Guaranteed Minimum Income**:
  - Providing an annual cash payment of $1,600 to a family of four, supplemented by food stamps.
  - Unlike traditional welfare, poor families were allowed to keep a portion of their earned wages without losing benefits, creating a work incentive.
- The plan passed the House of Representatives twice, but was ultimately killed in the Senate by a coalition of conservative Republicans (who opposed guaranteed welfare) and liberal Democrats (who demanded higher payment amounts).

### 8.4 The "Nixon Shock" of August 15, 1971
Faced with rising inflation, unemployment, and massive gold drains caused by foreign nations redeeming U.S. dollars for gold:
- Nixon secretly convened his economic team at Camp David over the weekend of August 13–15, 1971.
- On Sunday evening, August 15, he addressed the nation, unveiling the **New Economic Policy**:
  1. **Closing the Gold Window**: Unilaterally terminating the convertibility of the U.S. dollar into gold, effectively ending the 1944 Bretton Woods international monetary system and launching the modern era of floating fiat currencies.
  2. **Wage and Price Controls**: Imposing a mandatory 90-day freeze on all wages and prices across the American economy to combat inflation.
  3. **Import Surcharge**: Slapping a 10% tariff on all imported goods to protect American manufacturing.
- It was the most dramatic intervention of the federal government in the peacetime economy in American history.

## Unit 9: The Imperial Presidency & The Anatomy of Cover-Up: From "Smoking Gun" to "I Am Not a Crook"

### 9.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
After winning a historic 49-state re-election landslide in 1972, Nixon seemed invincible. But his deep paranoia led his team to break into the Democratic headquarters at the Watergate hotel. Instead of coming clean and firing the burglars, Nixon ordered the CIA to tell the FBI to stop investigating. He spent two years lying to the public on television, promising there was no cover-up, until his own secret White House tape recordings proved he had broken the law.

### 9.2 The Roots of Paranoia: The Plumbers and the Enemies List
Following the June 1971 leak of the **Pentagon Papers** (a top-secret Defense Department history of the Vietnam War) by Daniel Ellsberg:
- Nixon’s deep-seated siege mentality metastasized into outright paranoia. Convinced that the federal bureaucracy, the CIA, and anti-war activists were conspiring to destroy his presidency, he created a secret White House Special Investigations Unit known as **"The Plumbers"**.
- The Plumbers engaged in illegal wiretapping, burglarized the office of Ellsberg's psychiatrist in Los Angeles, and drafted White House "Enemies Lists" to audit opponents using the Internal Revenue Service.

### 9.3 The Watergate Break-In (June 17, 1972)
On June 17, 1972, five burglars working for the Committee to Re-Elect the President (CRP) were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the **Watergate complex** in Washington, D.C.:
- Initially dismissed by Press Secretary Ron Ziegler as a "third-rate burglary," the investigation by FBI agents and *Washington Post* reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein began uncovering secret slush funds and ties to the White House.

### 9.4 The "Smoking Gun" Tape (June 23, 1972)
Just six days after the break-in, Nixon met in the Oval Office with White House Chief of Staff **H.R. Haldeman**:
- The tape recorder, secretly installed by Nixon to record his meetings for his memoirs, captured Haldeman informing the President that the FBI investigation was tracing money back to CRP.
- Nixon ordered Haldeman to instruct CIA Deputy Director Vernon Walters to tell FBI Acting Director L. Patrick Gray:
  > *"Look, the problem is that this will open the whole, the whole Bay of Pigs thing... The President believes that it is going to open the whole Bay of Pigs thing up again... And that they should call the FBI in and say that we wish for the country, don't go any further into this case, period!"*
- This conversation was the **"Smoking Gun"**: clear, unambiguous proof that the President of the United States had personally ordered the misuse of the CIA to obstruct an ongoing FBI criminal investigation.

### 9.5 The Public Deceptions & The Saturday Night Massacre
Over the next eighteen months, Nixon delivered repeated televised addresses promising the American people that he was innocent:
- **April 30, 1973 Address**: *"There can be no whitewash at the White House."*
- **The Saturday Night Massacre (October 20, 1973)**: When Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox subpoenaed the secret tapes, Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Cox. Richardson refused and resigned in protest; Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus also refused and was fired; Solicitor General Robert Bork finally carried out the order. The firestorm of public outrage was catastrophic.
- **November 17, 1973 (Disney World)**: Before 400 managing editors:
  > *"I have never profited... from public service... I have never told a lie... People have got to know whether or not their President is a crook. Well, I'm not a crook. I’ve earned everything I’ve got."*

## Unit 10: The Tragic Fall & Constitutional Limits: Resignation, "My Mother Was a Saint" & Legacy

### 10.1 The Core Idea in Plain English
In the end, the American Constitution held. The Supreme Court unanimously ruled that even the President must obey subpoenas and hand over the tapes. When the tapes were released and his own Republican allies told him he would be impeached and convicted, Nixon resigned—the first and only American president ever to do so. In his final, tearful farewell to his staff, he left behind an unforgettable piece of advice: *"Always remember, others may hate you, but those who hate you don't win unless you hate them, and then you destroy yourself."*

### 10.2 *United States v. Nixon* (July 24, 1974)
Nixon claimed that the doctrine of **Executive Privilege** gave him absolute, unreviewable constitutional authority to withhold his White House tapes from criminal proceedings:
- In a unanimous 8–0 decision authored by Chief Justice Warren Burger (whom Nixon himself had appointed), the Supreme Court ruled:
  - While executive privilege exists for military and diplomatic secrets, it is not absolute.
  - **The President is not above the law.** The legitimate needs of the judicial process in a criminal trial outweigh presidential privilege.
- Nixon was forced to surrender sixty-four White House tape recordings, including the June 23, 1972 "Smoking Gun" recording.

### 10.3 The Inevitable Impeachment and the Goldwater Visit
Between July 27 and July 30, 1974, the House Judiciary Committee voted three bipartisan articles of impeachment against Nixon:
1. Obstruction of justice in the Watergate investigation.
2. Abuse of presidential power (misuse of the IRS, CIA, and FBI).
3. Contempt of Congress (refusal to comply with subpoenas).
- On August 7, 1974, a delegation of senior Republican leaders led by Senator **Barry Goldwater** visited the Oval Office and informed Nixon that he had no more than fifteen votes in the Senate—he would be impeached and convicted.

### 10.4 The Resignation Address (August 8, 1974)
On the evening of August 8, 1974, Nixon delivered his final address to the nation from the Oval Office:
- He announced that he would resign effective at noon the next day:
  > *"I have never been a quitter. To leave office before my term is completed is abhorrent to every instinct in my body. But as President, I must put the interest of America first... I no longer have a strong enough political base in the Congress... Therefore, I shall resign the Presidency effective at noon tomorrow."*
- Significantly, Nixon did not admit legal or criminal guilt; he framed his departure as a pragmatic sacrifice for national unity.

### 10.5 The Farewell to the Staff: "My Mother Was a Saint"
On the morning of August 9, 1974, before walking to the helicopter on the South Lawn:
- Nixon addressed his staff and Cabinet in the East Room in an unscripted, deeply moving, and raw psychological confession:
  - He spoke of his humble childhood, working in his father's lemon grove and grocery store, of his two brothers who died young of tuberculosis.
  - He broke down in tears: *"Nobody will ever write a book, probably, about my mother. Well, I guess all of you would say this about your mother—my mother was a saint."*
  - He closed with the supreme moral tragedy of his life:
    > *"Always give your best, never get discouraged, never be petty; always remember, others may hate you, but those who hate you don’t win unless you hate them, and then you destroy yourself."*
- Minutes later, Nixon climbed the steps of Army One, turned, threw up his famous double V-for-victory sign with a grim smile, and flew away into political exile.
