The Five Constant Factors and Seven Deliberations

Laying Plans (Initial Estimations & The Five Factors)
All warfare is based on deception; victory is determined by deliberate calculations in the temple before battle is joined.

Sun Tzu opens his ancient military manual not with romantic glorification of martial glory, but with a somber, realist warning: the military is the supreme business of the state, the domain of life and death, the road to survival or ruin. It cannot be entered into casually or emotionally. To assess whether a state should embark upon war, the sovereign and general must systematically calculate five invariant dimensions: (1) The Tao (Moral Law): that shared purpose and legitimacy which causes the populace to follow their leaders without fear of death; (2) Heaven: diurnal cycles, weather, extreme cold, summer heat, and the operational seasons; (3) Earth: distances, difficulty or ease of travel, open plains versus narrow defiles, and terrain that offers life or death; (4) The General: the personal embodiment of five virtues—Wisdom to foresee change, Sincerity to earn absolute trust, Benevolence to cherish the troops, Courage to seize fleeting opportunities, and Strictness to enforce obedience; and (5) Method and Discipline: administrative rank structures, logistics, supply corridors, and expenditure management.

Sun Tzu instructs that before troops march, the commander must convene the council in the temple to conduct 'calculations before battle' (Miao Suan). The general subjects both sides to Seven Forensic Questions: Which ruler possesses the greater Moral Tao? Which commander has greater capability? Who holds the advantages of Heaven and Earth? On which side are orders and discipline more strictly executed? Which army's weapons and horses are stronger? Whose officers and men are more rigorously trained? Whose rewards and punishments are more consistently and justly administered? The ancient commentator Cao Cao notes: 'If you calculate these seven factors accurately, victory and defeat are visible before a single spear is thrown.' The commander who calculates deeply before fighting wins; the one who computes few calculations loses.

The art of war is of vital importance to the State. It is a matter of life and death, a road either to safety or to ruin. Hence it is a subject of inquiry which can on no account be neglected.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Conduct rigorous pre-operational calculations: audit the seven variables before deploying capital or entering strategic competition.
  • Align the Tao: never launch high-risk campaigns without establishing absolute unity of purpose between leadership and frontline personnel.
  • Cultivate the five command virtues: balance strict discipline with genuine benevolence to maintain unshakable organizational morale.

The Deception Matrix: The 12 Asymmetrical Inversions

Laying Plans (Initial Estimations & The Five Factors)
All warfare is based on deception; victory is achieved by systematically manipulating enemy perception through asymmetrical psychological inversions.

In the second half of Chapter 1, Sun Tzu lays down the foundational epistemological principle of Chinese military thought: 'Warfare is the Tao of Deception' (Bing Zhe, Gui Dao Ye). Because human beings act upon their mental models of reality rather than reality itself, the commander who controls what the adversary perceives completely dictates the adversary's actions. The general does not fight the enemy's physical army first; he attacks the enemy's mind.

Sun Tzu codifies twelve precise operational inversions: When capable of striking, project total weakness and incompetence; when actively maneuvering forces, appear completely inert; when close to the objective, convince the enemy you are hundreds of miles away; when far away, make him believe you are at his gates. Hold out tempting baits to entice the greedy general; feign administrative disorder and internal rebellion, then strike him while he scrambles to exploit it. If the enemy is secure at all points, be prepared; if he possesses superior force, evade combat. If the opposing commander is choleric and hot-tempered, deliberately insult and irritate him to provoke irrational rashness. If he is arrogant, appear humble and timid to inflate his hubris. If his forces are rested, wear them down with false alarms; if his alliances are cohesive, drive wedges of suspicion between them. Attack where he is completely unprepared; emerge where you are least expected.

All warfare is based on deception. Hence, when able to attack, we must seem unable; when using our forces, we must seem inactive; when we are near, we must make the enemy believe we are far away; when far away, we must make him believe we are near.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Project weakness when you are poised to strike; project distance when you are within striking range.
  • Bait the greedy adversary with low-cost prizes to pull him out of defensive fortifications.
  • Invert your opponent's emotional stability: bait the angry leader into recklessness and inflate the proud leader's vanity.

The Compounding Bleed of Protracted Campaigns

Waging War (The Economics and Logistics of Attrition)
No nation has ever benefited from prolonged warfare; speed, decisive termination, and avoiding attritional bleed preserve the state's economic foundation.

Sun Tzu demonstrates a profound grasp of political economy: military operations are fundamentally an economic drain on the sovereign treasury. Mobilizing an army of 100,000 men requires an expenditure of 1,000 ounces of silver per day. This cost encompasses domestic provisioning, heavy transport carts, replacement of shattered armor, broken chariots, grease, glue, arrows, and stipends for envoys and advisers. When campaigns are prolonged across seasons, the state's agricultural base is hollowed out: 700,000 peasant households are disrupted to support the logistics of the frontline force.

Sun Tzu issues an immutable historical law: 'There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare' (Bing Jiu Er Guo Li Zhe, Wei Zhi You Ye). Protracted conflict blunts the edge of the finest weapons, dampens the psychological ardour of the troops, exhausts the national treasury, and leaves the homeland vulnerable. When your resources are bled dry and your soldiers are demoralized, neighboring rival states will inevitably capitalize on your extremity to invade. Even the wisest advisers cannot salvage a state ruined by military attrition. Therefore, while clumsy haste in war is undesirable, cleverness has never been seen associated with long delays.

There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare... In war, let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Prioritize decisive speed over prolonged attritional engagements in any competitive struggle.
  • Calculate total carrying costs before launching campaigns: account for administrative, logistical, and domestic drain.
  • Never allow pride to prolong a campaign whose economic costs exceed its strategic value.

The Foraging Law: The 20-to-1 Logistical Ratio

Waging War (The Economics and Logistics of Attrition)
Surviving distant campaigns requires living off the enemy's logistical surplus; transporting domestic supplies across long distances produces a 20-to-1 inefficiency ratio.

Sun Tzu analyzes the crushing mathematics of supply lines. When an army relies on grain hauled from the homeland over long distances, the draft animals and transport laborers consume nineteen bushels of grain along the road for every single bushel delivered to the frontline soldiers. Thus, the logistical ratio is 20-to-1: one cartload of grain captured from the enemy's granaries is equivalent in value and labor to twenty cartloads hauled from the home territory.

The expert general therefore makes it his primary business to forage on the enemy (Yin Liang Yu Di). Rather than devastating captured territory, Sun Tzu advocates a calculated integration policy: treat captured enemy soldiers generously, heal their wounded, feed them from captured supplies, and blend them into your own chariot ranks. Replace enemy flags with your own banners. By rewarding the first soldier who captures an enemy chariot, you stimulate aggressive offensive momentum. Sun Tzu terms this: 'Using the conquered foe to augment one's own strength' (Sheng Di Er Yi Qiang).

Bring war material with you from home, but forage on the enemy. Thus the army will have food enough for its needs... A cartload of the enemy's provisions is equivalent to twenty of one's own.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Feed off the competitor's resources: acquire enemy assets, talent, and distribution channels to finance your expansion.
  • Treat captured personnel and defectors with high generosity to convert enemy manpower into loyal assets.
  • Recognize the compounding penalty of extended supply lines: decouple your operations from distant support bases.

The Four-Tiered Strategic Hierarchy

Attack by Stratagem (The Hierarchy of Victory & The Rule of Five)
Supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting; capturing the enemy's state, army, and cities intact is superior to destroying them.

In Chapter 3, Sun Tzu presents the philosophical summit of Chinese strategic philosophy: 'To fight and conquer in one hundred battles is not the supreme of excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting' (Bu Zhan Er Qu Ren Zhi Bing, Shan Zhi Shan Zhe Ye). In Western attrition warfare (such as Clausewitzian annihilation), the goal is the physical slaughter of the enemy army. In Sun Tzu's holistic realism, the goal is preservation: preserving your own state and army whole, and capturing the enemy's state, cities, and troops intact (Quan Sheng). A shattered city and an exterminated population provide no tax base, no labor, and no enduring wealth.

Sun Tzu codifies the Four Tiers of Grand Strategy: (1) The supreme art of war is to attack the enemy's strategy (Fa Mou)—anticipating his plans, rendering his operational concepts obsolete before they can be executed; (2) Next best is to disrupt his alliances (Fa Jiao)—isolating the adversary through diplomatic statecraft, turning his confederates into neutrals or enemies; (3) Third is to attack his army in the field (Fa Bing)—engaging in open tactical combat, which inevitably expends blood and treasure; (4) The worst possible policy is to besiege walled cities (Gong Cheng). Besieging a fortified city consumes three months constructing assault mantlets and protective shelters, and another three months piling up mounds of earth against the walls. If the general loses his patience and sends his soldiers swarming up the walls like ants, one-third of his men will be slaughtered without taking the citadel. This is the calamity of siege.

To fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting. Hence the highest form of generalship is to balk the enemy's plans.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Aim for total victory through psychological, political, and strategic dominance rather than bloody attrition.
  • Attack the adversary's grand plan before it can be deployed; dismantle his coalition and partnerships before engaging in direct competition.
  • Never commit your resources to costly frontal sieges against entrenched competitors unless all other strategic options have failed.

The Five Relative Force Ratios & Sovereign Interference

Attack by Stratagem (The Hierarchy of Victory & The Rule of Five)
Tactical methods are strictly dictated by relative force ratios; the sovereign must grant the competent general absolute operational autonomy without political interference.

Sun Tzu establishes the strict mathematical laws governing relative combat power. Tactical maneuvers cannot be selected arbitrarily; they are determined by the ratio of forces: If your strength is ten times the enemy's, completely surround him. If five times his strength, launch a direct assault. If twice his strength, divide your forces into two: one to hold his front, and the other to strike his flank and rear. If equally matched, offer battle with high discipline. If slightly inferior in numbers, maintain defensive evasion. If vastly outmatched in all dimensions, avoid engagement entirely and retreat to preserve strength.

Sun Tzu then delivers a blunt critique of sovereign interference in military operations. A ruler harms the military in three fatal ways: (1) Ordering an advance or retreat when ignorant of the physical reality of the terrain, effectively hobbling the army; (2) Attempting to govern the army's internal administration using the civilian laws of the court, confusing the officer corps; (3) Interfering with the command assignments of military officers while ignorant of military flexibility, undermining the soldiers' confidence. Sun Tzu closes with the five conditions for victory, concluding with the famous dual formula: 'If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.'

If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat. If you know neither the enemy nor yourself, you will succumb in every battle.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Calibrate your tactical aggression strictly against your relative resources: never attack head-on when outnumbered.
  • Enforce the boundary between governance and operational execution: keep political and administrative meddling out of tactical execution.
  • Master the dual knowledge: continuously audit both your competitor's vulnerabilities and your own operational capabilities.

Invincibility and the Calculation of Victory

Tactical Dispositions (Disposition of the Military & Invincibility)
Invincibility lies in your own defensive preparations; vulnerability is provided by the enemy. The victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has already been secured.

In Chapter 4 (Xing), Sun Tzu explores the concept of tactical form and disposition. The ancient masters of war first made themselves invincible, and then waited for the moment when the enemy made himself vulnerable. Invincibility (Bu Ke Sheng) is entirely within your own control: it is forged through defensive fortifications, flawless discipline, hidden formations, and logistical resilience. But the opportunity to defeat the enemy (Ke Sheng) is provided exclusively by the adversary when he makes a mistake, exposes a flank, or overextends his lines.

Therefore, a skilled commander can guarantee that he will not be defeated, but he cannot force the enemy to be vulnerable. Standing on the defensive indicates insufficient strength or unfavorable conditions; attacking indicates an abundance of strength. Sun Tzu formulates the profound paradox of strategy: 'The victorious army first wins and then seeks battle; the defeated army first fights and then seeks victory.' The master strategist only commits his troops after he has pre-engineered conditions so thoroughly that defeat is mathematically impossible. This calculation follows five steps: Measurement of Earth, Estimation of Quantity, Calculation of Capacity, Balancing of Chances, and Final Victory.

To secure ourselves against defeat lies in our own hands, but the opportunity of defeating the enemy is provided by the enemy himself... Hence the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won, whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Focus first on establishing total operational invincibility: eliminate vulnerabilities before seeking offensive victories.
  • Never initiate battle in hopes of winning: only enter conflict when victory has already been structural and predetermined.
  • Wait patiently for the adversary to reveal his vulnerability: capitalize on his errors rather than forcing premature confrontations.

The Lifted Autumn Hair & The Effortless Triumph

Tactical Dispositions (Disposition of the Military & Invincibility)
True strategic mastery produces victories that appear completely effortless, winning without bloody heroics or public acclaim for courage.

Sun Tzu offers a brilliant critique of popular perceptions of heroism. Society celebrates the general who fights a bloody, desperate battle, suffers massive casualties, and barely extracts a theatrical victory at sunset. Sun Tzu condemns this as mediocre leadership. To foresee a victory that any ordinary peasant can see is not great skill. Lifting an autumn hair (the finest strand of rabbit fur) is no demonstration of physical strength; seeing the sun and moon is no sign of sharp vision; hearing the crack of thunder is no sign of acute hearing.

The true master of war wins victories that are so subtle, quiet, and effortless that they earn him no public reputation for wisdom and no medals for courage. Why? Because he positioned his forces so perfectly that the enemy collapsed before a pitched battle was ever fought. His victories are characterized by the absence of mistakes. He does not rely on heroic luck; he places himself in an unassailable position (Bu Bai Zhi Di) and does not miss the moment to topple his opponent. A victorious army entering combat is like a heavy pound weight placed against a single grain on a balance scale (Yi Yi Cheng Zhu).

What the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins, but excels in winning with ease. Hence his victories bring him neither reputation for wisdom nor credit for courage.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Do not measure strategic competence by the drama or bloodiness of the victory: effortless wins are the highest mark of genius.
  • Eliminate unforced operational errors: perfection in positioning eliminates the need for heroic, high-risk gambles.
  • Concentrate overwhelming mass at the decisive point: enter the arena with an overwhelming asymmetrical advantage.

Direct (Zheng) and Indirect (Qi) Tactical Maneuvers

Energy (Shih / The Dynamic Release of Latent Force)
Tactical combinations are endless through the dynamic interplay of the Direct (Zheng) fixing force and the Indirect (Qi) striking force.

Chapter 5 explores Shih—the concept of dynamic potential energy, momentum, and organizational architecture. Sun Tzu begins with organizational scaling: commanding a host of 100,000 men is fundamentally identical to commanding a squad of five men; it is entirely a question of organization, division of numbers (Fen Shu), and clear communication systems using flags, banners, gongs, and drums (Xing Ming).

Sun Tzu then introduces the foundational dualism of tactical maneuver: the Zheng (Direct, Orthodox) and the Qi (Indirect, Unorthodox, Surprise). In combat, the Zheng force is used to fix the enemy, engage his front, and absorb his attention. But decisive victory is achieved exclusively through the Qi force—the unexpected flanking strike, the hidden ambush, the sudden rear assault. Though there are only two methods of attack, their combinations are inexhaustible. Sun Tzu compares them to the five musical notes that produce melodies no ear has ever exhausted, the five primary colors that create hues no eye has ever seen, and the four seasons that rotate in endless succession. The Zheng and Qi give birth to each other in an infinite cycle, like two interlocking rings.

In all fighting, the direct method may be used for joining battle, but indirect methods will be needed in order to secure victory. Indirect tactics, efficiently applied, are inexhaustible as Heaven and Earth.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Structure large organizations into disciplined, modular units with unambiguous signaling mechanisms.
  • Always deploy a two-part operational plan: a direct force to fix the competitor's attention, and an indirect force to deliver the decisive blow.
  • Rotate constantly between orthodox and unorthodox maneuvers to keep the competitor perpetually off-balance.

The Momentum of the Torrent & The Enticement Trap

Energy (Shih / The Dynamic Release of Latent Force)
Strategic energy is released like the sudden swoop of a falcon or the rush of water rolling boulders; simulated disorder entices the adversary into total entrapment.

Sun Tzu illustrates the physics of Shih through two vivid natural images: water and the falcon. The energy of a developed military position is like the momentum of a torrential river that sweeps massive boulders along in its violent current. The timing of the strike is like the sudden swoop of a falcon: the bird glides silently at high altitude, judges distance with microscopic precision, and breaks its target's neck in a single catastrophic dive. Quality of energy depends on the momentum; timing depends on the brevity of the strike.

To unleash this energy effectively, the commander must master the art of simulated psychological postures. Simulated disorder requires flawless underlying discipline; simulated cowardice requires extraordinary inner courage; simulated weakness requires immense hidden strength. By dangling small advantages and staging apparent confusion, the commander entices the enemy to break his formations and advance recklessly. The master strategist then releases his concentrated force upon the disordered foe, like rolling round boulders down a mountain thousands of feet high.

Energy may be likened to the bending of a crossbow; decision, to the releasing of a trigger... The clever combatant looks to the effect of combined energy, and does not require too much from individuals.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Build latent organizational momentum before releasing energy: strike with the speed and precision of a hunting falcon.
  • Feign chaos and fear to draw the competitor into an overextended position.
  • Rely on structural positioning rather than individual heroics to generate overwhelming momentum.

Seizing the Initiative and Forcing the Enemy's Movement

Chapter 6: Weak Points and Strong (Xu and Shi)
The skilled commander imposes his will upon the enemy and refuses to allow the enemy's will to be imposed upon him.

Chapter 6 introduces Xu (Void, Weakness, Emptiness) and Shi (Solid, Strength, Fullness). The foundation of operational dominance is seizing and maintaining the initiative: 'The clever combatant imposes his will upon the adversary, but does not permit the adversary's will to be imposed upon him' (Zhi Ren Er Bu Zhi Yu Ren). The army that arrives first on the battlefield and rests its men in favorable terrain is fresh, relaxed, and fully prepared. The army that arrives late, forced to march in frantic haste, enters combat physically exhausted and mentally disoriented.

How does a general dictate the enemy's movements? By manipulating incentives and threats. You make it advantageous for him to come to you by holding out easy prizes; you make it impossible for him to approach by threatening his vital supply depots and home territory. If the enemy is at ease, harass and exhaust him; if he is well-fed, starve him; if he is quietly encamped, force him to march. March over miles of country without encountering resistance by advancing through areas where the enemy is absent. Attack places the enemy cannot defend, and defend places the enemy dare not attack.

Therefore the clever combatant imposes his will on the enemy, but does not allow the enemy's will to be imposed on him.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Arrive first in any competitive market or battleground to dictate terms from a rested, prepared position.
  • Retain operational initiative at all costs: force competitors to react to your strategic moves rather than reacting to theirs.
  • Strike where the opponent is empty (Xu); completely bypass his fortified strengths (Shi).

Formlessness and The Analogy of Water

Chapter 6: Weak Points and Strong (Xu and Shi)
Military tactics must be like water: flowing away from heights and hastening downward, adapting its form to the contours of the earth.

Sun Tzu rises to sublime philosophical poetry in describing Formlessness (Wu Xing). The supreme general is subtle to the point of formlessness, and mysterious to the point of soundlessness; thereby he holds the enemy's fate in his hands. If you keep your dispositions formless, the most penetrating spies cannot discover your intentions, and the wisest minds cannot devise plans against you. If you force the enemy to reveal his dispositions while concealing your own, you remain concentrated while he is divided.

If the enemy does not know where you intend to strike, he must prepare defenses on all fronts: in front, in rear, on his left flank, and on his right flank. When he prepares everywhere, his strength is diluted everywhere. 'He who prepares on his front makes his rear weak; he who prepares on his left makes his right weak; he who prepares everywhere will be weak everywhere.' Numerical inferiority is produced by having to prepare against attacks; numerical superiority is achieved by forcing the enemy to disperse his forces.

Sun Tzu crowns this chapter with the eternal analogy of water: military formations are like flowing water. Water avoids high ground and rushes downward into valleys; in the exact same manner, the military avoids fullness (strength) and strikes emptiness (weakness). Just as water has no constant shape, but conforms to the vessel or the riverbed, so in warfare there are no permanent dispositions or fixed conditions. He who can modify his tactics in response to his opponent and thereby achieve victory is termed divine (Shen).

Military tactics are like unto water; for water in its natural course runs away from high places and hastens downwards. So in war, the way is to avoid what is strong and to strike at what is weak.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Conceal your strategic plans until the moment of execution: maintain total operational formlessness.
  • Force competitors to dilute their resources across dozens of defensive fronts while concentrating your capital on one decisive point.
  • Flow like water: avoid head-on confrontations with entrenched strength and pour your resources into neglected gaps.

The Hazard of Forced Marches & Supply Deprivation

Chapter 7: Maneuvering (The Art of Turning the Devious into the Direct)
Maneuvering with an army is advantageous, but with an undisciplined multitude it is perilous; turning the devious into the direct is the supreme tactical art.

Chapter 7 tackles Jun Zheng—the direct clash and maneuvering of armed masses. Sun Tzu defines the supreme difficulty of maneuvering: turning the devious, circuitous route into the direct route, and transforming misfortune into advantage. By setting out along a roundabout path, dangling bait to distract the enemy, and marching with superior discipline, you can start after him and yet arrive before him. This is the art of the indirect approach.

However, Sun Tzu issues a stern warning against reckless forced marches. If you command an army to march one hundred li (roughly thirty miles) in a single day to seize an advantageous position, the commanders of your van will be captured, only the strongest men will arrive, and nine-tenths of the army will fall behind. If you march fifty li, half your army will drop out. If you march thirty li, two-thirds will arrive. An army stripped of its baggage train, provisions, and supply bases is doomed. Furthermore, you cannot maneuver through forests, mountains, defiles, and marshes without local guides who understand the physical earth.

Maneuvering with an army is advantageous; with an undisciplined multitude, most dangerous... If you march 100 li in one day to snatch an advantage, the leader of all your three divisions will fall into the hands of the enemy.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Master the roundabout approach: take an unexpected route to arrive at the objective ahead of the competitor.
  • Never push an organization into frantic forced marches that incinerate two-thirds of your operational capacity.
  • Hire local guides and regional experts: never operate in unfamiliar territory without native intelligence.

The Diurnal Rhythm of Morale & The Desperate Foe

Chapter 7: Maneuvering (The Art of Turning the Devious into the Direct)
Energy and morale follow a diurnal cycle; never intercept an army returning home or press a surrounded, desperate enemy to the point of suicidal fury.

Sun Tzu examines the psychology of combat morale (Qi). A soldier's spirit operates on a predictable diurnal rhythm: in the morning, his energy is fresh, sharp, and aggressive; by afternoon, his physical stamina begins to flag; by evening, his mind turns homeward to shelter and rest. The clever general avoids the enemy when his spirit is high and sharp, and strikes him when his morale is sluggish and his men are longing for home. This is the control of the factor of mental energy (Zhi Qi).

Sun Tzu provides four additional command controls: (1) Control of the Mind: using disciplined calm to await chaos; (2) Control of the Physical: using proximity to wait for the distant, using rest to wait for the fatigued, using full bellies to wait for the hungry; (3) Control of the Variable: not intercepting an army flying banners in perfect order; (4) The Law of the Desperate Foe: do not swallow bait offered by the enemy; do not interfere with an army returning home; when you surround an enemy force, you must deliberately leave an escape route open (Wei Shi Bi Que); do not press a desperate foe too hard. If an army is trapped with zero hope of escape, every soldier will fight to the death with the ferocity of a trapped tiger. Leaving a visible escape path breaks their collective resolve and induces them to flee, allowing you to crush them in transit.

In the morning a soldier's spirit is keen; by midday it has begun to flag; and in the evening, his mind is bent only on returning to camp... When you surround an army, leave an outlet free. Do not press a desperate foe too hard.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Time your competitive strikes against the competitor's energy lows, avoiding him when his momentum is fresh and high.
  • Always leave an opponent an honorable escape route: cornering an adversary into total ruin provokes lethal, suicidal resistance.
  • Use disciplined calm to wait out external market chaos.

The Nine Invariants of Situational Adaptation

Chapter 8: Variation in Tactics (Adapting to Variables & The 5 Fatal Faults)
Tactical mastery requires breaking dogmatic rules when terrain and conditions demand adaptation; the general who cannot vary his methods is doomed.

In Chapter 8, Sun Tzu addresses the fatal disease of military dogmatism. Rules are essential, but the commander who blindly executes standard operating procedures without adapting to situational variables will lead his army to destruction. Sun Tzu lists the legendary Nine Variations (Jiu Bian): Do not encamp in low-lying, marshy ground; unite with your allies on open intersecting ground; do not linger in dangerous, isolated territory; devise stratagems in hemmed-in defiles; fight to the death on desperate ground.

Sun Tzu issues an extraordinary decree regarding the sovereignty of tactical command: 'There are roads which must not be followed, armies which must not be attacked, walled cities which must not be besieged, territories which must not be contested, and commands of the sovereign which must not be obeyed' (Jun Ming You Suo Bu Shou). When the sovereign sitting in his distant palace issues an order that violates the physical reality of the battlefield, the competent general must refuse to obey. True loyalty to the state consists in preserving the army and achieving victory, not in blindly following disastrous political decrees.

There are roads which must not be followed, armies which must not be attacked, towns which must not be besieged, positions which must not be contested, commands of the sovereign which must not be obeyed.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Reject dogmatic adherence to procedure: adapt your tactical execution to fluctuating real-world ground conditions.
  • Have the courage to push back against executive leadership when directives contradict frontline operational reality.
  • Never contest territory or battles that yield no tangible strategic dividend even if won.

The Five Fatal Psychological Flaws of a Commander

Chapter 8: Variation in Tactics (Adapting to Variables & The 5 Fatal Faults)
The ruin of an army originates in the psychological blindspots of its general; character defects can be systematically baited and exploited by a clever opponent.

Sun Tzu turns his diagnostic lens inward upon the personality of the general, codifying the Five Fatal Psychological Faults (Wu Wei). These are not technical deficits in military knowledge, but uncalibrated emotional extremes that guarantee defeat: (1) Recklessness (Yong): the general who possesses courage without calculation can be baited into fatal ambushes; (2) Cowardice (Qie): the general who fears losses can be bullied and captured; (3) A Quick Temper (Fen Su): the choleric general who is easily enraged can be baited into reckless, ill-timed counterattacks by mock insults; (4) Excessive Delicacy of Honor (Lian Jie): the general who is overly sensitive to public shame can be paralyzed by slander and mockery; (5) Over-solicitude for his troops (Ai Min): the general who cannot bear to see his soldiers suffer becomes hesitant, exhausted by compassion, and incapable of executing hard, necessary maneuvers.

Sun Tzu notes: 'These five are the catastrophic errors of a general, the ruin of military operations. Whenever an army is overthrown and its commander slain, the cause is invariably found among these five flaws.' A master strategist studies his opponent's psychological profile to identify which of these five levers to manipulate.

There are five dangerous faults which may affect a general: (1) Recklessness, which leads to destruction; (2) Cowardice, which leads to capture; (3) A quick temper, which can be baited by insults; (4) A delicacy of honor, which is sensitive to shame; (5) Over-solicitude for his men, which exposes him to worry and trouble.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Audit your executive team for the five psychological flaws: eliminate recklessness, cowardice, quick temper, hypersensitive ego, and debilitating sentimentality.
  • Profile your adversary's emotional weaknesses: bait the angry leader into mistakes and prick the vain leader's honor.
  • Balance compassion with firmness: an executive who cannot impose necessary hardship will ruin the collective enterprise.

Topographical Rules of Encampment

Chapter 9: The Army on the March (Encampment & Forensic Battlefield Signals)
Operational encampments must align with natural physical terrain, camping on high sunny ground and avoiding enclosed watercourses.

Chapter 9 covers Xing Jun—marching the army and deciphering the physical signals of the battlefield. Sun Tzu provides precise rules for maneuvering across four distinct topographical environments: (1) Mountains: pass through mountains along valleys, camp on high ground facing the sun (Yang), and never climb heights to fight an enemy; (2) Rivers: after crossing a river, immediately move far away from the bank. When an invading enemy crosses a river, do not attack him in midstream; allow half his army to cross, and then strike him while his forces are divided. Never fight upstream from the enemy; (3) Marshes and Swamps: cross salt marshes with maximum speed; if forced into combat in a marsh, keep water and grass on your flank and forest at your back; (4) Plains: occupy level, open ground with rising terrain on your right and rear, so that danger lies in front and safety behind.

Sun Tzu emphasizes that all armies prefer high ground to low ground, and sunny positions to dark, damp ground. Proper encampment prevents infectious diseases, preserves the physical health of men and horses, and guarantees tactical superiority. These were the encampment methods by which the Yellow Emperor conquered his four rivals.

Camp in high places, facing the sun. Do not climb heights in order to fight. So much for mountain warfare. After crossing a river, you should get far away from it.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Secure the high, sunny ground in physical and operational positioning: avoid dark, low-lying, swampy operational environments.
  • Strike the crossing competitor in mid-transition: wait until half his operation is committed across the threshold before launching your attack.
  • Never advance upstream or fight against natural gravity.

Forensic Decoding of Enemy Battlefield Signals

Chapter 9: The Army on the March (Encampment & Forensic Battlefield Signals)
Subtle environmental cues and physical movements reveal the hidden psychological condition and impending maneuvers of the enemy.

In one of the most brilliant observational treatises in ancient literature, Sun Tzu lists dozens of forensic micro-signals that allow an alert commander to read the enemy's mind through nature and behavior: When trees in a distant forest shake and sway, the enemy is advancing secretly through the brush. When birds suddenly rise in flock from a canopy, an ambush is waiting beneath. When wild animals burst out of the woods in terror, a sudden assault is underway. High, narrow columns of rising dust indicate the approach of war chariots; low, wide dust clouds indicate marching infantry; dust rising in scattered patches indicates squads gathering firewood.

Sun Tzu decodes behavioral psychology inside the enemy ranks: If enemy emissaries speak in humble words while their army actively strengthens defenses, they are preparing to advance. If envoys speak aggressively and advance forward, they are preparing to retreat. If the enemy's frontline troops lean heavily on their spears while standing, they are starving. If water-bearers drink first before bringing water to camp, the army is suffering acute thirst. If birds gather and roost over an enemy encampment, the position is already abandoned. If soldiers whisper together in small knots and their commanders confer nervously, the leadership has lost the confidence of the men. If the enemy general hands out excessive rewards, his resources are running out; if he imposes excessive punishments, his army is on the verge of mutiny.

The rising of birds in their flight is the sign of an ambuscade. Startled beasts indicate that a sudden attack is coming... When the light chariots come out first and take up a position on the wings, it is a sign that the enemy is forming for battle.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Cultivate micro-observational vigilance: read the subtle environmental ripples that signal competitor movements.
  • Decode competitor communications inversely: humble language masks offensive preparations; blustering threats mask retreat.
  • Monitor internal organizational signals: excessive bonuses indicate operational desperation; excessive reprimands indicate failing leadership.

The Six Structural Terrains

Chapter 10: Terrain (The Six Terrains and The Six Calamities of Command)
Terrain is the natural ally of the military; victory requires adapting tactics precisely to the six structural geometries of the physical earth.

Chapter 10 codifies Di Xing—the relationship between terrain classification and military command. Sun Tzu categorizes the physical world into Six Terrains: (1) Accessible Ground (Tong): ground that can be freely traversed by both sides. On accessible ground, secure the high sunny positions first and protect your supply lines; (2) Entangling Ground (Gua): ground that is easy to abandon but impossible to reoccupy once lost. If the enemy is unprepared, sally forth and crush him; but if he is prepared and you fail to defeat him, retreat is impossible and disaster follows; (3) Temporizing Ground (Zhi): ground where neither side gains an advantage by advancing. Even if the enemy tempts you with bait, do not advance; feign retreat, draw him out, and strike him on the counter; (4) Narrow Passes (Ai): if you occupy a narrow defile first, garrison it heavily and block the opening. If the enemy occupies it first, never attack him unless the pass is poorly guarded; (5) Precipitous Heights (Xian): seize the high sunny peaks first and wait for the enemy. If he holds the heights, do not follow him; retreat and draw him away; (6) Great Distance (Yuan): when armies are positioned far apart and combat power is equal, it is difficult to provoke battle, and fighting will be disadvantageous to the initiator.

Sun Tzu concludes: 'These are the six principles connected with the Earth. The general who has reached a responsible post must make them the subject of his careful study.'

Terrain may be classified according to three nature, generally, as: (1) Accessible ground; (2) Entangling ground; (3) Temporizing ground; (4) Narrow passes; (5) Precipitous heights; (6) Positions at a great distance.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Classify your operational terrain before deploying capital: identify whether the field is accessible, entangling, or narrow.
  • Never advance onto entangling ground unless you are 100% certain of an immediate knockout blow.
  • Garrison chokepoints early: hold narrow regulatory and logistical defiles to control market access.

The Six Calamities Arising from Internal Command Failure

Chapter 10: Terrain (The Six Terrains and The Six Calamities of Command)
Defeat is rarely caused by natural obstacles; it results from the commander's failure to balance strength, discipline, and training, producing six fatal command calamities.

Sun Tzu distinguishes between natural topographical hazards and the Six Calamities of Command (Liu Bai) that originate entirely from leadership failure: (1) Flight (Tao): when other conditions are equal, if an army attacks an enemy ten times its size, the result is flight; (2) Insubordination (Chi): when the common soldiers are too strong and fierce while their officers are weak and timid, the army becomes insubordinate; (3) Collapse (Xian): when the officers are fierce and demanding while the common soldiers are weak and untrained, the army collapses under pressure; (4) Ruin (Beng): when junior officers are enraged and insubordinate, meeting the enemy and fighting on their own initiative out of personal anger while the general does not know their capabilities, the army is ruined; (5) Disorganization (Luan): when the general is weak and lacks authority, where his rules are ambiguous and inconsistent, and where troop assignments are sloppy, the army descends into utter disorganization; (6) Rout (Bei): when a general, unable to estimate enemy strength, pits a weak force against a strong force or fails to place elite shock troops in his vanguard, his army is routed.

Sun Tzu emphasizes that a great commander views his soldiers as his own children; hence they will follow him into the deepest valleys. He loves them like his beloved sons; hence they will die by his side. But if he is affectionate without authority, lenient without discipline, and incapable of enforcing orders, his soldiers become like spoiled children—useless for any practical enterprise.

Now an army is exposed to six several calamities, not arising from natural causes, but from faults for which the general is responsible. These are: (1) Flight; (2) Insubordination; (3) Collapse; (4) Ruin; (5) Disorganization; (6) Rout.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Maintain strict parity between authority and frontline capability: never allow soldiers to overpower officers or vice versa.
  • Eliminate ambiguity in command: ensure every operational directive is clear, consistent, and rigorously enforced.
  • Combine deep paternal benevolence with absolute, unwavering discipline: spoiled organizations collapse under stress.

The Nine Situations and Tactical Responses

Chapter 11: The Nine Situations (Psychological Conditioning on Death Ground)
Tactical psychology shifts across geographic depth; marching troops deep into hostile territory binds them together in unyielding solidarity.

Chapter 11 represents Sun Tzu's longest and most psychologically profound treatise: Jiu Di—The Nine Situations. Ground is classified not merely by geometry, but by the psychological conditioning it induces in the soldiers: (1) Dispersive Ground: fighting on one's own home soil. Soldiers are close to their families and will scatter at the first sign of defeat. Rule: do not fight; (2) Facile Ground: penetrating shallowly into enemy territory. Troops can easily retreat. Rule: do not halt; (3) Contentious Ground: territory that confers enormous advantage to whichever side seizes it. Rule: do not attack him if he holds it; (4) Open Ground: country where both sides can maneuver freely. Rule: do not block the enemy's path; (5) Intersecting Ground: territory bordered by three rival states; whoever controls it commands the empire. Rule: join hands with your allies; (6) Heavy Ground: marching deep into enemy territory, leaving hostile cities in your rear. Rule: forage on the country; (7) Bad Ground: difficult terrain of marshes, forests, and cliffs. Rule: press on steadily without halting; (8) Hemmed-in Ground: narrow defiles where a small force can crush a massive army. Rule: resort to stratagem; (9) Death Ground (Si Di): ground where an army can only survive by fighting with desperate, reckless fury. Rule: fight without hesitation.

The art of war recognizes nine varieties of ground: (1) Dispersive ground; (2) Facile ground; (3) Contentious ground; (4) Open ground; (5) Ground of intersecting highways; (6) Serious ground; (7) Difficult ground; (8) Hemmed-in ground; (9) Desperate ground.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Never engage in decisive battles on home territory: local proximity encourages organizational dispersal and desertion.
  • Push deeply into competitive campaigns to forge unbreakable internal solidarity among your frontline teams.
  • Form diplomatic and commercial alliances when operating on intersecting crossroads territory.

The Mount Chang Serpent (Shuai-ran) & Total Secrecy

Chapter 11: The Nine Situations (Psychological Conditioning on Death Ground)
Place soldiers on Death Ground and they will fight with supernatural ferocity; an elite army moves in organic unison like the mythical serpent of Mount Chang.

Sun Tzu presents the ideal military organism: the Shuai-ran, the legendary two-headed serpent of Mount Chang. If you strike its head, its tail lashes out to attack; if you strike its tail, its head snaps around to bite; if you strike its middle, both head and tail attack simultaneously in unison. When asked whether an army can be trained to emulate the Shuai-ran, Sun Tzu answers with an absolute yes: the people of Wu and Yue were bitter blood enemies, yet when they crossed a stormy river in the same boat and were caught in a gale, they assisted one another like the left hand and the right hand.

How does a general achieve this organic unison? Not by tying up horses or burying chariot wheels, but by managing the psychology of despair. Throw your soldiers into positions from which there is no escape (Tou Zhi Wang Di Er Hou Cun), and they will display supernatural valor. On Death Ground, fear vanishes; when there is no refuge, soldiers stand firm; when deep in enemy territory, they close ranks; when there is no alternative, they fight with unyielding ferocity. A great general leads his army like a shepherd leading a flock of sheep, driving them this way and that, while none know where they are going. He burns his boats behind them and smashes his cooking pots, plunging them into the abyss of Death Ground to extract total victory.

Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape, and they will prefer death to flight. If they will face death, there is nothing they may not achieve... For it is the soldier's disposition to offer an obstinate resistance when surrounded, to fight hard when he cannot help himself.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Design organizational feedback loops where every department automatically supports any division that comes under attack.
  • Eliminate escape hatches when executing critical strategic initiatives: commitment forces peak human performance.
  • Burn the boats: remove safety nets to transform complacency into focused, relentless execution.

The Five Methods of Incendiary Assault & Meteorological Windows

Chapter 12: The Attack by Fire (Incendiary Operations & Environmental Warfare)
Incendiary warfare requires strict environmental timing and material preparation, synchronizing fire with favorable wind and celestial seasons.

Chapter 12 details Huo Gong—the attack by fire and environmental weapons. Sun Tzu categorizes five targets for incendiary destruction: (1) Burning enemy personnel in their camp; (2) Burning their stored food provisions; (3) Burning their supply baggage-trains; (4) Burning their arsenals and weapon magazines; (5) Dropping incendiary weapons directly into their formation. Fire is an asymmetrical force multiplier that induces immediate, widespread panic and destroys logistics without requiring hand-to-hand combat.

However, launching a fire attack requires rigorous preparation and meteorological precision: material for igniting fire must be prepared long in advance, and the assault must be coordinated with the physical weather. Sun Tzu provides the precise celestial conditions: fire should only be raised in dry seasons, on days when the wind rises, specifically when the moon is in the astrological mansions of Ji (The Sieve), Bi (The Wall), Yi (The Wing), or Zhen (The Crossbar), for these are the days when ferocious winds blow. If the wind blows from the front, do not attack from downwind; fire burns with the wind, and attacking into it will consume your own forces.

There are five ways of attacking with fire. The first is to burn soldiers in their camp; the second is to burn stores; the third is to burn baggage-trains; the fourth is to burn arsenals and magazines; the fifth is to hurl fire among the enemy.

Emotional Regulation of the Sovereign and General

Chapter 12: The Attack by Fire (Incendiary Operations & Environmental Warfare)
A ruler must never launch a war out of personal anger, nor a general fight out of temporary pique; a destroyed state can never be restored, nor the dead brought back to life.

At the climax of Chapter 12, Sun Tzu delivers one of the most solemn, ethical admonitions in military history. Having explained the devastating destructive power of fire, he warns against the ultimate sin of leadership: using military force to satisfy personal emotional rage. 'A ruler must never mobilize an army simply out of anger; a general must never engage in battle simply out of pique' (Zhu Bu Ke Yi Nu Er Xing Shi, Jiang Bu Ke Yi Yun Er Zhi Zhan).

Why? Because human emotions are transient, but the consequences of war are eternal: 'An angry man may become happy again, and a wrathful heart may be soothed; but a nation that has been destroyed can never be restored to existence, nor can the dead ever be brought back to life.' The enlightened ruler is therefore heedful and cautious; the competent general is vigilant and restrained. This is the ultimate Tao of keeping the state at peace and preserving the military intact.

A ruler must not put forces into the field simply for pique; a general must not fight simply for anger... For an angry man may succeed to be happy again, and churlishness may be succeeded by mirth; but a destroyed state can never exist again, nor can the dead ever be brought back to life.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Impose an absolute ban on emotional decision-making: never initiate high-stakes conflicts out of anger, pride, or wounded ego.
  • Remember that destruction is irreversible: destroyed capital and burned bridges can never be resurrected.
  • Practice disciplined restraint: the highest mark of leadership is the strength to walk away from unnecessary fights.

The Five Classes of Espionage & The Divine Skein

Chapter 13: The Use of Spies (Yong Jian / Espionage and Foreknowledge)
Foreknowledge is the supreme weapon of war; spending immense fortunes on armies while begrudging small sums for espionage is the height of inhumanity.

In the thirteenth and final chapter, Sun Tzu lays down the economic and moral imperative of intelligence. Mobilizing 100,000 men and maintaining them in the field for years costs the state enormous fortunes and causes immense suffering to the peasantry. If a ruler or general refuses to spend a few hundred ounces of silver on espionage to discover the enemy's plans, resulting in a bloody, blind defeat, he is guilty of the height of inhumanity: 'He is no leader of men, no help to his sovereign, and no master of victory.'

Sun Tzu codifies the Five Classes of Spies (Wu Jian): (1) Local Spies: recruiting ordinary residents of the enemy's territory; (2) Inward Spies: recruiting enemy government officials who are disgruntled, passed over for promotion, or corrupt; (3) Converted Spies (Double Agents): capturing the enemy's spies, bribing them lavishly, and sending them back to feed false intelligence to the enemy; (4) Doomed Spies: our own agents who are deliberately fed false information about our plans, so that when captured and tortured by the enemy, they reveal the falsehood with complete sincerity; (5) Surviving Spies: elite operatives who infiltrate deep, observe everything, and return safely to deliver critical reports. When all five classes are operating simultaneously, it is called the 'Divine Skein' (Shen Ji)—a web of invisible foreknowledge that is the sovereign's most precious treasure.

Now this lack of foreknowledge regarding the enemy's conditions, which causes one to squander a hundred thousand pieces of gold on armies while begrudging a hundred pieces in honors and salaries, is the height of inhumanity. One who acts thus is no leader of men.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Invest aggressively in market intelligence: skimping on research and reconnaissance while spending fortunes on marketing is organizational malpractice.
  • Recruit insiders and converted informants: double agents and industry insiders provide the highest-fidelity strategic data.
  • Cultivate the Divine Skein: build multiple, overlapping streams of independent intelligence to cross-verify reality.

The Sovereign Secret of Foreknowledge

Chapter 13: The Use of Spies (Yong Jian / Espionage and Foreknowledge)
Foreknowledge cannot be gained from spirits, gods, analogies, or calculations; it must be obtained directly from human beings who know the enemy's condition.

Sun Tzu closes his masterpiece by establishing the rationalist epistemology of foreknowledge (Xian Zhi). What enables the enlightened ruler and the great commander to strike, conquer, and achieve triumphs beyond the reach of ordinary mortals is prior knowledge. But where does foreknowledge come from? In ancient times, kings consulted shamans, sacrificed animals, examined oracle bones, and prayed to spirits. Sun Tzu shatters this superstition completely: 'Foreknowledge cannot be elicited from ghosts and spirits; it cannot be gained inductively from past experience; it cannot be calculated by deductive mathematical rules. It must be obtained exclusively from men who know the enemy's condition.'

Sun Tzu concludes with a sweeping historical synthesis: the rise of the great Shang Dynasty was engineered by Yi Yin, an elite minister who served inside the court of the Xia Dynasty and understood its corruption. The rise of the glorious Zhou Dynasty was guided by Jiang Ziya (Lu Wang), who knew the internal weaknesses of the Yin Dynasty. Only the most enlightened ruler and wise general can deploy the highest intellects as spies, and thereby achieve world-historical victory. Spies are the most vital element in war, because on their reports the entire army's movements depend.

What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge. Now this foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation. Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.
Actionable Strategic Heuristics:
  • Base your strategic decisions on verified human intelligence, never on superstition, optimistic speculation, or algorithmic extrapolation.
  • Deploy your smartest, most ethical, and most resourceful operators into intelligence and reconnaissance roles.
  • Recognize that competitive victory is determined by asymmetric information: the side that sees first, moves first, and wins first.
