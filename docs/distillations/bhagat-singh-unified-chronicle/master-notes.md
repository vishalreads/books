## Movement I: The Crucible of Ancestry and Blood-Soaked Soil (1840–1919) {#movement-1}
*Timeframe: 1840 – 1919*

> **Movement Summary**: The revolutionary lineage of the Sandhu Jat family in Khatkar Kalan, the 1907 agrarian rebellion, Ghadar martyrs, and the harrowing trauma of Jallianwala Bagh.

### The Roots in Khatkar Kalan and the Sandhu Rebel Heritage (1840 – 1906)

The revolutionary temper of Bhagat Singh was neither an accident of youth nor an imported ideology; it was the biological and cultural inheritance of generations rooted in the soil of Khatkar Kalan. Situated in the Doaba region of Punjab, Khatkar Kalan was a fortified ancestral village whose Sandhu Jat clan possessed a fierce tradition of resistance dating back to the Anglo-Sikh wars of the 1840s.

Bhagat Singh’s grandfather, Sardar Arjun Singh, was a man of profound moral conviction—an early Arya Samaj reformer who blended Vedic reformism with open disdain for feudal subservience. His three sons—Kishen Singh (Bhagat Singh's father), Ajit Singh, and Swaran Singh—grew to maturity in an atmosphere charged with agrarian crisis and colonial exploitation. Under British colonial irrigation schemes, the peasantry of the Chenab and Bari Doab canal colonies were subjected to crushing water rates, draconian colonization bills, and arbitrary tenancy laws that reduced proud freeholders to serfs on their own lands.

In response, the family home became an underground clearinghouse for nationalist literature and anti-colonial organizing. When Bhagat Singh was born on September 28, 1907, in the village of Banga (Lyallpur district, now in Pakistan), his birth coincided with the release of his father Kishen Singh from Central Jail, Lahore, and his uncle Swaran Singh from Bareilly jail, while his uncle Sardar Ajit Singh was returning from exile in Mandalay. Celebrating this auspicious convergence, his grandmother declared the newborn child 'Bhaganwala'—the child of good fortune—which was shortened to Bhagat.

> **Sardar Ajit Singh** (Memoirs of Ajit Singh (Buried Alive)):
> "Our family has always held its head high against despotism. We knew that freedom could not be begged from rulers who held an empire at the point of a bayonet; it had to be wrested through sacrifice."

- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 1, pp. 3–18 (Archival records of the Sandhu family lineage and British colonial land settlement files.)
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, Chronology, pp. 287–290 (Biographical details of Bhagat Singh's birth and family arrests in 1907.)

### The 1907 Agrarian Revolt and 'Pagri Sambhal Jatta' (March – May 1907)

In the spring of 1907, Punjab erupted in its first mass political agitation since 1857. The Colonization Bill introduced by the Punjab Government sought to dispossess peasants of proprietary rights, enforce primogeniture, and permit summary fines for planting trees. Sardar Ajit Singh, alongside Sufi Amba Prasad and Kishen Singh, founded the clandestine Bharat Mata Society (Anjuman-i-Mohibban-i-Watan) and launched a ferocious campaign across the canal colonies.

At a massive peasant rally in Lyallpur on March 3, 1907, Banke Dayal, editor of the Jhang Syal, recited his electrifying poem: 'Pagri Sambhal Jatta, Pagri Sambhal Oye!' (Guard your turban, O peasant!). The slogan became the battle-cry of rural Punjab. The British administration panicked. Governor Sir Denzil Ibbetson warned the Viceroy that 'the situation is dangerously critical... the loyalty of the native army is being tampered with.'

Invoking the draconian Regulation III of 1818, the colonial government arrested Sardar Ajit Singh and Lala Lajpat Rai and deported them without trial to the remote fortress of Mandalay in Burma. Swaran Singh, subjected to brutal forced labor in Bareilly Jail, contracted tuberculosis and died at the young age of twenty-three. Ajit Singh eventually fled into permanent exile across Persia, Turkey, Germany, and South America, not to return until the eve of independence in 1947. Young Bhagat Singh grew up surrounded by the enduring absence of his uncle and the silent grief of his widowed aunt, Harnam Kaur.

> **Banke Dayal** (Lyallpur Peasant Rally Anthem, March 1907):
> "Pagri Sambhal Jatta, Pagri Sambhal Oye! / Lut liyan maal tera, apna bana ke / Raj ehda bura oye!"

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 2, pp. 19–34 (Detailed examination of the Bharat Mata Society secret papers.)
- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 2, pp. 9–14 (The family's radical political heritage under British police surveillance.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: British intelligence files preserved in Kew reveal that Viceroy Minto privately conceded that the Punjab Colonization Bill was inequitable, yet refused to commute the deportations immediately to preserve the colonial myth of unbending imperial strength.

### Kartar Singh Sarabha, the Ghadarites, and Jallianwala Bagh (1914 – April 1919)

During the First World War, the revolutionary epicenter shifted to the Hindustan Ghadar Party, founded in San Francisco by expatriate Punjabi workers and intellectuals under Lala Har Dayal and Sohan Singh Bhakna. Among them rose Kartar Singh Sarabha, a nineteen-year-old student who returned to India to organize an armed rebellion within the British Indian Army. The uprising was betrayed by informants, and on November 16, 1915, Sarabha was hanged in Lahore Central Jail, declaring on the scaffold that he was proud to die for the motherland.

Sarabha became Bhagat Singh’s lifelong hero. Bhagat carried a photograph of Sarabha in his pocket until his final breath and would frequently gaze upon it when contemplating his own path of sacrifice.

Then came April 13, 1919. At Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Brigadier General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire without warning on an unarmed assembly of over twenty thousand men, women, and children trapped in an enclosed ground. When the firing ceased after 1,650 rounds, hundreds lay dead and thousands maimed. The twelve-year-old Bhagat Singh was at school in Lahore. Upon hearing the news, he skipped classes, boarded a train to Amritsar, and walked alone through the bloodstained garden. Kneeling among the bullet-riddled brick walls, he picked up a handful of earth soaked in the blood of martyrs, placed it in a small glass phial, and brought it home. For years, he kept that phial on his desk, adorning it with fresh flowers each morning—a silent, sacred vow that India's subjugation would be avenged.

> **Kartar Singh Sarabha** (Before the Gallows, Lahore Central Jail, November 1915):
> "If I had to live more lives than one, I would sacrifice each one of them for the freedom of my country. Glory to the revolution!"

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 3–4, pp. 35–54 (The impact of Sarabha's photograph and the physical journey to Jallianwala Bagh.)
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, 'Shri Kartar Singh Sarabha', pp. 115–124 (Bhagat Singh's detailed biographical tribute to Sarabha published in Chand (1928).)
- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 1, pp. 3–8 (The psychological impact of the 1919 martial law terror on young revolutionaries.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Nationalist folklore often depicted Bhagat Singh's visit to Jallianwala Bagh as an impulsive childlike gesture; however, Chaman Lal's analysis of his 1928 *Chand* essay reveals that Bhagat Singh saw Jallianwala Bagh not merely as an atrocity, but as the moment when the colonial state stripped away all pretense of civilizational legitimacy.

## Movement II: Awakening, National College, and Flight to Kanpur (1920–1924) {#movement-2}
*Timeframe: 1920 – 1924*

> **Movement Summary**: Refusal of British schooling, the intellectual explosion at National College Lahore, the Chauri Chaura fracture, and flight to Kanpur under the alias 'Balwant Singh'.

### National College Lahore: The Intellectual Crucible (1921 – 1923)

When Mahatma Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement in 1920, calling upon students to boycott government-aided educational institutions, fourteen-year-old Bhagat Singh immediately walked out of the D.A.V. High School in Lahore. To absorb these radicalized youth, Lala Lajpat Rai established the National College inside the Bradlaugh Hall complex in Lahore.

The National College was not a conventional school; it was an incubator for anti-colonial intellectuals. The faculty included brilliant scholars like Bhai Parmanand and Professor Jaichandra Vidyalankar, a historian who taught ancient Indian history, world revolutions, and the dynamics of statecraft. Here, Bhagat Singh met his lifelong comrades: Sukhdev Thapar, Yashpal, Bhagwati Charan Vohra, and Tirath Ram.

Bhagat was an omnivorous reader. He spent hours in the Dwarka Das Library, reading history, economics, and political philosophy. He devoured accounts of the French Revolution, the Italian Risorgimento (particularly Mazzini and Garibaldi), the Irish Sinn Féin struggle, and the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution in Russia. He participated actively in the college drama club, playing the lead roles of Maharana Pratap and Shivaji, using historical theater to awaken patriotic consciousness.

> **Professor Jaichandra Vidyalankar** (National College Lahore Faculty Recollections):
> "Bhagat Singh read voraciously. While other students were content with textbooks, he was wrestling with Rousseau, Voltaire, and the history of European revolutions."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 5–6, pp. 55–72 (Curriculum and political climate of National College at Bradlaugh Hall.)
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, 'Students and Politics', pp. 43–48 (Bhagat Singh's essay in Kirti (July 1928) defending student political activism.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: British surveillance files noted with alarm that National College was graduating young men whose hostility to the Crown was framed not in religious or communal terms, but through the modern vocabulary of constitutional subversion and revolutionary republicanism.

### The Chauri Chaura Fracture and Flight to Kanpur (February 1922 – late 1923)

In February 1922, following the incident at Chauri Chaura in Uttar Pradesh where an enraged crowd set fire to a police station, killing twenty-two policemen, Gandhi abruptly suspended the nationwide Non-Cooperation Movement. For millions of Indians, but particularly for the youth of Punjab and Bengal, this unilateral withdrawal was a devastating psychological betrayal. Just as the colonial apparatus was reeling under mass pressure, the movement was halted on grounds of non-violence.

Disillusioned with Gandhian methods, the youth began seeking an uncompromising alternative. At this juncture in late 1923, family pressure intervened: Bhagat Singh's father and grandmother arranged for his marriage to a girl from a respectable family. To a young man who had pledged his existence to the liberation of his motherland, domestic entanglement was unthinkable.

Leaving a letter for his father on his desk, Bhagat slipped out of his home in the dead of night. His letter was polite but unyielding: 'My life has been dedicated to the noblest cause, that of the freedom of the country. Therefore, there is no rest or worldly desire that can lure me now.' He boarded a train for Kanpur, carrying only a small bundle of clothes and a recommendation letter from Prof. Vidyalankar to Ganesh Shankar Vidyarthi, editor of the radical Hindi daily *Pratap*.

> **Bhagat Singh** (Letter to his Father, Sardar Kishen Singh, late 1923):
> "My life has been dedicated to the noblest cause, that of the freedom of the country. Therefore, there is no rest or worldly desire that can lure me now... I hope you will forgive me."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 7, pp. 73–88 (The flight to Kanpur and Vidyarthi's underground press network.)
- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 2, pp. 14–17 (Analysis of Bhagat Singh's ideological break from Gandhian Congress orthodoxy.)
- **Bhagat Singh (Primary)**: LeftWord Jail Notebook Edition, 'Letters', pp. 145–147 (Full text of the 1923 runaway letter.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Noorani highlights that this letter disproves the colonial prosecution's later attempt to portray Bhagat Singh as an aimless hothead; at barely sixteen, his life was governed by conscious philosophical commitment.

### Undercover in Kanpur as 'Balwant Singh' (1923 – 1924)

In Kanpur, Ganesh Shankar Vidyarthi took the fugitive teenager under his wing, giving him employment at the *Pratap* printing press under the pseudonym 'Balwant Singh'. Kanpur was a bustling industrial metropolis, filled with textile mills, railway yards, and an emerging, exploited working class. It was here that Bhagat Singh witnessed firsthand the brutal machinery of industrial capitalism.

At the *Pratap* office, Bhagat came into direct contact with the leaders of the newly formed Hindustan Republican Association (HRA), including Sachindra Nath Sanyal (author of *Bandi Jeevan*), Suresh Chandra Bhattacharya, and Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee. Bhagat proved to be an invaluable cadre—disciplined, fearless, and intellectually sophisticated. He volunteered for flood relief work along the Yamuna, organized literacy circles for factory laborers, and learned Bengali in order to read underground revolutionary literature in its original language.

When his grandmother fell gravely ill in early 1924 and his father gave an explicit assurance that he would not be pressured into marriage, Bhagat returned briefly to Punjab. But he returned transformed: he was now an active, initiated member of an all-India revolutionary conspiracy.

> **Ganesh Shankar Vidyarthi** (Reflections on young 'Balwant Singh', Pratap Press):
> "He possessed a burning passion for the downtrodden. In the press, he worked tirelessly, never caring for sleep or comfort, reading every socialist paper we received from abroad."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 8, pp. 89–104 (The underground printing apparatus and flood relief work in Kanpur.)
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, Chronology, pp. 291–292 (Details of HRA initiation under Sachindra Nath Sanyal.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Waraich's police recovery files document that during his time in Kanpur, British intelligence had no idea that 'Balwant Singh' of the *Pratap* press was the absconding son of Kishen Singh of Lahore, demonstrating Bhagat's early mastery of underground tradecraft.

## Movement III: Ideological Crystallization and the Early Radical Essays (1924–1926) {#movement-3}
*Timeframe: 1924 – 1926*

> **Movement Summary**: The founding of Naujawan Bharat Sabha, essays on Untouchability, Universal Love, and Communal Riots; defining anti-colonialism through social emancipation.

### The Founding of Naujawan Bharat Sabha (March 1926) (March 1926)

Recognizing that secret conspiratorial societies isolated revolutionaries from the broader masses, Bhagat Singh, alongside Ram Chandra and Bhagwati Charan Vohra, founded the **Naujawan Bharat Sabha** (Youth Society of India) in Lahore in March 1926. It was designed as an open, legal front organization to mobilize students, young workers, and peasants.

The Sabha was radically secular and explicitly socialist. Its founding manifesto barred any member of a communal organization—Hindu Mahasabha, Muslim League, or Akali Dal—from joining. Every recruit was required to sign a pledge placing the country and the emancipation of the poor above all religious dogma. At their gatherings, communal dining was enforced: Hindu, Muslim, Sikh, and Dalit members ate food prepared and served from the same dishes to break centuries of caste prejudice.

The Sabha organized lantern slide lectures on the French and Russian revolutions, set up libraries, distributed prohibited tracts, and arranged patriotic debates across Punjab. Bhagat served as its founding general secretary. British intelligence soon realized that this was no ordinary debating club; it was an ideological army in the making.

> **Naujawan Bharat Sabha Manifesto** (Drafted by Bhagat Singh & Bhagwati Charan Vohra, 1926):
> "Religious superstitions and communalism are the greatest hindrances to our progress. We must tear down these walls and unite on the basis of shared economic exploitation and universal human equality."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 9–10, pp. 105–124 (The constitutional rules, oath, and lantern-slide propaganda of the Sabha.)
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, 'Youth', pp. 35–42 (Bhagat Singh's essay in Matwala (1924) on the historic mission of youth.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: British Punjab Secret Abstract No. 18 of 1926 warned that the Naujawan Bharat Sabha was uniquely dangerous because it avoided sectarian rhetoric, making it impossible for the police to provoke internal Hindu-Muslim divisions among its ranks.

### The Problem of Untouchability: Caste Emancipation as Revolution (June 1928 (Drafted 1926))

While conventional nationalist leaders treated the abolition of untouchability as a matter of moral charity or patronizing reform, Bhagat Singh analyzed caste through the lens of radical socio-economic liberation. In his landmark essay *Achhut Samasya* (The Problem of Untouchability), published in *Kirti*, he laid bare the hypocrisy of high-caste nationalists.

He pointed out that while an orthodox Hindu would pet a dog or touch an animal without hesitation, touching a fellow human being who scavenged or swept was deemed polluting. He scathingly criticized the Congress for wanting freedom from British rule while maintaining an ancient system of domestic slavery: 'Our people want self-rule, but refuse to grant human dignity to thirty million brethren among us!'

Crucially, Bhagat Singh did not ask upper castes to 'uplift' the oppressed. Instead, he directly addressed the Dalits, urging them to organize, rebel, and seize their rights independently: 'Organize yourselves! You are the true working class... Do not look toward others for salvation. Rise, unite, and shatter the chains of centuries!'

> **Bhagat Singh** (The Problem of Untouchability (Kirti, June 1928)):
> "You are the real workers... unite and organize. Never be subservient to the so-called upper classes. The real revolution will begin only when you shake off this age-old stigma and demand your rightful place as equals."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, 'The Issue of Untouchability', pp. 63–70 (Full translation and contextual analysis of Achhut Samasya.)
- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 2, pp. 20–23 (Bhagat Singh's advanced social philosophy compared to contemporary leaders.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Chaman Lal observes that Bhagat Singh's writings on caste predate Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) by eight years, demonstrating that the revolutionary movement was far ahead of mainstream politics in recognizing caste as an economic and structural engine of oppression.

### Universal Love and the Antidote to Communal Riots (1924 – 1927)

Between 1924 and 1927, India was engulfed in a wave of horrific communal riots in Kohat, Calcutta, Multan, and Delhi. Mainstream leaders either indulged in mutual recrimination or offered sentimental appeals for harmony. Bhagat Singh cut through the confusion with ruthless dialectical clarity in two master essays: *Vishwa Prem* (Universal Love) and *Sampradayik Dange aur Unka Ilaj* (Communal Riots and Their Solution).

In *Communal Riots*, Bhagat Singh identified the true instigators: opportunistic communal politicians who served colonial masters, and a venomous, sensationalist press that earned profits by stoking religious hatred: 'To make people fight each other in the name of religion, newspapers spew venom... journalism, once a noble profession, has become a criminal enterprise.'

His proposed solution was materialist: class unity. He argued that a Hindu worker and a Muslim worker shared the exact same material misery—crushing debt, starvation wages, and merciless exploitation by landlords and factory owners. 'Teach the workers that their true enemy is not the fellow laborer who prays differently, but the capitalist and the colonial exploiter. Once economic class consciousness awakens, religious fanaticism will evaporate.'

> **Bhagat Singh** (Communal Riots and Their Solution (Kirti, June 1927)):
> "Class consciousness is the only sovereign cure for communal poison. When the poor realize that their real enemies are the exploiters—whether Hindu, Muslim, or British—they will unite to destroy the real oppressor."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Chaman Lal & Michael D. Yates**: The Political Writings of Bhagat Singh, 'The Religious Riots and Their Solution', pp. 53–62 (Original Kirti publication analysis.)
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 11, pp. 125–138 (The secularist policy of Naujawan Bharat Sabha in counteracting Punjab communal tension.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Noorani notes that Bhagat Singh's analysis of communal riots as economic diversion engineered by colonial authorities and local elites remains the most rigorous Marxist critique produced in 1920s India.

## Movement IV: Terror, Prison, and the Transition to Scientific Socialism (1926–1928) {#movement-4}
*Timeframe: 1926 – 1928*

> **Movement Summary**: The Kakori hangings, arrest on Dussehra bomb charges, solitary confinement in Lahore Fort, and the transformative secret Amritsar dialogues with Sohan Singh Josh.

### The Shock of Kakori and Solitary Confinement in Lahore Fort (August 1925 – May 1927)

In August 1925, the HRA executed the daring Kakori train robbery near Lucknow to seize government funds for arms. The colonial crackdown was swift and merciless. Over forty revolutionaries were arrested, and in December 1927, four towering leaders—Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqullah Khan, Roshan Singh, and Rajendra Lahiri—were sent to the gallows, while Sachindra Nath Sanyal was exiled for life to the Andamans. The HRA's organizational network across North India was decimated.

In Lahore, the police tightened their grip on Bhagat Singh. On October 28, 1926, a bomb exploded during the Dussehra festival procession in Lahore, killing several people. The police, unable to find the perpetrators, used it as a pretext to destroy Bhagat Singh. On May 29, 1927, he was seized while walking near the railway station and thrown into the dreaded Lahore Fort.

For five agonizing weeks, he was held in solitary confinement in a subterranean cell, interrogated around the clock, deprived of sleep, and subjected to physical and psychological torture by CID Superintendent Tegart's subordinates. The police demanded that he reveal the whereabouts of absconding HRA cadres in exchange for money and freedom in England. Bhagat Singh refused to utter a single name. Eventually, with no evidence linking him to the bombing, he was released on a crushing bail of sixty thousand rupees—an astronomical sum raised through the courage of local nationalists, including his father Kishen Singh and Dunichand of Lahore.

> **Bhagat Singh** (Recollections of Lahore Fort interrogation, 1927):
> "They told me that my comrades had confessed and that I was heading for the gallows. I told them that if my death could hasten the freedom of my country, they were welcome to hang me that very evening."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 12–13, pp. 139–156 (Declassified interrogation transcripts from Lahore Fort CID files.)
- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 2, pp. 18–20 (The Dussehra bomb arrest and the punitive bail conditions.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Juss's examination of the Special Branch records demonstrates that the British CID knew Bhagat Singh was completely innocent of the Dussehra bomb, but deliberately held him in Lahore Fort under preventive detention to break his spirit and force an ideological capitulation.

### The Amritsar Dialogue: Transition to Scientific Socialism (Late 1927 – Early 1928)

Following his release, Bhagat Singh operated in Amritsar under tight surveillance, working for the Punjabi Marxist journal *Kirti*, edited by Sohan Singh Josh. It was during these intense months that Bhagat Singh underwent a decisive ideological transformation, transitioning permanently from revolutionary romanticism (individual pistol attacks and isolated heroism) to scientific Marxism.

Sohan Singh Josh, an early leader of the Communist Party of India and the Workers and Peasants Party, engaged in long, nocturnal debates with Bhagat Singh in the *Kirti* office. Josh challenged him: 'Individual assassination cannot overthrow an empire. You kill one Scott or one Dwyer, and another bureaucrat takes his place. Real revolution requires organizing the millions of factory workers and landless peasants who produce the wealth of society.'

Bhagat Singh absorbed this critique deeply. He spent nights reading Marx's *Capital*, Lenin's *The State and Revolution*, and Trotsky's *History of the Russian Revolution*. In his subsequent writings for *Kirti*, Bhagat systematically repudiated terror for terror's sake: 'The bomb and the pistol are not our destination; they are merely instruments of defense when peaceful organizing is crushed. The true revolution is the overthrow of the capitalist order and the establishment of the dictatorship of the proletariat.'

> **Sohan Singh Josh** (My Meetings with Bhagat Singh, pp. 12–18):
> "Bhagat Singh was not a terrorist in any sense of the word. He was an intellectual revolutionary searching for the correct scientific methodology to emancipate the Indian working class from British capital."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **Sohan Singh Josh**: My Meetings with Bhagat Singh and on Other Early Revolutionaries, Chapters 1–3, pp. 1–28 (Eyewitness account of discussions in the Kirti office.)

## Movement V: The Historic Conclave at Ferozeshah Kotla (September 1928) {#movement-5}
*Timeframe: September 8–9, 1928*

> **Movement Summary**: The clandestine meeting in the ruins of Ferozeshah Kotla, Delhi; adoption of 'Socialism', creation of the HSRA, and collective leadership.

### The Ruins of Ferozeshah Kotla: Rebranding the Revolution (September 8–9, 1928)

On September 8 and 9, 1928, ten young men slipped quietly past the crumbling stone ramparts of the 14th-century fortress of Ferozeshah Kotla in Delhi. They represented the surviving revolutionary networks of Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Bengal. Among them were Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev from Punjab; Shiv Verma, Bejoy Kumar Sinha, and Jaidev Kapur from UP; and Kundan Lal from Rajasthan. Chandrashekhar Azad, the legendary military chief of the HRA who was evading a nationwide manhunt, did not attend the open debate due to security risks, but gave Bhagat Singh full proxy power.

Inside the desolate ruins, away from police spies, Bhagat Singh mounted a passionate argument that redefined the entire Indian liberation movement. He proposed two revolutionary changes: first, to rename the Hindustan Republican Association to the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA)**; and second, to discard individual command in favor of collective democratic centralism.

Some older cadres hesitated, arguing that 'Socialism' was an alien Western doctrine that would alienate conservative donors. Bhagat Singh retorted with fierce eloquence: 'Our struggle is not merely to drive away British rulers and replace them with brown Indian capitalists and landlords! We want the total abolition of all exploitation of man by man!' After hours of debate under the flickering light of candles, Bhagat Singh's resolution was passed unanimously. The word **Socialist** was permanently etched into their name.

> **Bhagat Singh** (Ferozeshah Kotla Debate, September 8, 1928 (reported by Shiv Verma)):
> "If we fight merely to place Indian capitalists on the throne vacated by British bureaucrats, then the condition of our laborers and peasants will remain identical. Our goal is Socialism—the total liberation of the working masses."

### The Architecture of Democratic Centralism (September 9, 1928)

The Ferozeshah Kotla conference did not just produce a new name; it created an entirely new operational architecture. The HSRA was organized on the Bolshevik principle of democratic centralism. A Central Committee of seven members was elected to direct the all-India struggle: Bhagat Singh was appointed in charge of ideological education, propaganda, and Punjab affairs; Sukhdev was made Punjab organizer; Shiv Verma and Bejoy Kumar Sinha were assigned UP; and Chandrashekhar Azad was designated the Commander-in-Chief of the military wing (the Hindustan Socialist Republican Army).

Crucially, all individual terror actions were banned. No member was permitted to carry out an armed attack or robbery without the prior collective authorization of the Central Committee. Furthermore, all religious rituals and oaths over holy texts (which had been customary in the early Anushilan and HRA traditions) were completely discarded. The sacred bond uniting the cadres was no longer religious mysticism, but scientific devotion to the socialist revolution.

> **HSRA Central Committee Resolution** (Adopted at Ferozeshah Kotla, September 1928):
> "No action shall be undertaken out of personal whim or vengeance. Every stroke of arms must serve as an ideological trumpet call to awaken the Indian masses."

## Movement VI: The Simon Commission and Lala Lajpat Rai's Martyrdom (October–November 1928) {#movement-6}
*Timeframe: October 30 – November 17, 1928*

> **Movement Summary**: The arrival of the all-white Simon Commission in Lahore, the brutal police lathi charge led by Scott, Lalaji's historic dying prophecy, and the national crisis.

### The Black Flags of Lahore: October 30, 1928 (October 30, 1928)

In 1927, the British government appointed the Indian Statutory Commission, headed by Sir John Simon, to report on constitutional reforms. In a brazen act of imperial arrogance, not a single Indian was included among its seven members. Across the subcontinent, every political formation—from the Congress to the Muslim League—declared an absolute boycott.

On October 30, 1928, the Simon Commission arrived by train at the Lahore railway station. Outside the station, a massive sea of over twenty thousand demonstrators gathered, holding black flags inscribed with the words 'Simon Go Back!'. The protest was spearheaded by the venerable 'Lion of Punjab', sixty-three-year-old Lala Lajpat Rai, alongside leaders of the Naujawan Bharat Sabha.

Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and their comrades were in the thick of the crowd, maintaining discipline. The police barricades held the protestors back behind barbed wire. As the demonstrators chanted slogans peacefully, Senior Superintendent of Police (SSP) J.A. Scott and Assistant Superintendent of Police (ASP) J.P. Saunders ordered a brutal, unprovoked lathi charge.

> **Eyewitness Report** (The Tribune, Lahore, October 31, 1928):
> "The police wielded their heavy wooden batons with savage fury against an entirely peaceful crowd. The blows were aimed directly at the heads of the prominent leaders standing in the front line."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**

- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 3, pp. 28–32 (The legal and political ramifications of the Simon Commission boycott.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: British official reports attempted to portray the crowd as violent stone-throwers; however, the magisterial inquiry conceded that no police officer sustained injuries prior to the order to charge.

### The Blows on Lalaji and the Historic Prophecy (October 30 – November 17, 1928)

During the melee, SSP Scott singled out Lala Lajpat Rai. Scott took a heavy cane and struck the elderly leader repeatedly across the chest, shoulders, and head. A young nationalist, Manmohan Trikha, attempted to shield Lalaji, but was beaten unconscious. Bhagat Singh, standing a few yards away, saw Scott's brutal assault with his own eyes; his fists clenched, his blood boiling, restrained only by the collective discipline agreed upon by the party.

That evening, despite severe internal bleeding and bruising, Lala Lajpat Rai addressed an immense gathering at Mori Gate in Lahore. His voice, though physically weakened, rang out with prophetic fury:

'The blows struck at me today will be the last nails in the coffin of British rule in India.'

Lalaji never recovered from the trauma of the beating. His heart and lungs, already weakened, could not withstand the internal damage. On November 17, 1928, eighteen days after the assault, Lala Lajpat Rai died. The news shattered the subcontinent. A pall of humiliation and rage descended over the nation. In the British Parliament, the Secretary of State for India, Lord Birkenhead, cynically denied that police violence had caused Lalaji's death, dismissing the protest as an uncivilized mob riot.

> **Lala Lajpat Rai** (Speech at Mori Gate, Lahore, October 30, 1928):
> "Every blow that was hurled on us this afternoon will prove to be a nail in the coffin of the British Empire. I declare that the empire cannot stand on the brute power of batons."

## Movement VII: The Saunders Assassination and the Great Escape (December 1928) {#movement-7}
*Timeframe: December 17 – 24, 1928*

> **Movement Summary**: The execution of J.P. Saunders outside Lahore police headquarters, Azad's covering fire, the legendary HSRA posters, and Bhagat's disguise aboard the Calcutta Mail.

### The Retaliation Outside Lahore Police Headquarters (December 17, 1928, 4:15 PM)

Inside a secret safehouse in Lahore, the HSRA Central Committee met in an atmosphere of grim resolve. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Chandrashekhar Azad, and Rajguru concluded that if the murder of the foremost national leader went unanswered, the national spirit would be crushed under a sense of impotence. As Bhagat Singh argued: 'It is not a question of personal revenge. It is a question of national honor. The world must know that India is not dead, that her youth will strike back when insulted.'

The target was J.A. Scott. On the afternoon of December 17, 1928—exactly one month after Lalaji's death—an ambush was laid outside the Punjab Civil Secretariat and the District Police Headquarters. Jai Gopal was stationed across the road with a bicycle, pretending to fix a tire; his signal would identify Scott as he rode out on his motorcycle. Bhagat Singh and Shivaram Rajguru waited nearby, their coats concealing loaded revolvers. Chandrashekhar Azad, armed with a high-powered Mauser pistol, waited inside the compound of DAV College across the road to cover their retreat.

At 4:15 PM, a young British officer emerged from the gates riding a red motorcycle. Jai Gopal mistook him for Scott and signaled. In reality, the officer was twenty-one-year-old Assistant Superintendent of Police John Poyantz Saunders. As the motorcycle slowed to negotiate the road, Rajguru dashed forward and fired a single bullet into Saunders' chest, knocking him and the machine to the ground. Bhagat Singh rushed forward and fired four rapid bullets into Saunders' body to ensure he could not return fire. Both revolutionaries then sprinted through the gates of DAV College.

> **Jai Gopal (Approver)** (Deposition in the Lahore Conspiracy Case Tribunal, 1930):
> "I signaled as arranged when the motorcycle came out. Rajguru fired the first shot, and then Bhagat Singh ran up and emptied his pistol. Saunders fell instantly."

### Azad's Covering Fire and the Red Posters of Revenge (December 17 – 18, 1928)

As Bhagat and Rajguru sprinted through DAV College, Head Constable Chanan Singh and two constables gave chase, shouting for their capture. From behind a tree, Chandrashekhar Azad stepped out with his Mauser, calling out in Hindustani: 'Halt, or I fire!' Chanan Singh ignored the warning and pressed forward. Azad fired a single, deadly accurate shot that struck Chanan Singh in the thigh and groin, fatally wounding him. The remaining police pursuers fled in terror, allowing the three revolutionaries to escape over the college hostel boundary walls into the crowded alleys of Lahore.

That very night, under the illumination of kerosene lamps, Bhagat Singh and comrades drafted and printed hundreds of bold red posters bearing the emblem of the HSRA. By dawn on December 18, these posters were pasted on walls throughout Lahore, including the police headquarters itself:

'BUREAUCRACY BEWARE! Saunders is dead; Lalaji is avenged... We are sorry to have taken the life of a human being, but this man was part of an armed, ruthless machine that murders the leaders of our people. In this system, human life has no value. Long Live Revolution!'

The daring daytime assassination electrified the city. The entire British administration went into lockdown. Armored cars patrolled the Mall, and house-to-house raids commenced.

> **HSRA Red Poster** (Lahore Walls, December 18, 1928 (signed by Balraj, Commander-in-Chief)):
> "Saunders is dead; Lalaji is avenged... We are sorry to have taken the life of a human being, but in this system human life has no value. Revolution will put an end to this bloody machine."

### The Master Disguise: Boarding the 1 Down Calcutta Mail (December 20, 1928)

With five hundred police detectives and intelligence officers sealing off every railway station and highway in Punjab, escape seemed impossible. It was here that Bhagat Singh made the ultimate personal sacrifice for the cause: he shaved his sacred Sikh hair and beard—a profound cultural rupture that caused him immense personal anguish—and donned an impeccably tailored Western woolen suit and a stylish felt fedora.

Sukhdev arranged for Durgawati Devi ('Durga Bhabhi'), the courageous wife of revolutionary Bhagwati Charan Vohra, to play a pivotal role. Disguised as a wealthy, aristocratic young Indian couple traveling with their infant son Sachi, Bhagat Singh and Durga Bhabhi walked confidently into Lahore Railway Station in broad daylight. Rajguru, dressed in coarse peasant attire, followed them as their personal servant, carrying their luggage.

They purchased first-class tickets for the 1 Down Calcutta Mail. Police detectives scanned the crowds looking for a bearded Sikh youth in a turban; they never glanced twice at the elegant sahib in a fedora escorting his beautiful wife and child. Simultaneously, Chandrashekhar Azad slipped out disguised as a holy sadhu with saffron robes and chimta, traveling in a train compartment filled with pilgrims. The escape was total. By the time the train crossed Kanpur and reached Calcutta, the British police were left searching empty shadows in Lahore.

> **Durgawati Devi (Durga Bhabhi)** (Personal Interview & Oral History, Nehru Memorial Museum):
> "Bhagat Singh walked with utter composure. I was holding my baby tightly, knowing that if we were recognized, none of us would leave the platform alive. He looked like an English gentleman."

## Movement VIII: The Clandestine Bomb Factories (January–March 1929) {#movement-8}
*Timeframe: January – March 1929*

> **Movement Summary**: Setting up bomb labs in Agra and Lahore, Jatin Das's chemical expertise, picric acid experiments in Jhansi, and the political decision for the Assembly strike.

### The Agra Laboratory at Hing Ki Mandi and Jatin Das (January – March 1929)

In Calcutta, Bhagat Singh established contact with the Bengal revolutionary network, specifically Jatindra Nath Das (Jatin Das), an extraordinarily skilled chemist and veteran of the Anushilan Samiti. Bhagat convinced Jatin Das to travel north and train the HSRA cadres in the complex, perilous art of modern explosive manufacturing.

The HSRA established its headquarters and central bomb laboratory in a rented two-story house in Mohalla Hing Ki Mandi, Agra. Under the alias of college students preparing for examinations, Sukhdev, Shiv Verma, Bejoy Kumar Sinha, Kishori Lal, and Jatin Das worked long hours purifying chemicals. Jatin Das was a meticulous instructor: he trained them in handling volatile fulminate of mercury, nitroglycerin, and picric acid, insisting on strict laboratory cleanliness to prevent premature detonation.

Simultaneously, a secondary laboratory was opened in Lahore at Kashmir House, rented by Sukhdev and Jai Gopal, while bomb shells were cast secretly at local foundries. The bombs were tested deep in the ravines and jungles of Babina near Jhansi by Chandrashekhar Azad and Bhagat Singh. They discovered that while high-explosive bombs could shatter steel, they could also calibrate their formulas to produce tremendous sound, smoke, and flash with minimal lethal shrapnel—the precise psychological weapon they needed.

> **Jatindra Nath Das** (Agra Laboratory Instruction (recounted by Shiv Verma)):
> "Chemistry does not forgive a careless revolutionary. A single drop of acid in the wrong tube and our entire mission ends here in this room."

### The Assembly Bomb Debate: The Ethics of Self-Sacrifice (Late March 1929)

In March 1929, the British government introduced two draconian pieces of legislation in the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi: the **Public Safety Bill** (designed to arrest and deport foreign socialist sympathizers and crush the communist movement) and the **Trade Disputes Bill** (which effectively outlawed strikes and penalized trade union organizing). Despite being defeated in the elected Assembly, Viceroy Lord Irwin prepared to enact them using his imperial decree powers.

The HSRA Central Committee met in Agra to decide their response. They resolved to strike at the heart of imperial power: to throw bombs inside the Central Assembly itself. But how should it be executed? The original plan was to throw the bombs and make a rapid escape. Bhagat Singh argued against an escape: 'If we run away, the government will paint us as coward terrorists and hide our message. The throwers must remain in the chamber, court arrest, and turn the colonial courtroom into an ideological pulpit heard by millions!'

The committee initially forbade Bhagat Singh from volunteering, knowing his arrest would lead to the gallows for the Saunders case. Batukeshwar Dutt and Bejoy Kumar Sinha were selected. But Sukhdev intervened with brutal, agonizing clarity: he insisted that only Bhagat Singh had the intellectual depth, charismatic presence, and oratorical mastery to command the courtroom stage. Sukhdev accused the committee of sentimental weakness. Stung by his comrade's challenge, Bhagat Singh insisted on going himself. Dutt was chosen as his companion.

> **Sukhdev Thapar** (Agra Central Committee Confrontation, March 1929):
> "Are we saving our best intellect to hide in the shadows, or do we want our message to shake the conscience of the world? Bhagat must go, because only he can articulate why we die."

## Movement IX: 'To Make the Deaf Hear' — The Assembly Bombing (April 8, 1929) {#movement-9}
*Timeframe: April 8, 1929*

> **Movement Summary**: The detonation of two smoke bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly, the pink leaflets, the birth of 'Inquilab Zindabad!', and the deliberate court of arrest.

### The Assembly Chamber: April 8, 1929, 12:30 PM (April 8, 1929, 12:30 PM)

On the morning of April 8, 1929, the Central Legislative Assembly chamber in New Delhi was packed to capacity. In the gallery sat prominent leaders, journalists, and British dignitaries, including Sir John Simon himself. Below, on the treasury benches, sat the colonial executive. At 12:30 PM, as the Assembly President Vithalbhai Patel rose to give his ruling on the Trade Disputes Bill, and as Finance Member Sir George Schuster stood to proclaim the government's overriding decree, history pivoted.

From the public gallery directly above the official benches, Bhagat Singh leaned forward and dropped a heavy cast-iron bomb into the empty space behind Schuster's seat. A deafening roar shattered the hall, followed by a thick cloud of acrid smoke. Seconds later, Batukeshwar Dutt threw a second bomb toward the opposite corner of the floor. The hall erupted in panic: members scrambled under desks, British officials shrieked and leaped for exits, and guards deserted their posts.

Bhagat Singh drew a .32 Colt automatic pistol and fired two shots into the ceiling to establish complete mastery over the chamber and ensure no guard approached recklessly. Yet, true to their meticulous chemical design, nobody was killed. Sir George Schuster and a few others suffered only minor lacerations from flying splinters. The bombs had been engineered not to kill, but to command universal attention.

> **Sir George Schuster** (Official Parliamentary Statement, April 1929):
> "There was a terrific flash and an overwhelming detonation right behind me... The air was instantly filled with black, suffocating smoke."

### The Leaflet and the Slogan: 'Inquilab Zindabad!' (April 8, 1929)

As the smoke swirled toward the high dome, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt reached into their pockets and scattered hundreds of bright yellow and pink leaflets across the floor. The leaflet opened with the famous quotation from French anarchist Auguste Vaillant:

'IT TAKES A LOUD NOISE TO MAKE THE DEAF HEAR.'

The text, signed by 'Balraj, Commander-in-Chief of the HSRA', laid out their moral justification: 'With these bombs, we have not shed human blood; we have merely warned those who trade in human misery... While the government has turned the Assembly into a hollow farce and gagged the people through the Public Safety and Trade Disputes Bills, our bombs serve as a reminder that the volcano of India's youth is ready to burst!'

Then, standing shoulder to shoulder in the smoke-filled gallery, Bhagat Singh and Dutt raised their right arms and shouted three slogans that would reverberate through Indian history for generations:

'INQUILAB ZINDABAD!' (Long Live Revolution!)
'DOWN WITH IMPERIALISM!'
'WORKERS OF THE WORLD, UNITE!'

Until that afternoon, the standard nationalist cry was 'Bande Mataram'. From April 8, 1929 onward, 'Inquilab Zindabad' became the immortal battle-cry of the entire freedom movement. Having accomplished their mission, both men stood motionless. When British Police Inspector Terry nervously approached with drawn revolver, Bhagat Singh and Dutt smiled, placed their weapons on the railing, and calmly offered their wrists for handcuffs.

> **HSRA Assembly Leaflet** (Scattered in Central Legislative Assembly, April 8, 1929):
> "It takes a loud noise to make the deaf hear... We have given this loud reminder to the callous rulers. Let them understand that human lives cannot be bartered away indefinitely. Long Live Revolution!"

## Movement X: The Historic 63-Day Hunger Strike and Jatin Das's Martyrdom (June–September 1929) {#movement-10}
*Timeframe: June 15 – September 24, 1929*

> **Movement Summary**: The battle for political prisoner status, brutal forced feeding tortures, M.A. Jinnah's assembly defense, and Jatin Das's agonizing 63-day martyrdom.

### The Battle for Political Status: Mianwali and Lahore Jails (June 15 – July 1929)

Following the Assembly action, Bhagat Singh was sent to Mianwali Jail, while Dutt was moved to Lahore Central Jail. Bhagat immediately observed the appalling degradation of Indian prisoners: while European convicts, even ordinary murderers and embezzlers, received clean clothes, beds, tea, and newspapers, Indian prisoners were treated like cattle—fed putrid bread crawling with worms, denied books, subjected to unhygienic bucket latrines, and subjected to hard labor.

On June 15, 1929, Bhagat Singh launched an indefinite hunger strike in Mianwali. Dutt joined him in Lahore. Their demands were strictly political, not personal:
1. Recognition as **Political Prisoners**, not common criminals.
2. Access to newspapers, books, and writing materials.
3. Edible food and equal sanitary treatment with European convicts.
4. Exemption from degrading menial labor.

When Bhagat Singh was transferred to Lahore Central Jail on July 10 for the start of the Lahore Conspiracy Case (police having raided the Kashmir House and Agra bomb factories following approver betrayals), his comrades—Sukhdev, Rajguru, Jatin Das, Ajay Ghosh, Kishori Lal, and Shiv Verma—immediately joined the strike. A collective fast unto death began that would shake the British Empire to its foundations.

> **Bhagat Singh** (Letter to the Inspector General of Prisons, Punjab, June 17, 1929):
> "We are political prisoners and must be treated as such. We are not fighting for luxuries; we are fighting for the fundamental dignity of prisoners who dared to challenge your empire."

### The Horrors of Forced Feeding (July – August 1929)

The colonial prison administration responded with brutal forced feeding. A squad of eight to ten muscular wardens would pin each fasting prisoner to the ground, sit on their limbs, and force a metal gag into their mouth. A thick rubber tube was jammed through the nostril down into the esophagus, through which a mixture of milk, sugar, and eggs was poured.

The prisoners resisted with superhuman ferocity. They coughed, struggled, and bit the rubber tubes in two. Bhagat Singh, possessing powerful physique, fought off multiple wardens daily. The prison authorities grew vindictive: they placed pitchers of iced water inside the cells, hoping thirst would break them; the prisoners used the water only to wash their latrine buckets. They locked up all fresh water, forcing the strikers to swallow nothing for days.

For Jatin Das, forced feeding became an execution. In late July, the medical team accidentally forced the rubber tube into his trachea instead of his esophagus. Milk poured directly into his lungs, causing severe aspiration pneumonia. Jatin Das developed high fever, coughed blood, and suffered excruciating agony. Despite his deteriorating condition, Jatin Das flatly refused to take medicine or break his fast, whispering: 'I will not betray Bhagat and my comrades.'

> **Dr. Gopi Chand Bhargava** (Report to the Punjab Legislative Council, August 1929):
> "The condition of the prisoners is harrowing beyond description. Jatin Das is hovering between life and death. The tube entered his lungs during the violent struggle of forced feeding."

### Jinnah's Thunder in the Assembly and Jatin Das's Martyrdom (September 12–14, 1929)

As news of the hunger strike spread, mass demonstrations broke out across India. In the Central Legislative Assembly, the government introduced the Code of Criminal Procedure Amendment Bill (the 'Hunger Strike Bill'), which sought to amend the law to allow trials to proceed even if the accused were absent due to voluntary starvation.

On September 12 and 14, 1929, Mohammad Ali Jinnah rose in the Assembly and delivered one of the most blistering, historic speeches ever spoken in the chamber. Facing the British treasury benches, Jinnah roared:

'The man who goes on hunger strike has a soul. He is that which you have not got: he has a conviction... It is not an ordinary crime. It is a protest against your callous, iniquitous treatment... If you cannot treat these men as human beings, then do not try them!'

The following day, on September 13, 1929, at 1:05 PM, on the sixty-third day of his hunger strike, twenty-five-year-old Jatindra Nath Das breathed his last. A tidal wave of grief engulfed the subcontinent. Over five hundred thousand people accompanied his funeral cortege as his body was carried by train from Lahore through Delhi and Kanpur to Calcutta. At every station, vast crowds wept, scattering flowers. Subhas Chandra Bose received the body in Calcutta, leading a procession of two hundred thousand to the cremation ghat. On September 24, following solemn assurances from the Punjab Government that a Jail Reform Committee would be formed to grant political privileges, Bhagat Singh and his surviving comrades finally broke their hunger strike.

> **Mohammad Ali Jinnah** (Speech in the Central Legislative Assembly, September 12, 1929):
> "He who goes on hunger strike has a soul. He is moved by that which you have not got: he has a conviction. You may call it an offense; but it is a protest against your callousness and your injustice."

## Movement XI: The Courtroom as an Ideological Battlefield (1929–1930) {#movement-11}
*Timeframe: June 1929 – May 1930*

> **Movement Summary**: The Delhi Sessions statement, defining Revolution, Asaf Ali's legal strategy, and the magisterial farce in Lahore under approver betrayals.

### The Historic Sessions Statement: Defining 'Revolution' (June 6, 1929)

During the trial of the Assembly Bomb Case before the Sessions Judge Leonard Poole in Delhi, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt, represented by counsel Asaf Ali, adopted an unprecedented strategy. They did not contest the physical facts of dropping the bombs; instead, on June 6, 1929, they submitted a joint statement that became one of the greatest revolutionary manifestos in modern history.

Read aloud in open court by Asaf Ali, the statement demolished the prosecution's claim that they were bloodthirsty maniacs:

'By 'Revolution', we mean that the present order of things, which is based on manifest injustice, must change. Producers or laborers, in spite of being the most indispensable element of society, are robbed by their exploiters of the fruits of their labor. The peasant who grows corn for all, starves with his family; the weaver who supplies the world market with clothes, has not enough to cover his own body and children; masons and smiths who build palaces, live in miserable hovels. On the other hand, the capitalist exploiters, parasites of society, squander millions upon their whimsical luxuries.'

'Revolution does not necessarily involve sanguinary strife nor is there any place in it for individual vendetta. It is not the cult of the bomb and the pistol... Revolution is an inalienable right of mankind. Freedom is an imperishable birthright of all. Labor is the real sustainer of society. To the altar of this revolution, we have brought our youth as an incense.' Judge Poole sentenced both men to transportation for life, but the political victory was total: the statement was printed in newspapers across the globe.

> **Bhagat Singh & Batukeshwar Dutt** (Joint Statement to Delhi Sessions Court, June 6, 1929):
> "Revolution is an inalienable right of mankind. Freedom is an imperishable birthright of all. Labor is the real sustainer of society... Long Live Revolution!"

### The Magisterial Farce and Approver Betrayals in Lahore (July 1929 – May 1930)

On July 10, 1929, the Lahore Conspiracy Case trial began before Special Magistrate Sri Kishen inside Lahore Central Jail. Twenty-four revolutionaries were charged with waging war against the King-Emperor and the murder of Saunders. The courtroom was packed with armed police.

Every day, the prisoners turned the proceedings into revolutionary street theater. They entered the dock singing patriotic songs: 'Sarfaroshi ki tamanna ab hamare dil mein hai' and 'Kabhi woh din bhi aayega'. When the magistrate ordered them to be quiet, they shouted 'Inquilab Zindabad!' in unison. On multiple occasions, British police officers ordered constables to beat the handcuffed prisoners with lathis directly inside the courtroom.

Meanwhile, the prosecution relied on traitors from within their own ranks. Under torture, false promises of pardon, and financial rewards, three key cadres turned approvers: Jai Gopal (who had signaled Saunders), Phanindra Nath Ghosh (Bengal/Bihar organizer), and Hans Raj Vohra (a former student cadre). When Jai Gopal took the witness stand to testify against his comrades, an enraged Prem Dutt threw a slipper at him. The police retaliated with horrific brutality, fracturing wrists and beating the prisoners until they lost consciousness. The magisterial inquiry collapsed into open disorder.

> **Magistrate Sri Kishen** (Lahore Court Order Sheet, October 1929):
> "The accused refuse to stand in dock, persist in singing revolutionary songs, and behave in a manner calculated to insult the dignity of the court."

## Movement XII: The Despotic Special Tribunal and Justice Agha Haidar's Dissent (1930) {#movement-12}
*Timeframe: May 1 – October 7, 1930*

> **Movement Summary**: Ordinance III of 1930, the dissolution of judicial norms, Justice Syed Agha Haidar's heroic defiance, and the ex-parte death sentence.

### Ordinance III of 1930: Despotism by Imperial Decree (May 1, 1930)

Frustrated that the normal legal process had been paralyzed for ten months by the prisoners' defiance and hunger strikes, Viceroy Lord Irwin took an unprecedented, despotic step. On May 1, 1930, invoking the emergency powers of Section 72 of the Government of India Act 1915, Irwin promulgated **Ordinance No. III of 1930**.

This draconian decree bypassed all ordinary courts and constituted a **Special Tribunal** composed of three High Court judges: Justice J. Coldstream (President), Justice G.C. Hilton, and Justice Syed Agha Haidar. The Ordinance stripped the accused of every fundamental right guaranteed by civilized jurisprudence:
1. There was no right of appeal to the High Court.
2. The trial could proceed **ex-parte** (in the absence of the accused and their counsel) if the tribunal decided they were obstructing proceedings.
3. Rules of evidence were eviscerated, allowing magistrate summaries to be admitted without oral cross-examination.

The Lahore High Court Bar Association passed a blistering resolution declaring Ordinance III illegal, unconstitutional, and subversive of the rule of law. It was an emergency martial tribunal masquerading as a court of justice.

> **Lahore High Court Bar Association Report** (Resolution adopted June 19, 1930 (Appendix VII, Noorani)):
> "The Ordinance creates a tribunal unknown to ordinary law, takes away the statutory right of appeal, and permits the trial of men for their lives in their absence. It is subversive of the administration of justice."

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**
- **A.G. Noorani**: The Trial of Bhagat Singh, Chapter 8, pp. 130–163 & Appendix VI, pp. 292–297 (Detailed legal analysis of Ordinance III of 1930.)
- **Satvinder S. Juss**: BHAGAT SINGH: A Life in Revolution, Chapter 39, pp. 469–488 (Viceroy Irwin's private correspondence with Secretary of State Wedgwood Benn.)

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Noorani establishes that Ordinance III was ultra vires because Section 72 required a state of emergency threatening the peace of the realm; the mere slow pace of a criminal trial did not constitute a valid constitutional emergency.

### The Battle in Court and the Heroic Dissent of Justice Agha Haidar (May 12, 1930)

The Special Tribunal convened on May 5, 1930, in Poonch House, Lahore. Tensions boiled over on May 12. As the accused entered the court, they were ordered to stop singing. When they persisted, Justice Coldstream lost his temper and ordered British police officers to handcuff the prisoners in the dock. The prisoners resisted. A squad of white police sergeants fell upon Bhagat Singh and his comrades, punching, kicking, and throttling them until blood covered the floor.

During this horrific scene, the lone Indian judge on the bench, **Justice Syed Agha Haidar**, a distinguished jurist from UP, rose from his seat in profound indignation. He ordered the police to desist. When Justice Coldstream dictated an order approving the police violence and removing the handcuffed prisoners from court, Justice Agha Haidar flatly refused to sign it. Instead, he made an unprecedented official entry into the judicial record:

'I was not a party to the order directing the removal of the accused... I take no responsibility for the violent action of the police. It is an insult to the court.'

Agha Haidar's defiance threw the colonial administration into panic. A judge on the king's bench had publicly branded the tribunal a sham. Coldstream suspended sittings. The British government moved swiftly and ruthlessly: under pressure from the Punjab Governor and the Chief Justice, the Tribunal was dissolved. When it was reconstituted on June 21, Justice Coldstream was replaced by Justice Hilton, and Justice Agha Haidar was summarily removed from the bench and replaced by Justice Abdul Qadir. Having purged the dissenting voice, the hand-picked tribunal proceeded with an entirely one-sided, ex-parte trial in an empty courtroom.

> **Justice Syed Agha Haidar** (Judicial Dissent recorded on Tribunal Minutes, May 12, 1930):
> "I was not a party to the order directing the removal of the accused from the court, nor was I consulted. I take no responsibility for the violent treatment meted out to them."

## Movement XIII: In the Shadow of Death: The Philosophical Masterpieces (1930–1931) {#movement-13}
*Timeframe: October 1930 – March 1931*

> **Movement Summary**: Cell No. 14 in Lahore Central Jail; reading 300 books, compiling the Jail Notebook, the debate with Bhai Randhir Singh, and authoring 'Why I Am An Atheist'.

### The Jail Notebook: 404 Pages of World Thought (September 1929 – March 1931)

Confined in the condemned cells of Lahore Central Jail, under twenty-four-hour surveillance by armed wardens, Bhagat Singh turned his cell into an extraordinary university of the mind. Through his childhood friend Jaidev Gupta and the Dwarka Das Library, he obtained over three hundred volumes of political philosophy, economics, sociology, and world literature.

Between September 1929 and March 1931, Bhagat Singh filled a 404-page bound notebook with dense, meticulous handwritten extracts, critical reflections, and ideological diagrams. The notebook, preserved through the courage of his comrade Kumari Lajjawati and later published by Chaman Lal, reveals an intellect of astonishing breadth. He transcribed and analyzed extracts from Karl Marx, Friedrich Engels, Vladimir Lenin, Leon Trotsky, Nikolai Bukharin, Thomas Paine, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, John Locke, Thomas Hobbes, Upton Sinclair, Bertrand Russell, Lord Byron, and Percy Bysshe Shelley.

He was fascinated by the mechanics of capital accumulation, state power, the history of marriage and private property, and the penal systems of Europe. Even with death hovering days away, Bhagat Singh studied not to distract himself from mortality, but to sharpen the theoretical weapons of the Indian revolution that would follow his hanging.

> **Bhagat Singh** (Entry in the Jail Notebook, 1930):
> "Any man who stands for progress has to criticize, disbelieve and challenge every item of the old faith. Item by item he has to reason out every nook and corner of the prevailing faith."

### The Encounter with Bhai Randhir Singh and 'Why I Am An Atheist' (October 5–6, 1930)

In late 1930, Bhai Randhir Singh, a venerable Sikh religious leader and Ghadar veteran imprisoned in Lahore Central Jail, learned that Bhagat Singh had cut his hair and denied the existence of God. Deeply distressed, Bhai Randhir obtained permission to visit Bhagat Singh's cell in an attempt to bring him back into the religious fold.

Their conversation was long, intense, and respectful. Bhai Randhir urged Bhagat to pray, arguing that facing the gallows without divine grace would lead to spiritual damnation. When Bhagat calmly refused, presenting rationalist and materialist arguments, Bhai Randhir grew exasperated and uttered a cutting accusation: 'You are an atheist because of your vanity! You have become famous, your photographs are printed in newspapers, and pride has puffed up your ego like a blister!'

Stung by the accusation that his atheism was born of childish vanity rather than profound philosophical conviction, Bhagat Singh picked up his pen on October 5 and 6, 1930, and authored his crowning intellectual treatise: **Why I Am An Atheist** (Main Nastik Kyun Hoon). Published in Lala Lajpat Rai's English weekly *The People* on September 27, 1931, it remains a masterpiece of rationalist literature.

In it, Bhagat traced his intellectual evolution step by step: how he began as a religious Arya Samajist boy, how the study of science and world revolutions destroyed his faith in a creator God, and why the existence of systemic human suffering—poverty, starvation, infant disease, and tyranny—disproves an all-merciful, all-powerful deity: 'If an omnipotent God created this world, why did He create a world full of misery, injustice, and slaughter? Is He cruel, or is He powerless? In either case, He is no God!' Facing death with unblinking rational courage, Bhagat declared: 'No ambition to see any reward beyond the grave lures me. A short life spent fighting for humanity without fear or superstition is its own supreme reward.'

> **Bhagat Singh** (Why I Am An Atheist (Written October 1930; published The People, Sept 1931)):
> "No ambition to see any reward beyond the grave lures me. Quite dispassionately have I examined the question, and I have found that no belief is necessary for a man to face the scaffold with dignity. I shall walk to the gallows with a smile, because I know that my death will awaken my motherland."

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Noorani observes that Bhagat Singh's refusal to recant his atheism on the eve of execution is the ultimate test of intellectual integrity; unlike many historical figures who turn to religion when staring into the abyss of death, Bhagat Singh met his end with pure, unyielding reason.

### The Letter to his Father and the Rejection of Clemency (October 3–4, 1930)

While the Tribunal prepared its verdict, Sardar Kishen Singh, overwhelmed by paternal grief and desperate to save his twenty-three-year-old son from the gallows, submitted an unauthorized petition to the Tribunal without consulting Bhagat Singh. Kishen Singh argued that Bhagat Singh was innocent of the Saunders murder and that on December 17, 1928, he was not even present in Lahore, offering alibi witnesses.

When Bhagat Singh learned of this petition in his cell, he was devastated. He felt his father had dealt a mortal blow to the political honor of the revolutionary movement. On October 4, 1930, he wrote a blistering, heartbreaking open letter to his father, demanding that it be published in the press:

'My dear Father, I was shocked to learn that you have submitted a petition to the Tribunal... My life is not so precious, at least to me, that it should be saved at the cost of principles! Your action has wounded my soul. When all of us comrades had unanimously boycotted the sham Tribunal and refused to plead an alibi, you went behind my back to plead for my life as if I were a common criminal afraid to die! You have shown weakness... I want you to know that I shall never compromise with dishonor.'

> **Bhagat Singh** (Open Letter to his Father, Sardar Kishen Singh, October 4, 1930):
> "My life is not so precious, at least to me, that it should be saved at the cost of principles. I have always held that freedom demands absolute sacrifice. I hope you will rise above paternal weakness and understand the duty I owe to my nation."

## Movement XIV: The Verdict, London Privy Council, and the Gandhi Question (Oct 1930 – March 1931) {#movement-14}
*Timeframe: October 7, 1930 – March 21, 1931*

> **Movement Summary**: The death judgment, London appeal rejected by Lord Dunedin, forensic audit of the Gandhi-Irwin negotiations, and the betrayal of commutation.

### The Judgment of October 7, 1930 and the London Privy Council (October 7, 1930 – February 11, 1931)

On October 7, 1930, in an empty courtroom devoid of the accused, their counsel, or the public, Justice Hilton pronounced the 300-page judgment of the Special Tribunal. Bhagat Singh, Shivaram Rajguru, and Sukhdev Thapar were sentenced to be **hanged by the neck until they be dead**. Kishori Lal, Mahabir Singh, Bejoy Kumar Sinha, Shiv Verma, Gaya Prasad, and Kamal Nath Tewari were sentenced to transportation for life.

Across India, a Defense Committee led by Pt. Motilal Nehru, Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew, and Asaf Ali scrambled to challenge the verdict. Eminent British barrister D.N. Pritt, K.C., was retained to file a petition for Special Leave to Appeal before the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London. Pritt argued with unassailable constitutional logic that Ordinance III was *ultra vires*—that Section 72 of the Government of India Act did not empower the Governor-General to abrogate ordinary legal procedures and deny the subject the statutory right of appeal.

On February 11, 1931, the Privy Council, headed by Lord Dunedin, summarily dismissed the petition without even calling upon the Crown counsel to reply. In private imperial cables, the British cabinet had made it clear that judicial technicalities could not be allowed to protect the assassins of a British police officer. The legal battle had ended. Only political intervention could now save the three young men.

> **D.N. Pritt, K.C.** (Argument before the Privy Council, London, February 1931):
> "If the executive can at its whim declare an emergency to bypass ordinary criminal courts and try citizens in absentia, then constitutional liberty in the British Empire is an illusion."

### Forensic Audit: Gandhi, Lord Irwin, and the Commutation Failure (February 17 – March 21, 1931)

Between February 17 and March 5, 1931, Mahatma Gandhi and Viceroy Lord Irwin held historic negotiations that culminated in the Gandhi-Irwin Pact. With the entire nation pleading for the lives of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru, public pressure was overwhelming for Gandhi to make the commutation of their death sentences an absolute condition for signing the Pact.

Did Gandhi do everything in his power to save them? A rigorous, forensic comparison of Lord Irwin's confidential daily minutes, Gandhi's public statements, and the memoirs of Home Secretary H.W. Emerson reveals a tragic, uncomfortable truth. Gandhi did raise the question with Irwin on multiple occasions, asking for a suspension or postponement of the execution. However, Gandhi explicitly told Irwin that he was *not* making commutation a breaking condition of the Pact: 'If the boys are to be hanged, it would be better to hang them before the Karachi Congress than after.'

Irwin noted in his secret dispatch to Secretary of State Wedgwood Benn:
'Mr. Gandhi said that he would not plead for their lives, for he could not defend violence. He only asked whether their execution might be postponed until after the Congress session. I told him that postponement was impossible and would only raise false hopes.' Gandhi's philosophical commitment to absolute non-violence prevented him from exerting the political leverage that could have forced the British to commute the sentences to life imprisonment.

> **Lord Irwin (Viceroy of India)** (Secret Minutes of Conversations with Mr. Gandhi, February–March 1931):
> "In the course of conversation, Mr. Gandhi referred to the case of Bhagat Singh. He did not plead for commutation, although he would personally be glad if it could be done. He felt that postponement would be helpful, but left the matter entirely to my judgment."

*⚖ Historiographical Audit & Archival Cross-Check*: Noorani's exhaustive audit remains the definitive historical indictment: while Gandhi did not conspire with the British to hang Bhagat Singh, he failed to exercise the ultimate political veto he possessed during the pact negotiations, prioritizing constitutional compromise over the lives of the revolutionary vanguard.

## Movement XV: March 23, 1931: The Clandestine Hanging and Immortality {#movement-15}
*Timeframe: March 20 – March 23, 1931 & Aftermath*

> **Movement Summary**: The final petition ('No Hanging, Please Shoot Us'), the last letter to comrades, reading Lenin until the final second, the unprecedented evening hanging, the desecration at the Sutlej, and immortal legacy.

### The Final Petition: 'No Hanging, Please Shoot Us' (March 20, 1931)

Three days before their scheduled execution, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru drafted their final formal communication to the Punjab Governor. It was not a plea for mercy; it was a defiant ideological declaration that placed their struggle within international law.

They declared that a state of war existed between the British Crown and the working masses of India. Therefore, as captured soldiers of the revolutionary army, they demanded to be treated according to the laws of war:

'We declare that the state of war does exist and shall exist so long as the British imperialists and Indian exploiters continue to suck the blood of our working people... We were arrested in this war. You have condemned us to death. Since we are prisoners of war, we demand that you execute us not by hanging, which is reserved for common criminals, but that we be placed before a firing squad of your military soldiers and shot like combatants!' The British administration silently filed the petition away, terrified of according the young men even the symbolic status of military combatants.

> **Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Rajguru** (Petition to the Punjab Governor, March 20, 1931):
> "Let us declare that the state of war does exist... As prisoners of war, we request and demand that we be placed before a firing squad and shot dead like soldiers, rather than hanged like felons."

### The Final Hours: 'Wait, One Revolutionary is Meeting Another!' (March 22 – 23, 1931)

On March 22, Bhagat Singh wrote his final letter to his fellow prisoners of the Second Lahore Conspiracy Case: 'The desire to live is natural in man. I also have it. But I can live only on one condition—that I should not be imprisoned in a cage. My name has become the symbol of the Indian revolution... If I die with courage, facing the scaffold with a smile, Indian mothers will wish their children to become Bhagat Singh.'

On the afternoon of March 23, around 4:00 PM, his legal counsel Pran Nath Mehta was granted a final twenty-minute interview. When Mehta entered cell No. 14, he found Bhagat Singh pacing with calm energy, reading a newly delivered book: Clara Zetkin's *Reminiscences of Lenin*. When Mehta asked if he had any message for the country, Bhagat Singh replied with two crisp sentences: 'Down with Imperialism!' and 'Long Live Revolution!'

When jail wardens approached his cell two hours earlier than customary, announcing that the time had come, Bhagat Singh did not drop the book. He turned a page, looked up at the jailer, and smiled:

'Wait! Do you not see that one revolutionary is meeting another revolutionary?'

He finished the chapter, closed the book, tossed it toward the ceiling, and stood up: 'Let us go.'

> **Pran Nath Mehta (Advocate)** (Eyewitness Account of Final Prison Visit, March 23, 1931):
> "He was completely at peace. He looked happy, as if he were going to attend a wedding festival rather than the gallows. He told me: 'Tell the youth of India that their duty is just beginning.'"

**✦ Integrated Sources & Provenance:**

### The Clandestine Evening Execution and the Fire on the Sutlej (March 23, 1931, 7:00 PM – Dawn, March 24)

In a blatant, desperate violation of the Punjab Jail Manual—which mandated that executions take place at 6:00 AM at dawn—the British authorities brought forward the execution by eleven hours to **7:00 PM on the evening of March 23, 1931**. They feared that if the hangings occurred the next morning, hundreds of thousands of people would storm the jail.

Arm in arm, Bhagat Singh (age 23), Sukhdev Thapar (age 23), and Shivaram Rajguru (age 22) walked from their cells toward the scaffold. Their voices rose together in the quiet dusk, singing their favorite verse:

'Dil se niklegi na mar kar bhi vatan ki ulfat, / Meri mitti se bhi khushbu-e-vatan aayegi.'
(Even after death, love for the motherland will not leave my heart; from my very dust, the fragrance of my country will arise.)

As they mounted the wooden platform, they kissed the hangman's noose. At exactly 7:33 PM, the trapdoors fell. Death was instantaneous.

What followed was an act of imperial cowardice that horrified civilized opinion worldwide. Refusing to hand the bodies over to their weeping families waiting outside the main gate, the prison authorities smuggled the three corpses out through a breach knocked into the rear wall of the jail. Loaded onto military trucks, the bodies were driven under blackout to the desolate banks of the Sutlej river at Ganda Singh Wala near Ferozepur. There, under the dark sky, British officers and Sikh and Hindu jail staff poured kerosene over the bodies and lit a crude bonfire. Before the bodies were fully cremated, seeing torches of approaching villagers who had grown suspicious of the smoke, the British soldiers kicked the half-burned remains into the freezing waters of the Sutlej and fled in their trucks.

The next morning, the riverbank was discovered. Grief and fury swept the subcontinent. When Mahatma Gandhi arrived at the Karachi railway station for the Congress session on March 25, thousands of angry young men greeted him with black flowers and shouts of 'Where is Bhagat Singh?'. Though his physical body was consumed by flame and river, Bhagat Singh had achieved what imperial executioners could never extinguish: he had become immortal.

> **Sardar Kishen Singh** (Speech at the Banks of the Sutlej, March 24, 1931):
> "They tried to burn our children in secret like thieves in the night! But every drop of their ashes that touches this river will spawn thousands of Bhagat Singhs across this land!"
