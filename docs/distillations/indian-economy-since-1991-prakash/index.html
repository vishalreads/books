Executive Epistemological Overview

B.A. Prakash's *The Indian Economy Since 1991: Economic Reforms and Performance* (Pearson Education, 718 pages, ~2 million characters) stands as the premier academic and empirical reference work on India's post-liberalization economic journey. Unlike standard textbooks that approach economic policy through theoretical constructs or polemical political rhetoric, this masterwork conducts an **exhaustive, data-driven empirical autopsy across three decades of economic reforms**.

The text synthesizes specialized contributions from leading Indian economists, policy researchers, and central bank analysts across nine core structural dimensions: 1. **Macroeconomic Performance**: Documenting the historical break from the sluggish 3.5% "Hindu rate of growth" (1950–1980) to post-1991 expansion averaging 6.5% to 7.5%, analyzing changes in national income composition, gross capital formation, and domestic savings. 2. **Fiscal Architecture & Tax Restructuring**: Tracing the reform of direct and indirect taxation following the Chelliah and Kelkar committees, evaluating central and state fiscal deficits, and tracking public debt trajectories under the FRBM framework. 3. **Fiscal Federalism**: Evaluating the distribution of financial resources between the Union and the States under consecutive Finance Commissions, statutory grants-in-aid under Article 275, and state-level debt sustainability. 4. **Monetary & Financial System Overhaul**: The deregulation of commercial banking following the Narasimham Committee reports (1991 and 1998), the phased reduction of CRR and SLR statutory pre-emptions, and the modernization of capital markets under SEBI. 5. **External Sector Transformation**: The transition from inward-looking import substitution to export orientation, structural trends in merchandise trade and invisibles, the surge in foreign exchange reserves, and the debate over Capital Account Convertibility. 6. **Agrarian Reality & The Post-Reform Crisis**: A forensic assessment of the deceleration of agricultural growth post-1991, the Unit 1: Post-1991 Macroeconomic Trajectory & Structural Growth Shift

1.1 The Decisive Structural Break Prior to 1991, the Indian economy was characterized by what economist Raj Krishna termed the **"Hindu Rate of Growth"**—an average annual real GDP expansion of approximately **3.5% between 1950 and 1980** (translating to a meager 1.3% per capita growth after accounting for population growth of 2.2%). While growth accelerated to ~5.5% during the 1980s, this expansion was macroeconomically fragile, driven by debt-fueled government consumption expenditure and expensive short-term foreign commercial borrowing, culminating in the 1991 sovereign external liquidity crisis.

Post-1991, the Indian economy established a permanently higher growth trajectory: - **Eighth Five-Year Plan (1992–1997)**: Real GDP grew at **6.7% per annum**, far surpassing the targeted 5.6%. - **High-Growth Golden Era (2003–2008)**: India achieved unprecedented economic expansion, averaging **8.7% annually** over five consecutive years, propelled by surging global trade, rapid domestic credit expansion, and peak gross capital formation.

1.2 Sectoral Composition of National Output The structural transformation of India's Gross Value Added (GVA) diverged sharply from the classic industrialization paths of Western Europe, the United States, and East Asia:

\text{Traditional Developmental Trajectory: } \text{Agriculture} \longrightarrow \text{Manufacturing} \longrightarrow \text{Services}
\text{Indian Structural Trajectory: } \text{Agriculture} \longrightarrow \text{Services (Direct Jump, Bypassing Manufacturing)}

While agriculture's output share collapsed from 53% to 16%, its employment share only drifted from ~70% to ~45%. Meanwhile, the services sector expanded rapidly to generate over 56% of national output, but because services are technology- and skill-intensive, they absorbed less than 30% of the total national workforce.

1.3 Savings and Capital Accumulation Prakash documents that India's growth acceleration was fundamentally underpinned by domestic savings and capital investment: - **Gross Domestic Savings Rate**: Rose steadily from **22.8% of GDP in 1991–92** to an all-time peak of **36.8% of GDP in 2007–08**, driven primarily by household financial savings and rising private corporate retained earnings. - **Gross Capital Formation (Investment Rate)**: Climbed from **24.2% of GDP in 1991–92** to a historic high of **38.1% of GDP in 2007–08**, before experiencing a multi-year decline post-2012 due to corporate balance-sheet stress and banking NPAs.

Unit 2: Fiscal Policy, Tax Restructuring & Deficit Trajectories

2.1 The Crisis of Public Finance in 1991 The macroeconomic crisis of 1991 was at its root a crisis of fiscal governance: - Throughout the late 1980s, the Central Government's **Gross Fiscal Deficit averaged 8.2% of GDP**, while the **Revenue Deficit expanded from 1.5% to over 3.3% of GDP**. - The state was borrowing heavily merely to pay current operational revenue expenditures (interest on previous debt, administrative salaries, and non-targeted subsidies), violating the fundamental accounting canon that borrowings should finance productive capital assets.

2.2 The Chelliah & Kelkar Tax Reforms Post-1991 tax restructuring was guided by the **Tax Reforms Committee (chaired by Raja J. Chelliah, 1991–1993)** and subsequent Task Forces chaired by Vijay Kelkar: - **Direct Tax Rationalization**: Prior to reforms, India possessed absurdly high marginal income tax rates (peaking at 97.75% in the 1970s under Indira Gandhi, breeding pervasive tax evasion and a massive black money parallel economy). The Chelliah Committee recommended lowering rates, simplifying slabs, and eliminating distortionary exemptions to improve voluntary tax compliance. - **The Landmark 1997 "Dream Budget"**: Finance Minister P. Chidambaram implemented the Chelliah blueprint, slashing peak personal income tax rates to **10%, 20%, and 30%**, and corporate tax rates to 35%, generating substantial revenue buoyancy. - **Indirect Tax Modernization**: Systematic elimination of cascading excises: - Transitioned from physical excise verification to **MODVAT (Modified Value Added Tax)** in 1986. - Expanded to **CENVAT (Central Value Added Tax)** covering all inputs in 2000. - Phased introduction of state-level VAT (2005), culminating in the nationwide **Goods and Services Tax (GST) in 2017**.

2.3 Fiscal Deficit and Debt Dynamics To institutionalize fiscal discipline, Parliament enacted the **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003**: - Mandated eliminating the Revenue Deficit entirely by 2008–09 and containing the Gross Fiscal Deficit below **3.0% of GDP**. - While the Center successfully compressed its fiscal deficit to **2.5% of GDP in FY 2007–08**, the global financial crisis of 2008 prompted massive fiscal stimulus packages (farm loan waivers, sixth pay commission, tax cuts), causing fiscal deficits to explode to **6.0% of GDP in 2008–09** and delaying fiscal consolidation targets for over a decade.

Unit 3: Fiscal Federalism & Center-State Financial Allocations

3.1 Constitutional Framework of Fiscal Federalism Under the Constitution of India, fiscal powers exhibit a structural **Vertical Fiscal Imbalance (VFI)**: - The Central Government possesses the most elastic, high-yielding revenue sources (Income Tax, Corporation Tax, Customs, GST). - State Governments bear the overwhelming responsibility for heavy developmental, social, and economic expenditures (Public Health, Sanitation, Agriculture, Police, Education, Roads, Irrigation). - To bridge this structural revenue-expenditure mismatch, the Constitution mandates fiscal transfers via: 1. **Article 280 (Finance Commission)**: Statutory vertical sharing of divisible central taxes and horizontal formulaic devolution among States. 2. **Article 275**: Statutory grants-in-aid to revenue-deficit states. 3. **Discretionary Transfers**: Historically routed through the Planning Commission as Central Assistance to State Plans and Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS).

Unit 4: Monetary Management, Credit Policy & Financial Liberalization

4.1 The Pre-1991 Financial Repression Prior to 1991, India's banking system operated under severe financial repression: - **Statutory Pre-emptions**: The RBI maintained exorbitant statutory ratios to finance government budget deficits at artificially low interest rates: - **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)** reached **15.0%**. - **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** reached a crushing peak of **38.5%**. - *Result*: Together, **53.5% of total commercial bank deposits were expropriated by the state**, leaving less than 47% for commercial lending to industry and commerce. - **Administered Interest Rates**: The RBI fixed all deposit and lending rates administratively, creating negative real interest rates and misallocating capital.

4.2 The Narasimham Committee Blueprint (1991 & 1998) The Committee on the Financial System (chaired by former RBI Governor M. Narasimham) fundamentally modernized Indian commercial banking: 1. **Phased Reduction of Pre-emptions**: Recommended slashing SLR to 25% and CRR to competitive levels, unlocking hundreds of thousands of crores for commercial credit. 2. **Interest Rate Deregulation**: Abolished administered interest rates, allowing commercial banks to price loans and deposits based on market cost of funds and credit risk. 3. **Prudential Regulation**: Introduced international accounting standards: - Objective 90-day non-performing asset (NPA) classification. - Phased adoption of Basel Capital Adequacy Ratios (CAR/CRAR). 4. **Entry of New Private Banks (1993)**: Opened the banking sector to technology-driven private competitors (HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank), revolutionizing customer service, ATMs, and digital payments.

Unit 5: Capital Market Modernization & Regulatory Architecture

5.1 Dismantling the Controller of Capital Issues (CCI) Prior to 1992, corporate fundraising was regulated by the archaic **Capital Issues (Control) Act, 1947**. The bureaucratic Controller of Capital Issues (CCI) decided which company could issue equity shares, how many shares could be floated, and at what price (often forcing corporations to issue shares at steep artificial discounts). In May 1992, the Government repealed the Act, giving corporate issuers the freedom to price Initial Public Offerings (IPOs) at market rates.

5.2 Statutory Empowerment of SEBI (1992) Established initially as an administrative body in 1988, the **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)** was granted statutory autonomy via the SEBI Act, 1992, following the Harshad Mehta securities scam: - Empowered to register and regulate stock exchanges, merchant bankers, brokers, mutual funds, and credit rating agencies. - Prohibited insider trading, fraudulent market manipulation, and unfair trade practices. - Introduced stringent disclosure and corporate governance norms for publicly listed companies (Clause 49 of the Listing Agreement).

5.3 Modern Market Infrastructure Between 1992 and 2002, India replaced its obsolete physical floor trading systems with world-class capital market infrastructure: - **National Stock Exchange (NSE, 1994)**: Pioneered pan-India electronic screen-based trading, eliminating the physical floor cartels of the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). - **Dematerialization (The Depositories Act, 1996)**: Established the National Securities Depository Limited (NSDL) and Central Depository Services Limited (CDSL), converting paper share certificates into electronic records and eliminating fake certificates, bad deliveries, and transfer delays. - **Rolling Settlements**: Transitioned from multi-week account settlement periods to modern automated rolling settlement cycles (**T+5, then T+3, T+2, and eventually T+1**).

Unit 6: External Sector Reforms, Trade Patterns & Balance of Payments

6.1 The Transition from Autarky to Global Integration Pre-1991 external policy was anchored in **Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI)**, operating on the belief that foreign trade drained domestic wealth. The reform program reversed this doctrine: - **Abolition of Quantitative Restrictions (QRs)**: Quotas and discretionary import licensing were phased out, moving goods onto Open General Licenses (OGL). - **Tariff Reductions**: India's peak customs import duties fell from **over 300% in 1991** to 150% (1992), 50% (1995), 30% (2002), and stabilized around competitive levels (~10% for non-agricultural goods).

6.2 Structural Shifts in Merchandise Trade Prakash provides an extensive empirical audit of post-1991 trade flows: - **Merchandise Exports**: Surged from **$18.1 billion in 1990–91** to over **$300 billion by 2011–12**, shifting composition from traditional primary commodities (tea, jute, raw cotton) to value-added manufactured goods (refined petroleum products, gems and jewelry, pharmaceuticals, engineering goods, and automobiles). - **Merchandise Imports**: Expanded dramatically, dominated by three inelastic structural imports: **Crude Petroleum (POL)**, **Electronic Hardware**, and **Gold**. - **Geographic Diversification**: Trade shifted from historic dependence on the USSR and Western Europe toward developing economies in Asia, ASEAN, China, the Middle East, and Africa.

6.3 The Invisibles Boom & Forex Accumulation The structural merchandise trade deficit was consistently counterbalanced by a spectacular boom in the **Invisibles Account**: - **Software and IT-BPO Exports**: Expanded from negligible levels in 1991 to over $100 billion, providing a resilient cushion against external shocks. - **Diaspora Remittances**: Inward remittances from Indian workers abroad (primarily in the Gulf, North America, and Europe) surged past $70–80 billion annually. - **Foreign Exchange Reserves**: Built from barely **$1.2 billion in June 1991** to over **$300 billion by 2008** and exceeding **$600 billion by 2021**, transforming India from an external debtor vulnerable to balance-of-payments crises into an external creditor economy.

Unit 7: Foreign Direct Investment Dynamics & Capital Account Convertibility

7.1 FDI Policy Evolution Post-1991 Under the pre-1991 **Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA), 1973**, foreign equity ownership in domestic firms was strictly capped at 40%, forcing multinational corporations (e.g., Coca-Cola and IBM) to exit India. Post-1991 reforms radically dismantled these restrictions: - Replaced FERA with the investor-friendly **Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999**, treating foreign exchange violations as civil rather than criminal offenses. - **Two Investment Routes**: - **Automatic Route**: Inflows requiring only post-facto notification to the RBI within 30 days. Progressively expanded to cover over 90% of sectors up to 100% equity. - **Government Route**: Inflows requiring prior review by the Foreign Investment Promotion Board (FIPB, later dismantled and decentralized to individual administrative ministries). - **Cumulative FDI Inflows**: Rose from under **$100 million in 1990–91** to over **$40–50 billion annually** by the 2010s, shifting from industrial manufacturing toward services (telecommunications, financial services, software, computer hardware, and construction).

7.2 The Capital Account Convertibility (CAC) Debate While India achieved **Full Current Account Convertibility in August 1994** (under Article VIII of the IMF Articles of Agreement), it retained calibrated restrictions on the Capital Account. The **S.S. Tarapore Committees (1997 and 2006)** outlined rigorous macroeconomic preconditions before permitting full capital account openness: 1. Gross fiscal deficit reduced below 3.5% of GDP. 2. Mandated domestic inflation rate contained between 3% and 5%. 3. Commercial banking system gross NPAs reduced below 5%. 4. Robust foreign exchange reserves covering at least 6 to 9 months of imports and the entire stock of short-term external debt.

Because full capital account convertibility exposes an economy to volatile cross-border speculative capital flight (as witnessed during the 1997 East Asian Financial Crisis), Indian policymakers deliberately chose a gradual, sequenced liberalization path that successfully shielded the nation during the 1997 Asian crisis and the 2008 global financial meltdown.

Unit 8: Agricultural Sector Performance, Cropping Patterns & Agrarian Crisis

8.1 The Post-1991 Agrarian Deceleration Prakash's contributors conduct an unsparing audit of how economic reforms neglected the rural economy: - While non-agricultural GDP surged post-1991, **Agricultural GDP growth decelerated** from 3.6% per annum (1980–1990) to barely **2.4% per annum (1990–2005)**. - **The Stagnation of Public Investment**: Gross Capital Formation (GCF) in agriculture by the public sector collapsed in real terms, as budgetary funds were absorbed by rising input subsidies (fertilizer, power, water) rather than capital assets (canal networks, drainage, soil conservation, seed research).

8.2 Cropping Pattern Distortions and Farm Indebtedness - **Shift toward High-Risk Cash Crops**: Farmers shifted land acreage from drought-resistant traditional coarse cereals (jowar, bajra, ragi) toward commercial cash crops (Bt cotton, soybean, sugarcane). Because cash crops require expensive purchased seeds, chemical pesticides, and heavy irrigation, a single pest infestation or monsoon failure resulted in crippling debt. - **The Moneylender Resurgence**: Despite the expansion of Priority Sector Lending and Kisan Credit Cards (KCC), small and marginal farmers remained heavily dependent on informal local moneylenders for working capital, leading to severe agrarian distress and farmer suicides across Vidarbha, Andhra Pradesh, and Karnataka.

Unit 9: Industrial Performance, Disinvestment & Infrastructure Deficits

9.1 The Manufacturing Puzzle Despite radical industrial delicensing and the abolition of the MRTP Act, India's manufacturing sector failed to emerge as the primary engine of national growth: - Manufacturing's share of GDP remained stubbornly flat at **~15–16% for thirty years**. - **Small Scale Industry (SSI) De-reservation**: Prior to reforms, hundreds of commodities (garments, shoes, toys, electronic components) were legally reserved exclusively for small-scale factories, preventing Indian firms from achieving the massive economies of scale necessary to compete against Chinese factory complexes. De-reservation was completed by the 2000s, but domestic manufacturers faced severe infrastructure bottlenecks.

9.2 The Disinvestment Trajectory Post-1991 disinvestment of Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) progressed through three distinct phases: 1. **Minority Share Sales (1991–1998)**: Selling small bundles of equity shares (5–10%) to public financial institutions and retail investors to bridge annual fiscal deficit targets. 2. **Strategic Disinvestment (1999–2004)**: Under the NDA-1 government (Disinvestment Ministry headed by Arun Shourie), the state sold controlling management stakes (≥51%) in commercial CPSEs to strategic private buyers (Maruti Udyog, Bharat Aluminium, VSNL, IPCL, Hindustan Zinc). 3. **National Investment Fund (NIF, 2005)**: Mandating that disinvestment proceeds be channeled into social sector capital spending and CPSE modernization rather than funding routine government consumption.

Economic reforms decisively accelerated poverty reduction: the annual rate of poverty decline tripled from ~0.74 percentage points per year between 1993 and 2004 to **2.18 percentage points per year between 2004 and 2011**, lifting over 130 million citizens out of poverty during the high-growth decade.

10.2 The "Jobless Growth" Conundrum and Labor Informalization Despite high headline GDP growth, India's economy struggled with poor employment elasticity: - **Employment Elasticity Collapse**: The responsiveness of employment generation to GDP growth dropped from **0.44 in the 1970s and 1980s** to under **0.15 post-2000**, meaning that an 8% GDP growth rate generated barely 1% employment expansion. - **Informalization**: Over **90% of the total national workforce** remains employed in the unorganized, informal sector without written employment contracts, paid leave, health insurance, or statutory pensions.

10.3 Regional Divergence Economic liberalization exacerbated regional disparities: - Coastal southern and western states (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka) with superior physical infrastructure, higher literacy rates, and port connectivity attracted over **70% of total post-1991 private and foreign capital investment**. - Landlocked northern and eastern states (Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, Odisha) remained trapped in lower growth trajectories, widening inter-state per capita income gaps.

Unit 11: Regional Growth Divergence & Spatial Convergence Analysis

11.1 The Neoclassical Convergence Hypothesis in the Indian Context In standard neoclassical regional growth theory (Solow-Swan, Barro and Sala-i-Martin), regional economies with open borders, common currency, and free factor mobility should experience **$eta$-convergence** (poorer states growing faster than richer states due to diminishing marginal returns to capital) and **$sigma$-convergence** (a secular decline in the dispersion of per capita Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) across regions).

Prakash's contributors demonstrate that post-1991 India witnessed the exact reverse: **unambiguous $sigma$-divergence**: - Prior to 1991, the Freight Equalization Policy (which subsidized long-distance rail transport of coal and steel) artificially suppressed regional resource advantages. - Post-1991 deregulation allowed capital to flow freely to regions offering the highest agglomeration economies, superior transport logistics, stable political governance, and educated skilled labor. - **The Divergence Ratio**: In 1990–91, the per capita income ratio between India's richest major state (Punjab/Maharashtra) and poorest major state (Bihar) was approximately **2.7:1**. By 2011–12, this divergence expanded to **over 4.5:1**, demonstrating that market-driven growth without massive central regional equalization investments amplifies spatial inequality.

Unit 12: Labor Market Dualism, Wage Polarization & The NCEUS Findings

12.1 Organized vs. Unorganized Sector Dualism The post-reform Indian labor market is defined by profound structural segmentation: - **Organized / Formal Sector**: Accounts for barely **10% of total national employment**, offering legally protected contracts, minimum wages, paid annual leave, gratuity, and retirement benefits under EPFO/ESIC. - **Unorganized / Informal Sector**: Engages over **90% of the working population**, characterized by low capital intensity, absence of social protection, precarious oral contracts, and subsistence earnings.

12.2 The NCEUS (Arjun Sengupta Committee) Paradigm The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector (NCEUS) delivered the landmark empirical revelation that: - **77% of India's population (836 million citizens in 2004–05)** lived on an average per capita daily consumption of **less than ₹20 per day**, classified as "poor and vulnerable". - The commission established that informalization was not merely confined to traditional unorganized agriculture and tiny family workshops, but was actively proliferating within the formal corporate sector itself through the aggressive replacement of permanent unionized workers with **precarious contract labor**.

12.3 Employment Elasticity and Structural Transformation Limits The fundamental failure of post-1991 growth, Prakash argues, was its inability to replicate the labor-absorbing manufacturing model of East Asia: - Between 1999–2000 and 2004–05, India generated 60 million new jobs, but nearly all of them were in low-productivity, unorganized informal activities (casual construction, petty roadside vending, informal rickshaw transport). - High-value modern service exports (software engineering, banking, telecom, consulting) generated immense wealth and corporate profits but required English-speaking, tertiary-educated human capital, structurally excluding the 80% of India's labor force with high-school education or less.

Concluding Historiographical Synthesis B.A. Prakash's *The Indian Economy Since 1991* cements a vital scholarly conclusion: the 1991 economic reforms succeeded brilliantly in unshackling the corporate private sector, modernizing capital markets, building foreign exchange resilience, and dismantling bureaucratic industrial licensing. However, by disproportionately focusing on trade, finance, and corporate taxation while neglecting public investment in agriculture, rural irrigation, primary health, and foundational education, the post-reform growth model generated acute spatial, sectoral, and labor market inequalities that remain India's primary developmental challenges in the twenty-first century.
