Executive Epistemological Overview

Vedanta IAS Academy's *Modern History Notes for Civil Services* (1,179 pages) represents an exhaustive, analytical, and historiographically rigorous masterwork encompassing the transformation of the Indian subcontinent from the twilight of the Mughal Empire in the early eighteenth century to the midnight of Independence and Partition in August 1947. Designed specifically for the analytical demands of the UPSC Civil Services Preliminary and Main Examination (General Studies Paper I), this treatise avoids superficial narrative chronicle, organizing historical knowledge into **structural causal models, institutional mechanisms, economic transformations, and ideological dialectics**.

The masterwork addresses the central questions of modern Indian historiography: 1. **The Nature of Colonial Domination**: Was British rule in India a modernizing civilizing mission (Imperialist school), a brutal system of economic extraction and deindustrialization (Nationalist and Marxist schools), or an opportunistic collaboration with native elites (Cambridge school)? 2. **The Dynamics of Mass Nationalism**: How did the Indian national movement evolve from an elite petitioning forum of Western-educated lawyers into the greatest non-violent mass movement in human history, uniting diverse classes, castes, genders, and linguistic regions? 3. **The Tragedy of Partition**: Was the partition of India an inevitable consequence of pre-existing communal divides, the calculated outcome of British "Divide and Rule" policies, or the political failure of the Congress and Muslim League to negotiate a power-sharing federal consensus?

Unit 1: Decline of the Mughals & The Eighteenth-Century Transition

1.1 The Structural Crisis of the Mughal Empire The death of Emperor **Aurangzeb in 1707** at Ahmednagar marked the irreversible collapse of the centralized Mughal imperial system: - **The Jagirdari and Mansabdari Crisis**: Satish Chandra’s landmark historiographical thesis demonstrates that the Mughal crisis was fundamentally structural and agrarian. The imperial expansion into the Deccan under Aurangzeb drastically increased the number of noble claimants (*mansabdars*) seeking revenue assignments (*jagirs*), creating a severe shortage of productive land (*be-jagiri*). Nobles intensified surplus extraction from the peasantry to maximize yields, triggering widespread agrarian rebellions. - **Peasant and Regional Rebellions**: The Jats (led by Gokla, Rajaram, and later Suraj Mal), the Satnamis of Narnaul, the Bundelas under Chhatrasal, and the Sikhs under Banda Singh Bahadur waged protracted military resistance against imperial tax extraction, shattering imperial authority across Northern India. - **The War of Succession & The Weak Successors**: The rapid succession of puppet emperors (Bahadur Shah I, Jahandar Shah, Farrukhsiyar, the Sayyid Brothers as "Kingmakers", and Muhammad Shah "Rangeela") eroded imperial legitimacy and discipline in the royal army. - **The Devastating External Invasions**: - **Nadir Shah of Persia (1739)**: Crushed the Mughal army at the Battle of Karnal; looted Delhi, massacred 30,000 citizens, and plundered the imperial treasury, carrying away the legendary Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor diamond. - **Ahmad Shah Abdali (1748–1767)**: The Afghan ruler invaded India eight times, plundering Delhi and the Punjab, culminating in the fateful Third Battle of Panipat.

1.2 The Eighteenth-Century Political Landscape: Successor & Warrior States The disintegration of Delhi led not to anarchy, but to a vibrant decentralization into three broad categories of regional states:

By decapitating Maratha leadership and fracturing the Confederacy into autonomous houses (Scindias of Gwalior, Holkars of Indore, Gaekwads of Baroda, and Bhonsles of Nagpur), Panipat cleared the imperial path for the British East India Company.

Unit 2: European Mercantilism & The British Conquest of India

2.1 The Anglo-French Carnatic Wars (1746–1763) The struggle for mastery over South India was fought between the English East India Company and the French Compagnie des Indes, reflecting global conflicts (War of the Austrian Succession and Seven Years' War): 1. **First Carnatic War (1746–1748)**: Battle of St. Thome (1746) on the Adyar river; a tiny French force under Captain Paradise trained in European discipline defeated the massive army of Nawab Anwar-ud-din of Carnatic, exposing the vulnerability of traditional Indian armies. Ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748). 2. **Second Carnatic War (1749–1754)**: Dupleix, the French Governor-General, pioneered the strategy of intervening in indigenous succession disputes (Hyderabad and Carnatic) to secure territorial and financial concessions. Robert Clive’s heroic defense of Arcot (1751) turned the tide for the British. 3. **Third Carnatic War (1758–1763)**: Decisive **Battle of Wandiwash (January 22, 1760)**; British forces under Sir Eyre Coote crushed the French under Count de Lally. The Treaty of Paris (1763) reduced French settlements (Pondicherry, Chandernagore) to unfortified commercial factories, eliminating France as a political rival.

2.2 The Subjugation of Bengal: Plassey to Buxar Bengal was the wealthiest province in Mughal India, contributing 50% of British imports from Asia: - **The Farman of 1717**: Mughal Emperor Farrukhsiyar granted the Company the privilege of duty-free trade (*Dastaks*), which Company servants corruptly abused for their private trade, depriving the Bengal treasury of customs revenues. - **The Battle of Plassey (June 23, 1757)**: Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah challenged British fortification of Calcutta. Robert Clive entered into a conspiracy with Mir Jafar (Commander-in-Chief), Rai Durlabh, Jagat Seth (the richest banker), and Omichand. Siraj was betrayed on the battlefield, captured, and executed. Mir Jafar was installed as a puppet Nawab, ceding the 24 Parganas to the Company. - **The Battle of Buxar (October 22, 1764)**: Mir Qasim (Mir Jafar’s son-in-law) attempted to assert independence, abolished all internal duties to level the field for Indian merchants, and formed a grand confederacy with Nawab Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. Major Hector Munro decisively defeated the combined armies at Buxar. - **The Treaty of Allahabad (August 1765)**: Clive secured from Emperor Shah Alam II the **Diwani Rights** (sole right to collect civil and land revenue) for **Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa** in exchange for an annual tribute of ₹26 lakh: - Established the disastrous **Dual Government in Bengal (1765–1772)**: The Company possessed all financial and military power (*Diwani*) without administrative responsibility; the Nawab held all administrative and judicial responsibility (*Nizamat*) without financial resources. Resulted in the catastrophic **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**, which decimated one-third of Bengal's population (over 10 million people).

2.3 Diplomatic Tools of Imperial Expansion 1. **The Subsidiary Alliance System (Lord Wellesley, 1798–1805)**: - An indigenous ruler entering the alliance surrendered foreign relations to the British, agreed not to employ Europeans without British consent, maintained a British permanent army contingent within their territory at their own expense (or ceded territory in lieu of payment), and accepted a British Resident at their court. - *First State to Sign*: **Hyderabad (1798)**, followed by Mysore (1799), Tanjore (1799), Awadh (1801), Peshwa Baji Rao II (Treaty of Bassein 1802), Scindia, and Bhonsle. 2. **The Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie, 1848–1856)**: - Based on the feudal theory that dependent tributary states could not adopt heirs without imperial sanction. If an Indian ruler died without a natural biological male heir, their state automatically "lapsed" to the British Crown. - *States Annexed*: **Satara (1848)**, Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), **Jhansi (1853)**, and **Nagpur (1854)**. - *Awadh Annexation (1856)*: Annexed by Dalhousie on the grounds of "misgovernance" based on the Outram Report, deposing Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, causing immense fury among Bengal Army sepoys (75,000 of whom were from Awadh).

3.2 The Drain of Wealth Theory The intellectual foundation of Indian economic nationalism was established by **Dadabhai Naoroji** in his classic 1901 work *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*: - **The Concept**: A massive, continuous unilateral transfer of Indian economic surplus to Britain without any commercial, financial, or material return. - **Constituents of the Drain**: 1. *Home Charges*: Annual payments sent from India to London to maintain the India Office, pensions, furloughs, and salaries of retired British civil servants and army officers. 2. *Guaranteed Interest on Railway Capital*: Private British railway investors were guaranteed a 5% risk-free return paid entirely from Indian taxpayers' revenues ("private enterprise at public risk"). 3. *Profits of Foreign Capital Investment*: Remittances of British shipping, banking, and insurance monopolies. 4. *Military Expenditures Abroad*: Indian revenues used to finance British imperialist wars in Afghanistan, Burma, China, and Egypt. - **Quantification**: Naoroji calculated the annual drain at **£30 to £40 million**. R.C. Dutt (*Economic History of India*) demonstrated that one-half of India's net revenue flowed out annually.

3.3 Deindustrialization & Commercialization of Agriculture - **The Deindustrialization of Traditional Crafts**: Importation of cheap, machine-made British textiles from Manchester and Lancashire duty-free into India, while high tariffs were levied on Indian exports to Britain. Destroyed the historic spinning and weaving industries of Dacca, Murshidabad, and Surat, turning India from the world's greatest exporter of manufactured textiles into an exporter of raw cotton and an importer of foreign cloth ("Ruralization of India"). - **Commercialization of Agriculture**: Peasants were coerced into cultivating cash crops for global markets (Indigo, Opium, Jute, Cotton, Tea) rather than food grains, destroying traditional village food security and causing devastating famines (Orissa 1866, Great Famine 1876–78, Bengal 1943).

Unit 4: Popular Resistances & The Revolt of 1857

4.1 Tribal, Peasant and Civil Rebellions Pre-1857 Decades before 1857, colonial extraction sparked fierce localized uprisings: - **Santhal Rebellion (Hool, 1855–1856)**: Led by brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu** in the Damin-i-Koh region (Rajmahal Hills). Santhals rose against British revenue agents, police oppression, and moneylenders; crushed with brutal military force, leading to the creation of the Santhal Parganas district. - **Paika Rebellion (1817)**: The Paikas were traditional landed peasant-militias of Khurda, Odisha, led by **Bakshi Jagabandhu** against British land resumption and salt taxes. - **Kol Uprising (1831–32)**: Chhota Nagpur tribal rebellion led by Buddho Bhagat against land transfer to outsiders (*Dikus*). - **Munda Ulgulan (The Great Tumult, 1899–1900)**: Led by **Birsa Munda** against the erosion of the traditional *Khuntkatti* (joint landholding) system, missionary encroachment, and moneylenders; led to the Chhota Nagpur Tenancy Act of 1908.

4.2 The Great Revolt of 1857: The First War of Independence - **Multiple Underlying Causes**: - *Political*: Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse, annexation of Awadh, humiliation of Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar. - *Economic*: Ruin of artisans, destruction of landholding aristocracy (*Inam Commission 1852* confiscated 20,000 estates in Bombay), extreme land revenue. - *Socio-Religious*: Lex Loci Act 1850 (permitting Christian converts to inherit ancestral property), General Service Enlistment Act 1856 (mandating overseas service, violating Hindu caste purity). - *Military*: Racial discrimination, pay disparity between British and Indian troops, poor service conditions. - **The Immediate Spark**: Introduction of the **Enfield Rifle**, whose paper cartridge was greased with animal fat (beef and pork), requiring sepoys to bite it off before loading. On March 29, 1857, **Mangal Pandey** of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry mutinied at Barrackpore. - **Outbreak**: On **May 10, 1857**, sepoys at **Meerut** mutinied, killed European officers, and marched to Delhi, proclaiming **Bahadur Shah Zafar as the Emperor of Hindustan (*Shahenshah-e-Hindustan*)**.

5.2 Key Reform Pioneers and Institutions 1. **Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833)**: *"Father of Indian Renaissance"*: - Founded the **Atmiya Sabha (1815)** and the **Brahmo Sabha (1828)** (later Brahmo Samaj). - Monotheistic philosophy articulated in *Gift to Monotheists* (Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin). - Championed the abolition of Sati, leading to William Bentinck's **Regulation XVII of 1829**. - Pioneered Indian journalism: *Sambad Kaumudi* (Bengali) and *Mirat-ul-Akhbar* (Persian). 2. **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar**: - Sanskrit scholar and principal of Sanskrit College; opened admission to non-Brahmins. - Crusaded against child marriage and for widow remarriage, directly resulting in the **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (Act XV of 1856)** drafted under Dalhousie and passed under Canning. 3. **Swami Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883)**: - Founded the **Arya Samaj** in Bombay (1875). Authored *Satyarth Prakash* (The Light of Truth). - Gave the clarion call: **"Go Back to the Vedas"**; rejected later Puranic distortions, idol worship, polytheism, and hereditary caste hierarchy. - Pioneered the **Shuddhi Movement** (reconversion) and established the Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) educational network. 4. **Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902)**: - Disciple of Ramakrishna Paramahamsa; founded the **Ramakrishna Mission (1897)** at Belur. - Stunned the world at the **Parliament of Religions in Chicago (September 1893)**. - Preached Neo-Vedanta, synthesizing Hindu spirituality with Western social service: *"Service to Jiva is Service to Shiva."* 5. **Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890)**: - Founded the **Satyashodhak Samaj (1873)** in Maharashtra to emancipate lower castes and women. - Authored the seminal radical critique of Brahmanical supremacy: **Gulamgiri (1873)**. - With wife Savitribai Phule, established India’s first schools for girls and untouchables in Pune (1848). 6. **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898)**: - Founded the **Aligarh Movement** and established the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College** at Aligarh (1875), which evolved into Aligarh Muslim University (AMU). - Advocated modern Western scientific education for Indian Muslims, opposed purdah and polygamy, and edited *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*.

Unit 6: Early Nationalist Awakening & The Moderate-Extremist Phase

6.1 The Foundation of the Indian National Congress (1885) - Founded in **December 1885** at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College in Bombay, attended by **72 delegates**. - Organized by retired British civil servant **Allan Octavian Hume (A.O. Hume)**; **W.C. Bonnerjee** was elected the first President. - **The "Safety Valve" Controversy**: Lala Lajpat Rai (and later R.P. Dutt) popularized the thesis that Hume formed the Congress as a "safety valve" to release boiling mass discontent against British rule, avoiding another 1857-style explosion. Modern historians (Bipan Chandra) demonstrate that the early nationalist leaders utilized Hume as a "lightning conductor" to establish a pan-Indian political platform without facing immediate colonial suppression.

6.2 The Moderate Phase (1885–1905) - **Prominent Leaders**: Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, Dinshaw Wacha, Surendranath Banerjea, Justice M.G. Ranade. - **Methods**: Constitutional agitation within the four corners of the law; the **"3Ps" (Prayer, Petition, Protest)**, public speeches, and sending delegations to London. - **Contributions**: 1. *Economic Critique of Colonialism*: Unmasked the exploitative nature of British rule (Drain Theory, deindustrialization). 2. *Constitutional Reforms*: Demanded legislative expansion, resulting in the Indian Councils Act of 1892. 3. *Indianization of Services*: Demanded simultaneous civil service examinations in London and India. 4. *Defense of Civil Liberties*: Fought against press restrictions (Lord Lytton’s Vernacular Press Act 1878).

6.3 The Rise of the Extremists (1905–1919) - **The Trinity (Lal-Bal-Pal) & Aurobindo Ghose**: Lala Lajpat Rai (Punjab), Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Maharashtra), Bipin Chandra Pal (Bengal). - **Core Philosophy**: Hatred for foreign rule; absolute political independence (**Purna Swaraj**); pride in India’s ancient heritage; reliance on mass action and self-reliance (*Atmashakti*). - **Tilak’s Clarion Call**: *"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!"* Tilak revived the Ganpati (1893) and Shivaji (1895) festivals to foster nationalist consciousness, editing *Kesari* (Marathi) and *Mahratta* (English).

Unit 7: Swadeshi, Revolutionary Nationalism & The Home Rule Movement

7.1 The Partition of Bengal (1905) and the Swadeshi Movement In July 1905, Viceroy **Lord Curzon** issued the order partitioning the Presidency of Bengal into two provinces: Western Bengal (Hindu majority) and Eastern Bengal & Assam (Muslim majority), falsely claiming administrative necessity. - **Real Motive**: To fracture the nerve centre of Indian nationalism and drive a communal wedge between Bengali Hindus and Muslims. - **The Swadeshi Movement**: Formal proclamation on **August 7, 1905**, at Calcutta Town Hall; passing of the **Boycott Resolution**: - *Day of Partition (October 16, 1905)*: Observed as a day of national mourning; fasting, singing of Rabindranath Tagore's *Amar Sonar Bangla*, and tying *Rakshabandhan* threads as a symbol of Bengali brotherhood. - *Methods*: Boycott of foreign cloth, sugar, and salt; picketing of shops; burning of foreign goods; establishment of national educational institutions (National Council of Education 1906, Bengal National College headed by Aurobindo Ghose); promotion of indigenous industries (Prafulla Chandra Roy’s Bengal Chemicals). - *Annulment*: In **1911**, King George V announced the **annulment of the Partition of Bengal** at the Delhi Durbar, alongside shifting the imperial capital from Calcutta to Delhi.

7.2 The Surat Split (1907) At the December 1907 Surat Session of Congress (held on the banks of the Tapti river under the presidency of **Dr. Rashbehari Ghosh**), ideological factionalism between Moderates and Extremists exploded into open physical confrontation: - Moderates sought to confine Swadeshi and Boycott to Bengal; Extremists demanded extending Swadeshi, Boycott, and National Education across all of India to attain complete Swaraj. - Congress split; Extremists were expelled, while the colonial government cracked down brutally, imprisoning Tilak for six years in Mandalay, Burma (1908–1914), while Aurobindo Ghose retired to Pondicherry for spiritual asceticism.

7.3 Phase I of Revolutionary Nationalism Frustrated by constitutional moderation and state terror, radical youth turned to armed insurrection: - **Bengal**: Anushilan Samiti (Promotha Mitter, Barindra Ghosh, Bhupendranath Datta) and *Yugantar*; Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki threw bombs at Judge Kingsford’s carriage at Muzaffarpur (1908); Alipore Conspiracy Case. - **Maharashtra**: Chapekar Brothers (Damodar and Balkrishna) assassinated Plague Commissioner Rand (1897); V.D. Savarkar founded **Abhinav Bharat (1904)**. - **Abroad**: Shyamji Krishna Varma established **India House** and the Indian Home Rule Society in London (1905); Madan Lal Dhingra assassinated Curzon Wyllie (1909); **Madam Bhikaji Cama** unfurled the first Indian National Flag at the International Socialist Conference in Stuttgart, Germany (1907). - **The Ghadar Movement (1913)**: Founded in San Francisco by **Lala Hardayal**, Sohan Singh Bhakna, and Kartar Singh Sarabha, publishing the weekly paper *Ghadar*. The **Komagata Maru incident (1914)** (Japanese steamship carrying Sikh passengers denied entry into Canada) sparked armed mutiny attempts in Punjab.

7.4 The Lucknow Pact (1916) & The Home Rule League - **The Home Rule League Movement (1916)**: Inspired by the Irish Home Rule movement: - *Tilak’s League* (April 1916): Covered Maharashtra (excluding Bombay city), Karnataka, Central Provinces, and Berar. - *Annie Besant’s League* (September 1916): Covered the rest of India, operating through newspapers *New India* and *Commonweal*. - **The Lucknow Session of INC (1916)** (Presided over by Ambica Charan Mazumdar): 1. *Re-entry of Extremists*: Moderates and Extremists reunited after nine years of separation. 2. *The Lucknow Pact (Congress-League Pact)*: Congress and Muslim League presented a joint constitutional scheme, but Congress made the fateful concession of accepting **Separate Electorates for Muslims**.

Unit 8: The Gandhian Epoch: Early Experiments to Non-Cooperation & Civil Disobedience

8.1 Gandhi's Early Satyagraha Laboratories (1917–1918) Returning to India on **January 9, 1915** (*Pravasi Bharatiya Divas*), Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi spent two years touring the subcontinent on the advice of his political mentor, **Gopal Krishna Gokhale**: 1. **Champaran Satyagraha (1917, Bihar)**: First Civil Disobedience. Fought against European indigo planters forcing peasants to cultivate indigo on 3/20th of their land (**Tinkathia System**). Gandhi forced the government to abolish Tinkathia and refund 25% of illegally extracted money. 2. **Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918, Gujarat)**: First Hunger Strike. Dispute between mill owners and workers over the withdrawal of the "Plague Bonus." Gandhi undertook a fast unto death, securing a **35% wage hike** for workers. 3. **Kheda Satyagraha (1918, Gujarat)**: First Non-Cooperation. Crops failed due to drought; revenue code exempted taxes if yield was below 25%, but British authorities refused remissions. Gandhi and Sardar Patel organized peasants to withhold revenue; government secretly issued orders to collect taxes only from those who could pay.

8.2 The Rowlatt Satyagraha & Jallianwala Bagh (1919) - **The Rowlatt Act (Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, March 1919)**: Authorized the government to imprison any person suspected of terrorism for up to two years without trial ("No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"). - **Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919)**: On Baisakhi day, an unarmed crowd assembled at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar to peacefully protest the arrest of leaders **Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew** and **Dr. Satyapal**. Brigadier-General **Reginald Dyer** blocked the sole exit and ordered troops to fire 1,650 rounds without warning, killing over 1,000 citizens. - Rabindranath Tagore renounced his British **Knighthood**; Gandhi renounced his **Kaiser-i-Hind** gold medal.

8.3 The Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement (1920–1922) - Formally launched on **August 1, 1920** (the day Bal Gangadhar Tilak passed away). Unfolded in tandem with the **Khilafat Movement** (led by the Ali Brothers: Shaukat Ali and Mohammad Ali) protesting the dismemberment of the Ottoman Empire and the dethroning of the Caliph (*Khalifa*). - **Program of Action**: Surrender of titles and honorary offices; boycott of government schools, colleges, law courts, and foreign cloth; picketing of liquor shops; promotion of Charkha, Khadi, and national schools (Kashi Vidyapeeth, Jamia Millia Islamia). - **The Chauri Chaura Incident (February 5, 1922)**: At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district, UP, an agitated peasant procession was fired upon by police; the mob retaliated by locking 22 policemen inside the police station and setting it on fire. - Gandhi abruptly **suspended the Non-Cooperation Movement on February 12, 1922** (Bardoli Resolution), holding that the masses had not yet mastered the spiritual discipline of non-violence.

8.4 The Civil Disobedience Movement & The Salt Satyagraha (1930) - **The Simon Commission Boycott (1927–28)**: An all-white, seven-member statutory commission appointed under Sir John Simon to review the working of the 1919 Act. Greeted with black flags and "Simon Go Back" slogans. Lala Lajpat Rai was fatally beaten by police lathis at Lahore. - **The Nehru Report (1928)**: Chaired by Motilal Nehru; first indigenous draft of a constitution. Demanded **Dominion Status**, Joint Electorates with reservation of seats for minorities, adult suffrage, and 19 fundamental rights. Jinnah countered with his **Fourteen Points**. - **The Lahore Congress (December 1929)**: Chaired by Jawaharlal Nehru; passed the historic **Purna Swaraj (Complete Independence)** resolution. January 26, 1930, was declared the first Independence Day. - **The Dandi Salt March (March 12 – April 6, 1930)**: Gandhi, accompanied by 78 chosen disciples, walked **240 miles** from Sabarmati Ashram to the coastal village of Dandi. On the morning of April 6, 1930, Gandhi picked up a handful of salt from the seashore, violating the British monopoly law, igniting the **Civil Disobedience Movement** across India. - **The Gandhi-Irwin Pact (March 5, 1931)**: Viceroy Irwin agreed to release political prisoners not guilty of violence and permit peaceful picketing; Gandhi agreed to suspend Civil Disobedience and participate in the **Second Round Table Conference** in London. - **The Communal Award & The Poona Pact (1932)**: British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald announced the **Communal Award (August 1932)**, extending separate electorates to the "Depressed Classes." Gandhi undertook a fast unto death in Yerwada Jail, Poona. On September 24, 1932, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and Gandhi's representatives signed the Poona Pact**: abandoned separate electorates for depressed classes in exchange for **increasing reserved seats in provincial legislatures from 71 to 147**.

Unit 9: The Radical Left, Subhas Chandra Bose & The Road to Quit India

9.1 The Radical Left and Revolutionary Phase II - **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA, 1928)**: Reorganized at Feroz Shah Kotla, Delhi, by **Chandrashekhar Azad, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru**, adding "Socialist" to the organization's name: - *Saunders Assassination (Lahore, December 1928)*: Avenged the death of Lala Lajpat Rai. - *Central Legislative Assembly Bombing (April 8, 1929)*: Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw harmless smoke bombs into the assembly to protest the Public Safety Bill and Trade Disputes Bill: *"To make the deaf hear."* - Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru were hanged on **March 23, 1931** (*Shaheed Diwas*). - **Chittagong Armoury Raid (April 1930)**: Led by **Surya Sen (Masterda)** and the Indian Republican Army (including heroic female revolutionaries Kalpana Datta and Pritilata Waddedar) in Bengal.

9.2 The Gandhi-Bose Rift (1938–1939) - **Haripura Congress (1938)**: Subhas Chandra Bose was unanimously elected President; established the **National Planning Committee** headed by Jawaharlal Nehru. - **Tripuri Congress (1939)**: Bose contested for re-election against Gandhi’s chosen candidate, **Pattabhi Sitaramayya**, and won by 1,580 to 1,377 votes. Gandhi declared: *"Pattabhi's defeat is my defeat."* - Ideological impasse: Bose advocated delivering an immediate six-month ultimatum to Britain to grant independence or face mass armed rebellion during the impending European war; Gandhi maintained that the nation was not ready for mass struggle. - Faced with executive non-cooperation from the Congress Working Committee, Bose resigned in April 1939, founded the **Forward Bloc**, and was subsequently expelled from all official Congress posts.

9.3 The Quit India Movement (August 1942) Following the collapse of Sir Stafford Cripps' mission (which offered post-war Dominion Status, famously rejected by Gandhi as a *"post-dated cheque on a crashing bank"*), the All-India Congress Committee met at **Gowalia Tank Maidan, Bombay (August 8, 1942)**: - Gandhi delivered his historic address: > *"Here is a mantra, a short one, that I give you. The mantra is: **DO OR DIE**. We shall either free India or die in the attempt."* - **Operation Zero Hour**: In the early hours of August 9, 1942, the colonial government arrested Gandhi and all members of the Congress Working Committee. - **The Leaderless Mass Upsurge**: The movement erupted into spontaneous mass fury: youth attacked government infrastructure, burned police stations, severed railway and telegraph lines. - **Underground Networks & Parallel Governments (*Prati Sarkars*)**: - *Underground Leaders*: Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali, and Usha Mehta (operating the secret Congress Radio). - *Parallel Governments*: Ballia (UP) under Chittu Pandey; Tamluk (Bengal) Jatiya Sarkar; Satara (Maharashtra) *Prati Sarkar* under Nana Patil.

Unit 10: Wavell Plan, Partition Dialectics & The Indian Independence Act (1947)

10.1 The INA Exploits & The Royal Indian Navy Mutiny - **The Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)**: Conceived initially by Captain Mohan Singh with Japanese military cooperation; resurrected and commanded by **Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose** in Singapore in 1943. Set up the Provisional Government of Free India (*Arzi Hukumat-e-Azad Hind*). Formed the Rani of Jhansi Regiment under Captain Lakshmi Sahgal. Marched to the Indian border with the battle cry *"Chalo Delhi!"* - **The INA Trials (Red Fort, 1945–46)**: Court-martial of Prem Sahgal (Hindu), Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon (Sikh), and Shah Nawaz Khan (Muslim) triggered unprecedented pan-Indian emotional solidarity. Congress organized a legal defense team (Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, Jawaharlal Nehru). - **The Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (February 18, 1946)**: Over 20,000 ratings on the naval training ship **HMIS Talwar** in Bombay struck work over racial discrimination, bad food, and the arrest of B.C. Dutt. Spread to 78 ships across Karachi, Calcutta, and Madras. The mutiny shattered Britain's fundamental assumption: **the loyalty of the Indian armed forces to the Crown had evaporated**.

10.2 The Constitutional Endgame 1. **The Cabinet Mission Plan (May 1946)**: Comprising Pethick-Lawrence, Stafford Cripps, and A.V. Alexander. Rejected the demand for Pakistan; proposed a loose three-tier federation with an autonomous grouping of provinces into three sections: Group A (Hindu-majority), Group B (Muslim-majority northwest), Group C (Bengal and Assam). Disagreements over compulsory grouping led to breakdown. 2. **Direct Action Day (August 16, 1946)**: Jinnah called for "Direct Action" to enforce Pakistan, triggering the horrific **Great Calcutta Killings** (over 4,000 deaths in 72 hours), igniting communal pogroms across Noakhali, Bihar, and Punjab. 3. **Attlee's Statement (February 20, 1947)**: British Prime Minister Clement Attlee declared the British intention to transfer power to responsible Indian hands by **June 30, 1948**, appointing Lord Louis Mountbatten as the last Viceroy. 4. **The Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947)**: Advanced the transfer of power by ten months to **August 15, 1947**. Envisaged the partition of British India into two sovereign Dominions (India and Pakistan); Punjab and Bengal assemblies voted for partition; the **Radcliffe Boundary Commission** drew the borders. 5. **The Indian Independence Act of 1947**: Passed by the British Parliament on July 18, 1947; terminated British suzerainty over India from August 15, 1947, repealing the Government of India Act 1935 and ending two centuries of colonial subjugation.
