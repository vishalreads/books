## Unit 1: Macroeconomic Fundamentals, Circular Flow & Output Accounting

### 1.1 The Central Economic Problem and Economic Organization
Every economic society, irrespective of its ideological configuration, faces the inescapable reality of **scarcity**: human wants are unlimited, whereas the resources (factors of production: land, labor, capital, and enterprise) available to satisfy them are finite and have alternative uses. This scarcity necessitates choices across three fundamental economic questions:
1. **What to produce and in what quantities?** Allocation of scarce resources between consumption goods and capital goods, civil commodities and defense supplies.
2. **How to produce?** The choice of production technique—labor-intensive techniques (utilizing abundant labor relative to capital) versus capital-intensive techniques (utilizing machinery and advanced technology).
3. **For whom to produce?** The distribution of aggregate national output among the owners of the factors of production in the form of rent, wages, interest, and profit.

Economic systems organize these choices through three institutional paradigms:
- **Command / Socialist Economy**: Resource allocation determined centrally by the state through central planning authorities.
- **Market / Capitalist Economy**: Resource allocation governed entirely by the price mechanism through the interplay of decentralized consumer demand and producer supply.
- **Mixed Economy (India)**: A dual institutional framework where strategic core industries and social infrastructure operate under public ownership/regulation, while consumer commodities and services operate via competitive private enterprise.

### 1.2 The Four Sectors and Circular Flow of Income
Vivek Singh frames the macroeconomic system through the interactions of four distinct sectors:
1. **Household Sector**: Owners of factors of production (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship) who provide factor services to firms and consume final goods and services.
2. **Firm / Business Sector**: Productive units that hire factor services from households to generate goods and services, paying factor compensation (wages, rent, interest, profit).
3. **Government Sector**: Enacts laws, levies direct and indirect taxes, purchases commodities, pays subsidies, and provides public goods (defense, law and order, administrative infrastructure).
4. **External / Rest of the World Sector**: Mediates international trade (exports and imports of merchandise and services) and cross-border capital transactions.

```
       Factor Services (Land, Labor, Capital, Enterprise)
     ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                        │
┌──────────────┐      Factor Payments (Rent, Wages, Interest) ┌──────────────┐
│  HOUSEHOLDS  │ ───────────────────────────────────────────► │    FIRMS     │
└──────────────┘                                              └──────────────┘
     │ ▲              Consumer Expenditure on Final Goods     │ ▲
     │ └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ │
```

The circular flow identity mandates that across the aggregate economy:
$$\text{National Product} \equiv \text{National Income} \equiv \text{National Expenditure}$$

### 1.3 National Income Aggregates and NSO Methodology
In India, the National Statistical Office (NSO) within the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) compiles and reports national accounts data. The core aggregates follow established structural definitions:

$$\text{GDP at Market Prices} = \sum_{i} (P_i \times Q_i) \quad \text{within the domestic territory}$$

$$\text{NDP at Market Prices} = \text{GDP} - \text{Depreciation (Consumption of Fixed Capital)}$$

$$\text{GNP at Market Prices} = \text{GDP} + \text{Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)}$$

$$\text{NNP at Market Prices} = \text{GNP} - \text{Depreciation} = \text{National Income}$$

The 2015 national accounts methodological overhaul introduced the **GVA at Basic Prices** framework aligned with the United Nations System of National Accounts (SNA 2008):

$$\text{GVA at Basic Prices} = \text{GVA at Factor Cost} + (\text{Production Taxes} - \text{Production Subsidies})$$

$$\text{GDP at Market Prices} = \text{GVA at Basic Prices} + (\text{Product Taxes} - \text{Product Subsidies})$$

- **Production Taxes/Subsidies**: Unrelated to the volume of physical output (land revenues, stamp duties, municipal property tax; input subsidies to producers).
- **Product Taxes/Subsidies**: Levied strictly on a per-unit output basis (GST, central excise duties, customs levies; per-unit product subsidies like food and fertilizer subsidies).

### 1.4 Productivity, Capital Output Ratio and ICOR
The efficiency with which capital is utilized in an economy is governed by the **Capital-Output Ratio (COR)** and the **Incremental Capital-Output Ratio (ICOR)**:

$$\text{ICOR} = \frac{\Delta K}{\Delta Y} = \frac{\text{Investment as a \% of GDP}}{\text{GDP Growth Rate}}$$

A lower ICOR indicates higher capital efficiency—fewer units of capital investment are required to produce an additional unit of real GDP. In developing economies like India, ICOR typically hovers between **3.8 and 4.5**. If India's investment rate (Gross Capital Formation) is 32% of GDP and its ICOR is 4.0, the sustainable annual growth rate is:
$$\text{Growth Rate} = \frac{32\%}{4.0} = 8.0\%$$

### 1.5 Potential GDP and the Output Gap
$$\text{Potential GDP} = \text{Highest level of real output an economy can sustain without triggering inflation}$$

$$\text{Output Gap} = \text{Actual GDP} - \text{Potential GDP}$$

- **Positive Output Gap**: Actual GDP exceeds Potential GDP, indicating resource over-utilization, labor shortages, and demand-pull inflationary pressures.
- **Negative Output Gap**: Actual GDP falls short of Potential GDP, signaling idle factory capacity, high unemployment, and deflationary or stagnation pressures.

## Unit 2: Monetary Architecture, Money Creation & Financial Markets

### 2.1 The Concept of Money and Fiat Currency
Money performs four classic functions:
1. **Primary**: Medium of exchange and unit of account.
2. **Secondary**: Store of value and standard of deferred payments.

Modern currencies operate as **Fiat Money**—they have no intrinsic commodity value (unlike gold or silver coins) and are not backed by precious metal reserves. They derive purchasing power solely from **statutory legal tender declaration** by the sovereign government (e.g., Section 26 of the RBI Act, 1934). **Legal Tender** implies that by law, a creditor cannot refuse settlement of a debt tendered in this currency.
- **Limited Legal Tender**: Coins (under Coinage Act 2011, coins can only be legally tendered up to ₹1,000).
- **Unlimited Legal Tender**: Currency notes issued by the Reserve Bank of India.

### 2.2 Money Stock Measures and Monetary Aggregates
The Reserve Bank of India classifies domestic money supply into four standardized monetary aggregates based on liquidity:

$$\text{M}_0 \text{ (Reserve Money / High-Powered Money / Monetary Base)} = C + OD + \text{Bank Deposits with RBI}$$

$$\text{M}_1 \text{ (Narrow Money)} = C + DD + OD$$

$$\text{M}_2 = \text{M}_1 + \text{Post Office Savings Bank Deposits}$$

$$\text{M}_3 \text{ (Broad Money)} = \text{M}_1 + \text{Time Deposits with Banking System}$$

$$\text{M}_4 = \text{M}_3 + \text{Total Post Office Deposits (excluding National Savings Certificates)}$$

*Where:*  
- $C$ = Currency in circulation with the public (notes + coins).  
- $DD$ = Demand deposits of the public with commercial banks (current accounts + savings account balances).  
- $OD$ = 'Other' deposits held with the RBI (deposits of foreign central banks, quasi-government institutions, multilateral agencies like IMF/World Bank).

### 2.3 Fractional Reserve Banking and the Money Multiplier
Commercial banks do not keep 100% of customer deposits in cash vaults; they maintain a legally mandated fraction as liquid reserves (CRR and SLR) and lend out the remainder, generating secondary credit:

$$\text{Money Multiplier } (m) = \frac{\text{Broad Money } (\text{M}_3)}{\text{Reserve Money } (\text{M}_0)} = \frac{1 + c}{c + r}$$

*Where:*  
- $c = \text{Currency-to-Deposit Ratio} = \frac{\text{Currency held by public}}{\text{Total Bank Deposits}}$  
- $r = \text{Reserve-to-Deposit Ratio} = \frac{\text{Bank Cash Reserves}}{\text{Total Bank Deposits}}$

When the public withdraws large cash volumes from banks (increasing $c$), or the RBI hikes statutory cash reserve ratios (increasing $r$), the money multiplier contracts, curbing the banking sector's capacity to create new deposit money.

#### Key Money Market Instruments
1. **Call Money / Notice Money**: Interbank borrowing market for immediate cash management without collateral. *Call Money* = 1 day (overnight); *Notice Money* = 2 to 14 days; *Term Money* = 15 days to 1 year.
2. **Treasury Bills (T-Bills)**: Short-term zero-coupon debt instruments issued by the RBI on behalf of the Central Government to meet temporary cash mismatches. Issued at three maturities: **91-day, 182-day, and 364-day**. Issued at a discount to face value and redeemed at par (zero coupon).
3. **Commercial Paper (CP)**: Unsecured short-term promissory notes issued by highly rated corporate entities, primary dealers, and all-India financial institutions to raise working capital. Tenor ranges from **7 days to 1 year**.
4. **Certificate of Deposit (CD)**: Promissory notes issued by scheduled commercial banks and financial institutions against bulk funds deposited for a specified maturity (**7 days to 1 year for banks**).

### 2.5 Bond Pricing and Yield Mechanics
$$\text{Bond Price} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1 + Y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1 + Y)^n}$$

$$\text{Current Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Coupon Payment}}{\text{Current Market Price of Bond}}$$

There is an invariant **inverse relationship between bond prices and bond yields**:
$$\text{Bond Price } \uparrow \iff \text{Bond Yield } \downarrow$$

- **Inverted Yield Curve**: Occurs when short-term interest rates exceed long-term yields. This phenomenon indicates that bond market participants expect future economic deceleration, monetary easing, or a recession, and constitutes one of the most reliable leading indicators of economic downturns.

## Unit 3: Reserve Bank of India & Monetary Policy Transmission Architecture

### 3.1 Statutory Mandate and the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)
Under the **RBI Act, 1934 (amended in 2016)**, India formally instituted a statutory **Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT)** framework:
- **Inflation Target**: Mandated under **Section 45ZA** at **4% Consumer Price Index (Combined)** with a tolerance band of **±2%** (minimum 2%, maximum 6%).
- **Monetary Policy Committee (Section 45ZB)**: A 6-member body:
  - 3 Members from the RBI: Governor (ex-officio Chairperson with casting vote), Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and one RBI officer.
  - 3 External Members: Appointed by the Central Government for a non-renewable 4-year term.
- **Accountability Clause**: If the RBI fails to meet the inflation target for three consecutive quarters, it must submit an official explanatory report to the Central Government detailing: (a) reasons for failure, (b) proposed corrective measures, and (c) an estimated timeline within which the target will be restored.

### 3.2 Quantitative Monetary Policy Instruments

```
                    LIQUIDITY ADJUSTMENT FACILITY (LAF) CORRIDOR
   Ceiling: Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) Rate ─── [Repo + 25 bps]
                          ▲
                          │
   Anchor Policy Rate:   REPO RATE
                          │
                          ▼
   Floor:   Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) Rate ───── [Repo - 25 bps]
```

1. **Repo Rate (Repurchase Option)**: The benchmark interest rate at which the RBI lends short-term liquidity to scheduled commercial banks against eligible government securities with a repurchase agreement.
2. **Reverse Repo Rate**: The rate at which the RBI borrows liquid funds from commercial banks against collateralized government securities.
3. **Standing Deposit Facility (SDF)**: Introduced under Section 17 of the RBI Act to absorb excess overnight liquidity without requiring the RBI to provide collateral securities in return, overcoming the central bank's constraint of holding insufficient government paper during massive liquidity surpluses.
4. **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)**: An emergency borrowing window through which banks can borrow overnight funds up to a designated percentage of their Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) dipping into their SLR securities at a penal interest rate.
5. **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)**: The statutory percentage of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that commercial banks must maintain in liquid cash form with the RBI. No interest is paid by the RBI on CRR balances.
6. **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)**: The percentage of NDTL that commercial banks must invest in approved liquid assets—unencumbered government securities, treasury bills, state development loans (SDLs), gold, or cash—before extending credit to the private sector.
7. **Open Market Operations (OMOs)**: Direct outright purchase or sale of government securities in secondary markets by the RBI to inject or drain durable liquidity from the banking system.

### 3.3 The RBI Balance Sheet and the Economic Capital Framework
The RBI's income is generated from interest on domestic government bonds, returns on foreign currency assets, and underwriting operations. The **Bimal Jalan Committee (2019)** reviewed the RBI's Economic Capital Framework (ECF), dividing reserve capital into:
- **Contingency Risk Buffer (CRB)**: Maintained between **5.5% and 6.5% of total balance sheet assets** to protect against unforeseen financial, monetary, and exchange rate shocks.
- **Surplus Transfer**: Realized profits beyond the CRB threshold are legally transferred to the Government of India as an annual dividend, serving as a non-tax revenue source in the Union Budget.

## Unit 4: Banking Evolution, NPA Resolution & Prudential Architecture

### 4.1 Chronology of Lending Rate Frameworks in India
To ensure transparent monetary transmission from the RBI's policy repo rate to real loan interest rates, the pricing mechanism evolved through three regimes:
1. **Base Rate System (2010–2016)**: Banks determined a minimum threshold rate based on average cost of funds. Criticized for opacity, as banks delayed rate cuts when the RBI lowered Repo rates.
2. **Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate (MCLR) (2016–2019)**: Calculated using marginal cost of borrowing, operating costs, tenor premium, and CRR cost. Transmission improved but remained slow, as deposit rate adjustments lagged policy changes.
3. **External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR) (Mandatory from October 2019)**: For all new floating-rate retail loans (home, auto) and loans to MSMEs, banks are mandated to link lending rates directly to an external benchmark:
   - RBI Policy Repo Rate, OR
   - Government of India 91-Day or 182-Day Treasury Bill Yield.
   - Banks may add a transparent credit risk spread, but transmission of RBI rate cuts is immediate and automated.

### 4.2 Basel III Capital Adequacy Architecture
Mandated by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) and implemented by the RBI, Basel III mandates capital reserves to absorb shocks:

$$\text{Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) / CRAR} = \frac{\text{Tier 1 Capital} + \text{Tier 2 Capital}}{\text{Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)}} \times 100$$

- **Minimum CAR for Scheduled Commercial Banks in India**: **9.0%** (RBI mandates **11.5%** including the 2.5% Capital Conservation Buffer).
- **Tier 1 Capital (Going-Concern Capital)**: Common Equity Tier-1 (CET-1: paid-up equity, statutory reserves) + Additional Tier-1 (AT-1 bonds with loss-absorption write-down clauses).
- **Tier 2 Capital (Gone-Concern Capital)**: Subordinated debt, general provisions, and revaluation reserves.

### 4.3 Stressed Assets and NPA Taxonomy
An asset becomes a **Non-Performing Asset (NPA)** when it ceases to generate income for the bank:
- **Special Mention Accounts (SMA)**: Early stress detection:
  - **SMA-0**: Principal or interest overdue for 1–30 days.
  - **SMA-1**: Overdue for 31–60 days.
  - **SMA-2**: Overdue for 61–90 days.
- **NPA Classification**:
  - **Sub-standard Asset**: Overdue > 90 days, remains an NPA for up to 12 months. Requires 15% general provisioning.
  - **Doubtful Asset**: Remains sub-standard for > 12 months. Provisioning escalates: 25% (up to 1 yr), 40% (1–3 yrs), and 100% (unsecured).
  - **Loss Asset**: Uncollectible and of negligible value, identified by internal/external auditors or the RBI. Requires 100% provisioning.

### 4.4 Resolution Mechanisms: SARFAESI and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC)
- **SARFAESI Act, 2002 (Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest)**: Empowers secured banks and financial institutions to enforce security interests and seize collateralized physical assets without the intervention of a court or tribunal. Does not apply to agricultural land or debts below ₹1 lakh.
- **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016**: Shifted Indian corporate finance from a "Debtor-in-Possession" to a **"Creditor-in-Control"** regime:
  - Triggered by default (minimum threshold ₹1 crore).
  - Managed by an Insolvency Resolution Professional (IRP) appointed by the **Committee of Creditors (CoC)**.
  - Mandated **Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)** timeline: 180 days, extendable to 330 days.
  - If no viable resolution plan receives approval from **66% of voting financial creditors**, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) orders corporate liquidation.
- **Bad Bank Architecture**: **National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL)** acquires bad loans from banks at an agreed value (paying 15% cash and 85% in Government-guaranteed Security Receipts), while **India Debt Resolution Company Limited (IDRCL)** manages turnaround, restructuring, and asset disposal.

## Unit 5: Government Budgeting, Fiscal Deficits & Debt Sustainability

### 5.1 Constitutional Funds of India
Under the Constitution of India, all state revenues, expenditures, and capital flows pass through three accounts:
1. **Article 266(1): Consolidated Fund of India**: All revenues received by the Government (taxes, cess, fees), all loans raised by issuing Treasury Bills, and all money received in repayment of loans. **No amount can be withdrawn without parliamentary statutory authorization (Appropriation Act)**.
2. **Article 266(2): Public Account of India**: All public moneys received by or on behalf of the Government other than those credited to the Consolidated Fund (Provident Funds, Small Savings collections, Postal Insurance, Judicial deposits). Operated by executive action; does not require prior parliamentary appropriation.
3. **Article 267: Contingency Fund of India**: An imprest fund held by the President of India (administered by the Finance Secretary) to meet urgent, unforeseen expenditures pending parliamentary approval. Recharged from the Consolidated Fund via supplementary grants.

### 5.3 Mathematical Identities of Fiscal Deficits

$$\text{Revenue Deficit (RD)} = \text{Revenue Expenditure} - \text{Revenue Receipts}$$

$$\text{Effective Revenue Deficit (ERD)} = \text{RD} - \text{Grants for Creation of Capital Assets}$$

$$\text{Fiscal Deficit (FD)} = \text{Total Expenditure} - (\text{Revenue Receipts} + \text{Non-Debt Capital Receipts})$$

$$\text{Primary Deficit (PD)} = \text{Fiscal Deficit} - \text{Net Interest Payments}$$

- **Fiscal Deficit** represents the total borrowing requirement of the Union Government from all domestic and external sources to bridge the budgetary expenditure gap.
- **Primary Deficit** measures the current fiscal stance independent of past debt burdens. A zero Primary Deficit indicates that government borrowings are dedicated entirely to financing interest charges inherited from historical debt.

### 5.4 The FRBM Act, 2003 and the N.K. Singh Committee
The **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003** institutionalized fiscal discipline. The **N.K. Singh Committee (2017)** restructured the framework around a debt-to-GDP anchor:
- **Debt Anchor**: Recommended general government debt-to-GDP ratio of **60% by 2023** (**40% for the Central Government** and **20% for all State Governments combined**).
- **Fiscal Deficit Target**: 3.0% of GDP.
- **Escape Clause**: Allows the Government to breach fiscal deficit targets by up to **0.5% of GDP** under specified exceptional circumstances: national security threats, war, national natural calamities, collapse of agricultural output, or structural economic reforms resulting in unanticipated fiscal disruption.

## Unit 6: Taxation Architecture, GST Mechanics & Fiscal Federalism

### 6.1 Direct vs. Indirect Taxes and Tax Buoyancy
- **Direct Taxes**: Incidence and impact fall on the same legal entity (Income Tax, Corporation Tax). Progressive in nature—marginal tax rates increase with income, mitigating wealth disparities.
- **Indirect Taxes**: Incidence falls on the producer/seller, but the impact is passed on to the final consumer via commodity pricing (GST, customs duty, excise duty). Regressive in nature, as poor and wealthy consumers pay identical nominal taxes on identical goods.
- **Tax Buoyancy**:
$$\text{Tax Buoyancy} = \frac{\% \text{ Change in Gross Tax Revenue}}{\% \text{ Change in GDP (Nominal)}}$$
A tax buoyancy greater than 1.0 indicates that tax collections expand faster than the underlying national output without requiring frequent increases in statutory tax rates.

### 6.2 Goods and Services Tax (GST) Architecture
Enacted via the **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016**, GST subsumed 17 central and state levies into a comprehensive, destination-based value-added tax on the manufacture, sale, and consumption of goods and services:

```
                                GST TAX STRUCTURE
                ┌───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
                ▼                       ▼                       ▼
              CGST                    SGST / UTGST             IGST
        (Central Govt on        (State/UT Govt on        (Central Govt on
        Intra-state sales)      Intra-state sales)       Inter-state trade &
                                                          imports; apportioned)
```

#### The Input Tax Credit (ITC) Mechanism
GST eliminates the compounding "tax-on-tax" (cascading effect) through an unbroken Input Tax Credit chain:

$$\text{Net Tax Payable at Stage } N = \text{Tax on Output Value} - \text{Input Tax Credit (Taxes Paid at Stage } N-1)$$

- **Inverted Duty Structure**: Arises when the tax rate on raw material inputs is higher than the GST rate on the finished final product, trapping unutilized input credits on corporate balance sheets and requiring administrative tax refunds.

### 6.3 Constitutional Mechanics of the GST Council (Article 279A)
The GST Council serves as the constitutional body determining tax slabs, exemption lists, and statutory rules:
- **Composition**: Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), Union Minister of State for Finance, and State Finance Ministers from all 28 States and 3 UTs with legislatures.
- **Voting Architecture**:
  - The Central Government holds **one-third (33.33%) of total votes cast**.
  - All State Governments combined hold **two-thirds (66.67%) of total votes cast**.
  - Decision Threshold: A statutory **three-fourths (75%) weighted majority** is required to pass any resolution.
  - This structure enforces genuine cooperative federalism: neither the Center alone nor all States acting collectively without the Center can push through unilateral changes.

### 6.4 Fiscal Federalism: The 15th Finance Commission (Article 280)
The Finance Commission, constituted every five years by the President under Article 280, recommends the distribution of the net proceeds of Union divisible taxes:
- **Vertical Devolution**: Recommended allocating **41% of net divisible tax revenue** to the States (adjusted from 42% under the 14th FC to accommodate the newly formed Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh).
- **Horizontal Devolution Criteria (15th FC)**:
  1. **Income Distance (45%)**: Rewarding poorer states with lower per capita GSDP.
  2. **Population (2011 Census) (15%)**: Reflecting sheer demographic scale.
  3. **Geographical Area (15%)**: Administrative costs of spatial dispersion.
  4. **Forest and Ecology (10%)**: Rewarding states preserving dense forest cover.
  5. **Demographic Performance (12.5%)**: Rewarding states that successfully lowered Total Fertility Rates (TFR) to replacement levels.
  6. **Tax Effort (2.5%)**: Rewarding states demonstrating efficiency in own tax mobilization.

## Unit 7: Economic History, Planning Paradigms & Modern Industrial Policy

### 7.1 Historical Arc: Colonial Drainage to the Planned Era
- **Colonial Deindustrialization (Dadabhai Naoroji's Drain of Wealth)**: British colonial rule systematically destroyed India's indigenous textile and artisan manufacturing, converting the economy into an exporter of primary agricultural raw materials (cotton, indigo, jute) and an importer of British factory manufactures.
- **The Mahalanobis Heavy Industrialization Strategy (Second Five-Year Plan, 1956–61)**: Emphasized state-led investment in capital goods (steel, heavy chemicals, machine building, power) based on the premise that building domestic capital goods capacity would establish long-term economic independence.
- **The "Hindu Rate of Growth" (1950–1980)**: Raj Krishna's term describing India's sluggish average real GDP growth rate of ~3.5% per annum, caused by the rigidities of industrial licensing, import substitution policies, price controls, and bureaucratic inertia.

### 7.2 The 1991 LPG Inflection (Liberalization, Privatization, Globalization)
Triggered by an acute BoP crisis (forex reserves down to barely two weeks of imports), India instituted structural adjustment reforms:
- **Liberalization**: Abolition of industrial licensing (except 5 strategic sectors), dismantling of the MRTP asset ceilings, opening the financial sector to private banks, and deregulation of interest rates.
- **Privatization**: Disinvestment of public sector enterprise equity, elimination of state monopolies, and corporatization of government departments.
- **Globalization**: Reduction of peak customs tariffs from over 300% to competitive levels, adoption of current account convertibility, and opening domestic equity and infrastructure to foreign capital.

### 7.3 Planning Commission vs. NITI Aayog
On January 1, 2015, the Government replaced the 65-year-old Planning Commission with the **National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog)**:

| Institutional Feature | Planning Commission (1950–2014) | NITI Aayog (2015–Present) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Philosophical Model** | Top-down, centralized socialist planning; "one-size-fits-all" | Bottom-up, cooperative and competitive federalism |
| **Financial Powers** | Allocated central plan funds and discretionary state capital grants | Pure advisory think-tank; **no financial allocation powers** |
| **State Representation** | States consulted via the National Development Council (NDC) | State Chief Ministers are permanent members of the Governing Council |
| **Planning Horizon** | Rigid Five-Year Plans with mid-term appraisals | 15-Year Vision, 7-Year Strategy, and 3-Year Action Agendas |

### 7.4 Modern Industrial Revival: PLI and Composite MSME Criteria
To overcome the stagnation of domestic manufacturing (~15% of GDP):
- **Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme**: Offers financial incentives of **4% to 6% on incremental sales** of goods manufactured in domestic units across 14 strategic sectors (electronics, pharmaceuticals, solar PV modules, advanced chemistry cell batteries, specialty steel, automobiles).
- **Revised MSME Composite Classification (2020)**: Replaced separate criteria for manufacturing and services with a unified standard combining **Investment in Plant/Machinery** and **Annual Turnover**:
  - **Micro**: Investment $\le$ ₹1 Crore AND Turnover $\le$ ₹5 Crore.
  - **Small**: Investment $\le$ ₹10 Crore AND Turnover $\le$ ₹50 Crore.
  - **Medium**: Investment $\le$ ₹50 Crore AND Turnover $\le$ ₹250 Crore.
  - *Export turnover is legally excluded when calculating the turnover thresholds.*

## Unit 8: Demographics, Employment Dynamics & Labor Law Codification

### 8.1 The Demographic Dividend and Labor Force Metrics
India is currently experiencing a historic demographic transition with a median age of ~28 years. This demographic window offers maximum economic returns only if the working-age population is productively employed:
- **Labor Force Participation Rate (LFPR)**: The percentage of the working-age population (15 years and older) that is either employed or actively seeking employment:
$$\text{LFPR} = \frac{\text{Employed} + \text{Unemployed}}{\text{Total Working-Age Population}} \times 100$$
- **Worker Population Ratio (WPR)**: The proportion of the population that is actively engaged in economic production:
$$\text{WPR} = \frac{\text{Total Employed Persons}}{\text{Total Population}} \times 100$$
- **Unemployment Rate (UR)**:
$$\text{UR} = \frac{\text{Unemployed Persons}}{\text{Total Labor Force}} \times 100$$

### 8.2 Methodological Approaches: UPS vs. UPSS
The National Statistical Office (NSO) measures employment through the **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS)** using two primary reference periods:
- **Usual Principal Status (UPS)**: Classifies individuals based on their primary economic activity status pursued for the majority of the preceding 365 days (minimum 183 days).
- **Usual Principal and Subsidiary Status (UPSS)**: Expands employment to include anyone who pursued an economic activity for at least 30 days during the year, even if their principal status was unemployed or outside the labor force. UPSS yields higher employment figures by capturing temporary, seasonal, and distress informal labor.

### 8.3 Labor Law Consolidation: The Four Labor Codes
To overcome rigidities that encouraged firms to remain small and informal, Parliament amalgamated 29 central labor enactments into **Four Labor Codes**:
1. **Code on Wages, 2019**: Subsumes Payment of Wages Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Bonus Act, and Equal Remuneration Act. Establishes a statutory **National Floor Wage** binding across all states and universalizes minimum wages to all workers across both formal and informal sectors.
2. **Industrial Relations Code, 2020**: Subsumes Trade Unions Act, Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, and Industrial Disputes Act. Raises the employee threshold for requiring prior government permission before closure, retrenchment, or lay-offs from **100 workers to 300 workers**, providing hiring flexibility while formalizing **Fixed-Term Employment (FTE)**.
3. **Code on Social Security, 2020**: Subsumes EPF Act, ESI Act, Maternity Benefit Act, and Payment of Gratuity Act. Extends social security coverage to informal workers, platform gig workers, and contract laborers funded by aggregator contributions.
4. **Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020**: Consolidates 13 acts regulating factory hours, hazardous environments, safety audits, and interstate migrant worker welfare.

## Unit 9: Agriculture Architecture, Subsidies & Food Supply Chains

### 9.1 Agrarian Structural Disparities and Farm Subsidies
Indian agriculture employs ~45% of the national workforce while contributing under 18% of GDP. Government interventions dominate input markets:
- **Fertilizer Subsidy**: Historically operated through the Retention Price Scheme (RPS) for urea and the **Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS)** for phosphatic and potassic (P&K) fertilizers. Because urea remains under statutory Maximum Retail Prices (MRP) heavily subsidized by the Center, farmers over-apply nitrogen, distorting the optimal N:P:K soil application ratio (ideal 4:2:1; skewed to >8:3:1 in northwestern states).
- **Power and Canal Subsidies**: Free or heavily subsidized agricultural electricity encourages excessive extraction of groundwater via submersible tubewells, causing severe depletion of aquifers across Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan.

### 9.2 Minimum Support Price (MSP) and CACP Cost Concepts
The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommends MSPs for **22 mandated crops** (7 cereals, 5 pulses, 7 oilseeds, and 3 commercial crops) plus the Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) for sugarcane, based on three distinct cost concepts:
- **$A_2$ Cost**: Covers all direct out-of-pocket expenses incurred by the farmer on seeds, fertilizers, pesticides, hired labor, fuel, irrigation, and machinery rental.
- **$A_2 + FL$ Cost**: Adds the imputed economic value of unpaid family labor to the $A_2$ direct cash costs.
- **$C_2$ Cost (Comprehensive Cost)**: The most comprehensive economic measure, adding the imputed rental value of owned agricultural land and the imputed interest on owned fixed capital assets to $A_2 + FL$.

$$\text{Statutory MSP Formula (Post-2018)} = (A_2 + FL) + 50\% \text{ Margin}$$

The M.S. Swaminathan Commission recommended setting MSP at **$C_2 + 50\%$**, a demand consistently advanced by farmer federations to guarantee comprehensive farm profitability.

### 9.3 Supply Chain Architecture: APMC, e-NAM, and Food Processing
- **Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC)**: State-level statutory market yards designed to protect producers from local exploitation. In practice, APMCs became oligopolistic cartels where licensed commission agents (*arhtiyas*) extracted high fees while price discovery remained opaque.
- **Electronic National Agriculture Market (e-NAM)**: A pan-India virtual trading platform networking physical APMC mandis to create a unified national agricultural market with electronic bidding, assaying facilities, and direct online settlement into farmer bank accounts.
- **Pradhan Mantri Kisan SAMPADA Yojana**: Comprehensive supply-chain scheme aimed at creating modern food processing infrastructure:
  - Mega Food Parks with integrated cold chains, testing labs, and processing units.
  - Operation Greens (extending from Tomato, Onion, Potato—TOP—to all 22 perishable crops).

## Unit 10: External Sector, Balance of Payments & Multilateral Governance

### 10.1 Double-Entry Balance of Payments (BoP) Structure
The Balance of Payments records all financial and commercial transactions between domestic residents and the rest of the world within an accounting year:

$$\text{Current Account} + \text{Capital Account} + \text{Errors and Omissions} = \Delta \text{Foreign Exchange Reserves}$$

| BoP Component | Credit (+) / Inflows | Debit (-) / Outflows | Net Balance Driver |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Merchandise Trade** | Merchandise Exports | Merchandise Imports | Chronically in deficit due to crude oil, electronics, and gold imports. |
| **Invisibles: Services** | Software exports, IT services, tourism | Foreign travel, software royalties | Consistently in large surplus, offsetting merchandise trade deficit. |
| **Invisibles: Transfers** | Remittances sent by Indian diaspora abroad | Outward personal gifts, grants | Surplus; India is the world's leading remittance recipient (>$100B). |
| **Invisibles: Income** | Profits, dividends, interest earned abroad | Profits remitted abroad by foreign MNCs | Historically negative net balance. |
| **Capital: FDI** | Direct investments acquiring $\ge 10\%$ equity | Overseas direct investments by Indian firms | Non-debt creating, stable long-term capital inflows. |
| **Capital: FPI** | Portfolio investments in domestic stocks/bonds | Outward equity divestment | Liquid, sentiment-driven "hot money" flows. |
| **Capital: Debt** | ECBs raised abroad, NRI deposits | Loan repayments, debt servicing | Debt-creating liabilities. |

### 10.2 Exchange Rate Dynamics: NEER vs. REER
- **Nominal Effective Exchange Rate (NEER)**: The weighted average of bilateral nominal exchange rates of the Indian Rupee relative to a trade-weighted basket of foreign currencies (RBI maintains 40-currency and 6-currency baskets).
- **Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER)**: Adjusts NEER for domestic inflation differentials relative to trading partners:

$$\text{REER} = \text{NEER} \times \left( \frac{\text{Domestic Price Index}}{\text{Foreign Price Index (Weighted)}} \right)$$

- **REER > 100**: Indicates that domestic prices are rising faster than foreign prices, causing domestic export goods to become uncompetitive (currency overvaluation in real terms).
- **REER < 100**: Indicates that the domestic currency is undervalued in real terms, boosting price competitiveness for merchandise exports.

### 10.3 Multilateral Trade Architecture: India and the WTO
Vivek Singh deconstructs the key WTO agreements governing international trade:
- **Agreement on Agriculture (AoA)**:
  - **Green Box**: Subsidies with minimal or no trade-distorting effects (agricultural research, environmental programs, decoupled income support). Permitted without limits.
  - **Blue Box**: Direct payments under production-limiting programs (subsidies linked to fixed acreage or animal herds). Permitted.
  - **Amber Box**: Trade-distorting market price supports and input subsidies (fertilizer, power, MSP). Subject to a **de minimis threshold of 10% of total agricultural output value** for developing nations.
  - **The Bali Peace Clause (2013)**: Legally shields India's MSP procurement programs for public stockholding from dispute panel challenges, even if total support breaches the 10% amber box cap.
- **Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS)**:
  - India amended its Patents Act in 2005 to transition from process patents to **product patents** in pharmaceuticals and chemicals.
  - **Section 3(d)** prevents "evergreening"—patenting minor modifications or new forms of known chemical substances without proven enhancement of therapeutic efficacy (upheld in the landmark *Novartis* ruling).
  - Sovereign right to issue **Compulsory Licenses (Section 84)** to domestic manufacturers to produce patented life-saving drugs at affordable rates during public health emergencies.
