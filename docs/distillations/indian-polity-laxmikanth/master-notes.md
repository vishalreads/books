## Executive Epistemological Overview

M. Laxmikanth's *Indian Polity (7th Edition)* (McGraw-Hill) is universally recognized by constitutional scholars, civil service commissioners, and UPSC aspirants as the definitive, encyclopedic treatise on the Constitution and political system of India. Spanning 80 chapters, 11 comprehensive thematic parts, and extensive appendices containing statutory tables and landmark constitutional amendments up to the 106th Amendment Act (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam 2023), the work provides an exhaustive structural, statutory, and jurisprudential analysis of the world's longest written democratic constitution.

The treatise is anchored upon a fundamental constitutional paradox:
> How does a nation of continental proportions—possessing immense linguistic, ethnic, religious, and socio-economic diversity, emerging from two centuries of colonial plunder and the trauma of Partition—sustain a resilient democratic republic governed by the Rule of Law, without succumbing to autocracy or Balkanization?

Laxmikanth demonstrates that the survival, stability, and dynamism of the Indian Republic is the direct outcome of an intricate constitutional engineering that balances:
1. **Constitutional Supremacy vs. Parliamentary Sovereignty**: Merging the British doctrine of Parliamentary Sovereignty with the American doctrine of Judicial Supremacy under the overarching aegis of the **Basic Structure Doctrine**.
2. **Asymmetric Federalism with a Unitary Bias**: Structuring a "Union of States" (*Indestructible Union of Destructible States*) capable of mobilizing centralized executive power during national crises while preserving vibrant linguistic and sub-national democratic space.
3. **The Dialectic of Liberty and Social Transformation**: Synthesizing justiciable individual civil liberties (Part III Fundamental Rights) with non-justiciable socialist and egalitarian state mandates (Part IV Directive Principles of State Policy).

## Unit 1: Historical Evolution & The Constituent Assembly Architecture

### 1.1 The Constitutional Pedigree: The Company Rule (1773–1858)
The institutional foundation of modern Indian public administration, judicial hierarchy, and civil governance was established through successive British parliamentary statutes:
- **The Regulating Act of 1773**: The foundational step toward centralized British rule in India.
  - Designated the Governor of Bengal as the **Governor-General of Bengal** (Warren Hastings), creating an executive council of four members to assist him. Subordinated the Governors of Bombay and Madras to the Governor-General of Bengal.
  - Established the **Supreme Court of Judicature at Calcutta (1774)**, comprising one Chief Justice (Sir Elijah Impey) and three other judges.
  - Prohibited servants of the East India Company from engaging in private trade or accepting presents/bribes from natives.
- **Pitt's India Act of 1784**: Established the system of **Double Government**:
  - Separated the commercial and political functions of the Company.
  - Allowed the Court of Directors to manage commercial affairs, while creating a new six-member **Board of Control** appointed by the British Crown to supervise all civil, military, and revenue affairs, officially designating Company territories in India as the *"British possessions in India."*
- **Charter Act of 1833**: The final step toward centralization in British India:
  - Elevated the Governor-General of Bengal to the **Governor-General of India** (Lord William Bentinck), vesting in him all civil and military powers, completely depriving the Governors of Bombay and Madras of their legislative powers.
  - Transformed the East India Company from a commercial trading corporation into a purely administrative body.
  - Added a fourth, non-voting **Law Member** to the Governor-General's Council (Lord Macaulay), who headed the First Law Commission of 1834 that drafted the Indian Penal Code (IPC 1860).
- **Charter Act of 1853**: The legislative separation:
  - Separated for the first time the **legislative and executive functions** of the Governor-General’s Council, adding six new members termed Legislative Councilors, establishing the Indian (Central) Legislative Council operating on Westminster parliamentary procedure.
  - Introduced an **open competition system** for the recruitment of civil servants (Macaulay Committee on the Indian Civil Service appointed in 1854).

### 1.2 The Crown Rule (1858–1947) & The Major Reform Acts
Following the 1857 Revolt (*First War of Independence*), direct administrative sovereignty was assumed by the British Crown:
- **Government of India Act of 1858 (*Act for the Better Government of India*)**:
  - Abolished the East India Company, the Court of Directors, and the Board of Control, ending the Double Government.
  - Created the office of the **Secretary of State for India** (a member of the British Cabinet, assisted by a 15-member advisory Council of India) and replaced the Governor-General with the title of **Viceroy of India** (Lord Canning), who served as the direct representative of the Crown.
- **Indian Councils Act of 1861**:
  - Initiated the process of **decentralization** by restoring legislative powers to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies.
  - Institutionalized the **Portfolio System** introduced by Lord Canning in 1859.
  - Empowered the Viceroy to issue **Ordinances** without the concurrence of the Legislative Council during an emergency (valid for 6 months), establishing the statutory ancestor of Article 123.
- **Morley-Minto Reforms (Indian Councils Act of 1909)**:
  - Introduced communal representation for Muslims through **Separate Electorates**, legally embedding the seed of political communalism (Lord Minto became known as the *"Father of Communal Electorates"*).
  - Provided for the association of Indians with the executive councils of the Viceroy and Governors (Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council as Law Member).
- **Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms (Government of India Act of 1919)**:
  - Introduced **Dyarchy** (*rule of two*) in the provinces: divided provincial subjects into **Transferred Subjects** (administered by the Governor with the advice of ministers responsible to the Legislative Council, e.g., education, health) and **Reserved Subjects** (administered by the Governor and his executive council without legislative accountability, e.g., police, revenue, justice).
  - Introduced **Bicameralism** (Council of State and Legislative Assembly) and direct elections at the Centre.
  - Extended separate electorates to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans, and established a **Public Service Commission** (Central PSC set up in 1926).
- **Government of India Act of 1935**: The primary structural blueprint of the 1950 Constitution:
  - Envisaged an **All-India Federation** consisting of provinces and princely states (never materialized as princely states refused to join).
  - Divided powers between the Centre and units into three lists: **Federal List** (59 items), **Provincial List** (54 items), and **Concurrent List** (36 items), vesting **Residuary Powers** in the Viceroy.
  - Abolished provincial dyarchy, introducing **Provincial Autonomy**; introduced dyarchy at the Centre (never implemented).
  - Established the **Federal Court** (set up in 1937, forerunner to the Supreme Court of India), the **Reserve Bank of India (RBI 1935)**, and Provincial Public Service Commissions.

### 1.3 The Making of the Constitution: The Constituent Assembly (1946–1949)
The Constituent Assembly of India was constituted in **November 1946** under the framework formulated by the **Cabinet Mission Plan** (comprising Lord Pethick-Lawrence, Sir Stafford Cripps, and A.V. Alexander):
- **Total Membership**: 389 seats originally (296 allocated to British India, 93 to Princely States). Following the Partition under the Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947), the membership shrank to **299**.
- **Election Mechanism**: Indirect election by the members of the Provincial Legislative Assemblies using the system of proportional representation by means of single transferable vote. Princely state representatives were nominated by the heads of the princely states. The Assembly was partly elected and partly nominated.
- **First Meeting**: **December 9, 1946**, attended by 211 members (Muslim League boycotted). **Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha**, the oldest member, was elected temporary Chairman following French practice. On December 11, 1946, **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** was elected President and **H.C. Mukherjee** and **V.T. Krishnamachari** were elected Vice-Presidents. **Sir B.N. Rau** was appointed Constitutional Advisor.
- **The Objective Resolution**: Moved by **Jawaharlal Nehru** on **December 13, 1946** (unanimously adopted on January 22, 1947). It laid down the fundamental philosophy and guiding principles that formed the bedrock of the Constitution, later inspiring the Preamble.
- **The Drafting Committee**: Constituted on **August 29, 1947**, chaired by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** (*Father of the Constitution of India* / *Modern Manu*), comprising 7 members: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswamy Iyer, Dr. K.M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, N. Madhava Rau (replaced B.L. Mitter), and T.T. Krishnamachari (replaced D.P. Khaitan).
- **Enactment and Adoption**: The Constitution was adopted on **November 26, 1949** (celebrated as *Constitution Day* / *Samvidhan Divas*), containing a Preamble, 395 Articles, and 8 Schedules. Certain provisions (Citizenship, Elections, Provisional Parliament) came into force immediately on November 26, 1949; the remaining major provisions came into full enforcement on **January 26, 1950** (*Republic Day*), commemorating the historic declaration of *Purna Swaraj* at the Lahore Session of the Indian National Congress (December 1929).

## Unit 2: The Preamble, Union Territory & Citizenship Framework

### 2.1 The Preamble: The Philosophical Manifesto
The Preamble embodies the summary, essence, and soul of the Constitution. N.A. Palkhivala, the eminent jurist, termed the Preamble the *"Identity Card of the Constitution."*

```
                    THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE PREAMBLE
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 1. SOURCE OF AUTHORITY: "We, the People of India"               │
   ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │ 2. NATURE OF INDIAN POLITY:                                     │
   │    • SOVEREIGN (Free from external control, internal supremacy)  │
   │    • SOCIALIST (Democratic Socialism / mixed economy, not State) │
   │    • SECULAR (Positive secularism: equal respect to all faiths) │
   │    • DEMOCRATIC (Representative parliamentary democracy)         │
   │    • REPUBLIC (Elected head of state; no hereditary monarch)    │
   ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │ 3. OBJECTIVES OF THE CONSTITUTION:                              │
   │    • JUSTICE (Social, Economic, and Political)                  │
   │    • LIBERTY (Thought, Expression, Belief, Faith, and Worship)   │
   │    • EQUALITY (Status and Opportunity; civic and political)     │
   │    • FRATERNITY (Assuring Dignity of the Individual & Unity)    │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### The Great Judicial Preamble Triad:
1. **Berubari Union Case (1960)**: The Supreme Court held that the Preamble is *not* a part of the Constitution, functioning merely as a key to open the minds of the makers.
2. **Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973)**: The 13-judge Constitutional Bench reversed the Berubari ruling, holding unequivocally that the **Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution** and can be amended under Article 368, provided the amendment does not damage or destroy the *Basic Structure*.
3. **LIC of India Case (1995)**: Re-affirmed that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution, but is **non-justiciable** (its provisions are not directly enforceable in courts of law).
- **The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act (1976)**: Amended the Preamble for the first and only time in history, inserting three new words: **"Socialist"**, **"Secular"**, and **"Integrity"**.

### 2.2 Union and Its Territory (Articles 1 to 4)
- **Article 1**: Declares that *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."* Dr. Ambedkar clarified two crucial rationales for using "Union" rather than "Federation":
  1. The Indian Federation is not the result of an agreement among the states (unlike the American federation).
  2. The states have no right to secede from the federation. The federation is an indestructible Union.
- **Article 2**: Empowers Parliament to admit into the Union of States, or establish, new states on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit (deals with admission of territories that were *not* part of India).
- **Article 3**: Authorizes Parliament to reorganize internal states:
  - Form a new state by separation, union, or altering boundaries; increase or diminish the area of any state; alter the name of any state.
  - **Procedural Safeguards**: A bill contemplating such change can be introduced only on the prior recommendation of the President. The President must refer the bill to the concerned state legislature for expressing views within a specified period; however, **Parliament is not bound by the views of the state legislature**.
  - Hence, India is characterized as an **"Indestructible Union of Destructible States"** (in sharp contrast to the US, which is an *"Indestructible Union of Indestructible States"*).
- **Article 4**: Declares that laws made under Articles 2 and 3 are not considered constitutional amendments under Article 368 and can be passed by a **simple majority**.

### 2.3 Citizenship Architecture (Part II, Articles 5 to 11)
Unlike federal systems like the United States that feature dual citizenship (National and State), the Indian Constitution provides for **Single Citizenship** to foster national unity.
- **Constitutional Categories at Inception (Articles 5–8)**: Citizenship by domicile (Art 5), citizenship of migrants from Pakistan (Art 6), citizenship of migrants who returned to Pakistan (Art 7), and citizenship of persons of Indian origin residing abroad (Art 8).
- **Article 9**: Termination of Indian citizenship automatically upon voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.
- **Article 11**: Empowers Parliament with plenary authority to regulate the right of citizenship by law.
- **The Citizenship Act of 1955**: Provides five modes of acquisition (Birth, Descent, Registration, Naturalization, and Incorporation of Territory) and three modes of loss (Renunciation, Termination, and Deprivation).
- **Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2019 (CAA)**: Granted eligibility for expedited naturalization to non-Muslim persecuted minorities (Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Christians) from Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan who entered India on or before December 31, 2014.

## Unit 3: Fundamental Rights & The Matrix of Judicial Enforceability (Part III)

### 3.1 Structural Philosophy of Part III (Articles 12 to 35)
Part III of the Constitution is described as the **Magna Carta of India**. Fundamental Rights are justiciable guarantees protecting individual liberty against authoritarian state encroachment.
- **Article 12 (Definition of "State")**: Defines "State" to include: 1. Government and Parliament of India; 2. Government and Legislature of States; 3. All local authorities (Municipalities, Panchayats, District Boards); 4. All other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.
  - In *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981)*, the Supreme Court laid down the test for determining whether a statutory corporation or registered society is an agency/instrumentality of the State (e.g., LIC, ONGC, SAIL qualify as "State" under Article 12).
- **Article 13 (Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights)**:
  - **Article 13(1)**: Pre-constitutional laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of such inconsistency (**Doctrine of Severability** and **Doctrine of Eclipse**).
  - **Article 13(2)**: The State shall not make any post-constitutional law that takes away or abridges Fundamental Rights; any law made in contravention shall be void to the extent of contravention.
  - The definition of "law" includes Acts, ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, and customs.

### 3.2 The Right to Equality (Articles 14 to 18)
- **Article 14**: Guarantee of **Equality before the Law** (British negative concept: absence of arbitrary privilege) and **Equal Protection of the Laws** (American positive concept: equality of treatment in equal circumstances). Permits *reasonable classification* based on an intelligible differentia with a rational nexus to the objective sought, while outlawing class legislation. In *E.P. Royappa (1974)*, the SC expanded Art 14 as an antithesis to arbitrariness.
- **Article 15**: Prohibits discrimination against any citizen on grounds *only* of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
  - *Article 15(4) & 15(5)*: Enable special provisions and affirmative action reservations for socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs/OBCs), SCs, and STs in educational institutions.
  - *Article 15(6)* (inserted by 103rd CAA 2019): Provides up to 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS).
- **Article 16**: Equality of opportunity in matters of public employment.
  - *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) [Mandal Case]*: Upheld 27% OBC reservation in central services, ruled that total reservations must not exceed the **50% ceiling** (except in extraordinary situations), excluded the "Creamy Layer," and declared reservations in promotions unconstitutional (subsequently addressed by 77th, 81st, 85th CAAs).
  - *Article 16(6)* (103rd CAA): 10% reservation for EWS in government jobs.
- **Article 17**: Abolition of **Untouchability** and prohibition of its practice in any form. Enforced through the *Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955* and *Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989*.
- **Article 18**: Abolition of all feudal titles (e.g., Rai Bahadur, Maharaja), while permitting military and academic distinctions.

### 3.3 The Six Freedoms of Article 19
Article 19 guarantees six basic democratic freedoms to **citizens only**, subject to specified "reasonable restrictions":
1. **Article 19(1)(a)**: Freedom of speech and expression (includes freedom of the press, right against phone tapping, right to broadcast, right to remain silent). Reasonable restrictions under Art 19(2): sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence.
2. **Article 19(1)(b)**: Freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms (subject to public order under Art 19(3)).
3. **Article 19(1)(c)**: Freedom to form associations, unions, or co-operative societies (co-operatives added by 97th CAA 2011).
4. **Article 19(1)(d)**: Freedom to move freely throughout the territory of India (restricted for protection of general public and interests of Scheduled Tribes).
5. **Article 19(1)(e)**: Freedom to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India.
6. **Article 19(1)(g)**: Freedom to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business. *(Note: Article 19(1)(f) - Right to Property - was deleted by 44th CAA 1978).*

### 3.4 Protection of Life and Personal Liberty (Articles 20 to 22)
- **Article 20**: Protection in respect of conviction for offences:
  - *Art 20(1)*: Protection against **Ex-post Facto laws** (retrospective criminal legislation).
  - *Art 20(2)*: Protection against **Double Jeopardy** (no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once).
  - *Art 20(3)*: Protection against **Self-Incrimination** (no accused shall be compelled to be a witness against himself; *Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010)* banned involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph, and brain-mapping).
- **Article 21**: *"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law."*
  - **A.K. Gopalan Case (1950)**: Strict, narrow literal interpretation; procedural compliance alone was sufficient.
  - **Maneka Gandhi Case (1978)**: Watershed transformation! The Supreme Court read the American concept of **"Due Process of Law"** into Article 21. A law depriving a person of life or liberty must not merely be procedural; it must be **"just, fair, and reasonable."**
  - **The Proliferation of Rights under Art 21**: Right to clean environment (*Subhash Kumar*), Right to livelihood (*Olga Tellis*), Right to speedy trial, Right to free legal aid, and the landmark **Right to Privacy** (*K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India 2017*).
- **Article 21A**: **Right to Education** (inserted by 86th CAA 2002): Free and compulsory education for all children between ages 6 and 14, operationalized by the *Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009*.
- **Article 22**: Protection against arbitrary arrest and detention (provides right to be informed of grounds, right to consult lawyer, and production before magistrate within 24 hours). Also governs **Preventive Detention** laws.

### 3.5 Right Against Exploitation, Religious Freedoms & Cultural Rights
- **Article 23**: Prohibits trafficking in human beings, *begar* (forced labor), and involuntary servitude.
- **Article 24**: Prohibits employment of children below 14 years in factories, mines, or hazardous occupations (*Child and Adolescent Labour Prohibition Act*).
- **Articles 25 to 28 (Freedom of Religion)**: Individual freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion (Art 25); collective freedom to manage religious affairs (Art 26); freedom from payment of taxes for promotion of any particular religion (Art 27); freedom from attending religious instruction in wholly state-funded educational institutions (Art 28).
- **Articles 29 & 30 (Cultural and Educational Rights)**: Protection of distinct language, script, or culture of any section of citizens (Art 29); fundamental right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice (Art 30).
- **Article 31**: Right to Property—repealed as a Fundamental Right by the **44th Amendment Act 1978** and re-enacted as a non-fundamental legal/constitutional right under **Article 300A** in Part XII.

### 3.6 The Heart and Soul: Article 32 & The Five Prerogative Writs
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar famously proclaimed Article 32 as:
> *"The very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it... an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity."*

Article 32 guarantees the fundamental right to approach the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of Part III rights. The Supreme Court (Art 32) and High Courts (Art 226) are empowered to issue **Five Prerogative Writs**:

| Writ | Latin Translation | Definition & Purpose | Issued Against |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Habeas Corpus** | *"To have the body of"* | Orders the producing authority to bring a detained person before the court to inspect the legality of detention; releases person if detention is unlawful. | Public authorities AND private individuals. |
| **Mandamus** | *"We command"* | Directs a public official, inferior court, or corporation to perform a mandatory public or statutory duty that they have refused or failed to execute. | Public officials, statutory bodies, tribunals (Cannot be issued against President or Governor). |
| **Prohibition** | *"To forbid"* | Issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping jurisdiction it does not possess. | Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies only (preventive). |
| **Certiorari** | *"To be certified"* | Quashes an illegal order passed by a lower court, tribunal, or administrative authority that acted without or in excess of jurisdiction or violated Natural Justice. | Judicial, quasi-judicial, AND administrative authorities (curative & preventive). |
| **Quo-Warranto** | *"By what authority"* | Inquires into the legality of a person's claim to a public office; ousts usurpers from public statutory offices. Can be sought by any citizen, not only an aggrieved person. | Substantive public offices created by statute or Constitution. |

## Unit 4: Directive Principles, Fundamental Duties & The Basic Structure Doctrine

### 4.1 Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36 to 51)
Borrowed from the **Irish Constitution of 1937** (which had derived them from the Spanish Constitution), the DPSPs constitute the moral, social, and economic charter of the Indian Republic. Dr. Ambedkar called them **"Novel Features"** designed to establish **Economic and Social Democracy**, transforming India into a **Welfare State** rather than a colonial police state.
- **Article 37**: Declares that Directive Principles are **non-justiciable** (cannot be directly enforced by courts of law), but are nevertheless **"fundamental in the governance of the country and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws."**

#### The Threefold Classification of DPSPs:
1. **Socialistic Principles**: Articles 38 (Promote welfare of people, minimize inequalities), 39 (Equitable distribution of material resources, prevention of concentration of wealth, equal pay for equal work), 39A (Free legal aid to the poor, added by 42nd CAA), 41 (Right to work, education, public assistance in unemployment/old age), 42 (Just and humane conditions of work, maternity relief), 43 (Living wage and decent standard of life for workers), 43A (Workers' participation in management).
2. **Gandhian Principles**: Articles 40 (Organize village panchayats as units of self-government), 43 (Promote cottage industries), 43B (Promotion of autonomous co-operative societies, added by 97th CAA 2011), 46 (Promote educational and economic interests of SCs, STs, and weaker sections), 47 (Prohibit intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health), 48 (Prohibit slaughter of cows, calves, and milch cattle; organize modern agriculture/husbandry).
3. **Liberal-Intellectual Principles**: Articles 44 (Secure a **Uniform Civil Code** throughout the territory of India), 45 (Early childhood care and education below age 6), 48A (Protect and improve environment, forests, wildlife, added by 42nd CAA), 49 (Protect national monuments and historic sites), 50 (Separation of Judiciary from Executive in public services), 51 (Promotion of international peace, security, and treaty obligations).

### 4.2 The Historic FR vs. DPSP Jurisprudential Dialectic
The constitutional tension between fundamental individual rights (Part III) and collective socialist mandates (Part IV) evolved across five historic Supreme Court milestones:
1. **Champakam Dorairajan Case (1951)**: The SC held that Fundamental Rights are sacrosanct and DPSPs must run subsidiary to them; in case of conflict, Fundamental Rights prevail.
2. **Golaknath Case (1967)**: The SC ruled that Parliament cannot amend Part III to dilute Fundamental Rights, even to implement Directive Principles.
3. **The 25th Constitutional Amendment Act (1971)**: Inserted **Article 31C**, providing that laws enacted to implement Article 39(b) and 39(c) (wealth distribution) cannot be challenged for violating Articles 14, 19, or 31.
4. **Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973)**: Upheld the first part of Article 31C, asserting that laws securing Article 39(b) and (c) can supersede Articles 14 and 19.
5. **Minerva Mills Case (1980)**: Struck down the 42nd Amendment’s attempt to give all DPSPs precedence over Fundamental Rights. Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud penned the immortal constitutional principle:
   > *"The Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV. To give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution. They are like the two wheels of a chariot."*

### 4.3 Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)
The original 1950 Constitution did not contain Fundamental Duties. 
- In **1976**, the **Swaran Singh Committee** recommended incorporating duties to instill civic discipline during the Emergency.
- The **42nd Amendment Act (1976)** inserted Part IVA containing a single Article: **Article 51A**, specifying **10 Fundamental Duties** (borrowed from the **USSR Constitution**).
- The **86th Amendment Act (2002)** added the **11th Duty** under Article 51A(k): the duty of every parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to their child between ages 6 and 14.
- **The Justice J.S. Verma Committee (1999)**: Identified existing legal statutes that operationalize fundamental duties (Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971, Unlawful Activities Prevention Act, Wildlife Protection Act 1972, Forest Conservation Act 1980). Duties are non-justiciable directly, but courts use them to interpret the constitutionality of legislative statutes.

### 4.4 The Emergence of the Basic Structure Doctrine
The evolution of the amending power under **Article 368** represents the epic struggle over constitutional supremacy:

$$	ext{Shankari Prasad (1951)} ightarrow 	ext{Sajjan Singh (1965)} ightarrow 	ext{Golaknath (1967)} ightarrow 	ext{Kesavananda Bharati (1973)}$$

- **Shankari Prasad (1951)** & **Sajjan Singh (1965)**: Upheld Parliament's absolute power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Part III, holding that an amendment under Art 368 is not ordinary "law" within the meaning of Art 13.
- **Golaknath (1967)**: An 11-judge bench ruled 6:5 that an amendment under Art 368 *is* a "law" under Art 13(2); Parliament had no power to abridge Fundamental Rights.
- **The 24th Amendment Act (1971)**: Parliament amended Articles 13 and 368, expressly asserting that nothing in Article 13 applies to amendments under Article 368.
- **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (April 24, 1973)**: A historic 13-judge bench, by a 7:6 majority, delivered the seminal verdict of Indian jurisprudence:
  - Upheld the validity of the 24th Amendment and Parliament’s power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
  - **The Inviolable Limitation**: Parliament's amending power is **constituent, but not unlimited**. Parliament cannot alter, damage, or destroy the **"Basic Structure" (Basic Features)** of the Constitution!
- **Core Elements of Basic Structure**: Supremacy of the Constitution, Republican and Democratic form of government, Secular character, Separation of Powers, Federalism, Rule of Law, Judicial Review, Independence of the Judiciary, Free and Fair Elections, and the Balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs (*Indira Nehru Gandhi 1975, Minerva Mills 1980, S.R. Bommai 1994, I.R. Coelho 2007*).

## Unit 5: System of Government: Asymmetric Federalism & Emergency Governance

### 5.1 Parliamentary vs. Presidential Government
The framers preferred the British **Parliamentary (Cabinet) System** over the American Presidential model for two overriding reasons: 1. Familiarity with the system operating under British colonial rule; 2. The imperative of **executive accountability over stability**. In the words of Dr. Ambedkar:
> *"The American Presidential system gives more stability but less responsibility. The British Parliamentary system gives more responsibility but less stability. The Draft Constitution has preferred the system of more responsibility to more stability."*

- **Core Principles of Parliamentary Governance**:
  - Dual Executive: De jure nominal executive (President) vs. De facto real executive (Prime Minister).
  - Majority Party Rule: Political party with a Lok Sabha majority forms the government.
  - **Collective Responsibility**: The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Article 75). If a No-Confidence Motion passes, the entire ministry resigns.
  - Political Homogeneity & Double Membership (Ministers must be members of Parliament).
  - Secrecy of Procedure and Dissolution of the Lower House by the President on Prime Ministerial advice.

### 5.2 Federalism with a Unitary Bias (Quasi-Federalism)
The Indian Constitution does not use the word "Federation." Constitutional scholar **K.C. Wheare** famously defined the Indian system as **"Quasi-Federal"**—a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features.

```
                    THE INDIAN FEDERAL EQUILIBRIUM
   ┌───────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┐
   │ FEDERAL FEATURES                  │ UNITARY BIAS / CENTRALIZED CORE   │
   ├───────────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
   │ • Dual Polity (Centre & States)   │ • Single Constitution for both    │
   │ • Written Constitution            │ • Single Integrated Judiciary     │
   │ • Division of Powers (7th Sched)  │ • Single Citizenship              │
   │ • Supremacy of Constitution       │ • Flexibility of Constitution     │
   │ • Rigidity of Amending Powers     │ • Centre Appoints State Governors │
   │ • Independent Judiciary           │ • All-India Services (IAS, IPS)   │
```

### 5.3 Centre-State Relations (Part XI & XII, Articles 245 to 293)
- **Legislative Relations (Articles 245–255)**:
  - Territorial jurisdiction: Parliament can make laws for whole or any part of India (and has extra-territorial legislation powers); State legislatures legislate for state territory only.
  - Distribution of Legislative Subjects (Seventh Schedule): Union List (originally 97, now 100 items), State List (originally 66, now 61 items), Concurrent List (originally 47, now 52 items; 42nd CAA transferred education, forests, weights/measures, wildlife, and justice administration from State to Concurrent List).
  - **Residuary Powers (Article 248)**: Vested exclusively in Parliament (unlike US and Australia, where residuary powers belong to the States).
  - **Parliamentary Legislation on State List**: Parliament can legislate on State subjects under five extraordinary circumstances: 1. When Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by 2/3rd majority under **Article 249**; 2. During a National Emergency under **Article 250**; 3. When two or more States pass resolutions requesting Parliament under **Article 252**; 4. To implement International Treaties under **Article 253**; 5. During President's Rule under **Article 356**.
- **Administrative Relations (Articles 256–263)**: Executive power of states must ensure compliance with parliamentary laws (Art 256). Centre can give executive directions to states regarding communication, railways, and mother-tongue education (Art 257).
  - **Inter-State Council (Article 263)**: Established by Presidential order to investigate and advise on inter-state disputes (Sarkaria Commission recommendation; established 1990).
  - **Inter-State River Water Disputes (Article 262)**: Parliament can by law provide for adjudication of river disputes and bar the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (*Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956*).
- **Financial Relations (Articles 264–293)**: Allocation of taxing powers; revenue distribution through the **Finance Commission (Article 280)**; the **Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council (Article 279A)** introduced by the 101st Amendment Act 2016.
- **Commissions on Centre-State Relations**:
  - **Sarkaria Commission (1983–1988)**: Recommended rare use of Article 356, strengthening Inter-State Council, consultation with Chief Ministers before appointing Governors, and maintaining All-India Services.
  - **Punchhi Commission (2007–2010)**: Recommended localized emergency provisions, fixed 5-year tenure for Governors with impeachment procedure analogous to the President, and limiting Governor's discretion in government formation.

### 5.4 Part XVIII Emergency Provisions (Articles 352 to 360)
The Emergency provisions transform the federal character of India into a unitary system without formal constitutional amendment:
1. **National Emergency (Article 352)**:
   - *Grounds*: War, External Aggression (*External Emergency*), or Armed Rebellion (*Internal Emergency*). (The term "Armed Rebellion" replaced "Internal Disturbance" via the 44th CAA 1978).
   - *Procedure*: The President can proclaim emergency only upon the **written recommendation of the Union Cabinet** (Prime Minister plus Cabinet Ministers). Must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within **one month** by a **special majority** (majority of total membership + 2/3rd of members present and voting). Can continue indefinitely with 6-month parliamentary re-approvals.
   - *Effects*: Centre can issue executive directions to states on any matter; Parliament can legislate on State subjects; fundamental freedoms under Article 19 are suspended automatically under **Article 358** (only during War or External Aggression). Under **Article 359**, the President can suspend the right to move court for enforcement of other Fundamental Rights, **except Articles 20 and 21** (which can never be suspended, 44th CAA).
2. **President’s Rule / State Emergency (Articles 356 & 365)**:
   - *Grounds*: Failure of constitutional machinery in a State (Art 356) or failure of a State to comply with Central directions (Art 365).
   - *Approval*: Must be approved within **two months** by a **simple majority**. Valid for 6 months, maximum up to 3 years (beyond 1 year requires Election Commission certification of election difficulties and existing National Emergency).
   - *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)*: Historic 9-judge bench ruled that Presidential proclamation under Article 356 is subject to **Judicial Review**. The assembly cannot be dissolved until Parliament approves the proclamation; secularism is basic structure; floor test is mandatory.
3. **Financial Emergency (Article 360)**:
   - *Grounds*: Threat to financial stability or credit of India. Approved within two months by simple majority; continues indefinitely until revoked. Salaries of all public officials, including Supreme Court and High Court judges, can be reduced. *(Has never been declared in India)*.

## Unit 6: The Union Executive & Parliament: Legislative, Financial & Oversight Mechanics

### 6.1 The President of India (Articles 52 to 62)
The President is the head of the Indian State, the first citizen, and the symbol of unity, integrity, and solidarity of the nation.
- **Election (Article 54 & 55)**: Indirect election by an **Electoral College** consisting of: 1. Elected members of both Houses of Parliament; 2. Elected members of Legislative Assemblies of States; 3. Elected members of Legislative Assemblies of UTs of Delhi, Puducherry (70th CAA), and Jammu & Kashmir. Nominated members and Legislative Council members do not vote.
  - Employs proportional representation by means of single transferable vote with secret ballot. Value of MLA vote is determined by state population; aggregate MLA vote value equals aggregate MP vote value.
- **Impeachment (Article 61)**: For "violation of the Constitution" (phrase undefined). Charges can be initiated in either House (requires 1/4th signatures, 14 days notice), passed by a majority of **not less than two-thirds of the total membership** of the House. Investigating House passes it by two-thirds of total membership, whereupon the President stands removed.
- **Pardoning Power (Article 72)**: Executive clemency to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, and remissions, or suspend, remit, or commute sentences: 1. In all cases of punishment by Court Martial; 2. In all cases under Union laws; 3. In all cases of death sentence. (*Kehar Singh Case 1989*: Pardoning power is subject to limited judicial review against arbitrariness or mala fide intent).
- **Ordinance-Making Power (Article 123)**:
  - President can promulgate Ordinances when either House of Parliament is not in session and circumstances require immediate action.
  - Ordinances have the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but are temporary: must be laid before Parliament within **six weeks from reassembly**, or cease to operate. Cannot amend the Constitution or abridge Fundamental Rights (*D.C. Wadhwa 1987* & *Krishna Kumar Singh 2017*: Re-promulgation of ordinances without legislative placement is an unconstitutional subversion of the democratic process).

### 6.2 The Prime Minister & Council of Ministers (Articles 74 & 75)
- **Article 74(1)**: There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to **aid and advise** the President, who *shall*, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice. (The 42nd CAA made advice binding; the 44th CAA gave the President the power to return advice once for reconsideration, after which the President must accept reconsidered advice).
- **Article 75**: Prime Minister is appointed by President; other ministers appointed by President on PM's advice. Total number of ministers shall not exceed **15% of the total strength of the Lok Sabha** (91st CAA 2003). Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**.

### 6.3 Parliament: Bicameral Architecture & Legislative Procedure
- **Composition**: **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States, max 250 members: 238 elected from States/UTs, 12 nominated by President for literature, science, art, social service; permanent body, 1/3rd retire every 2 years); **Lok Sabha** (House of the People, max 550 members: 530 from States, 20 from UTs; Anglo-Indian nomination discontinued by 104th CAA 2019).
- **Types of Bills**:
  1. **Ordinary Bills**: Introduced in either House; disagreement resolved via **Joint Sitting (Article 108)** summoned by President, presided over by Lok Sabha Speaker, decided by simple majority.
  2. **Money Bills (Article 110)**: Can be introduced **only in Lok Sabha** and only on the recommendation of the President. Speaker has sole final authority to certify a Money Bill. Rajya Sabha cannot reject or amend; must return within **14 days**, otherwise deemed passed. No provision for a Joint Sitting.
  3. **Financial Bills (Type I - Art 117(1) & Type II - Art 117(3))**: Blend features of Money Bills and Ordinary Bills.
  4. **Constitutional Amendment Bills (Article 368)**: Passed by each House separately by a special majority (majority of total membership + 2/3rd of members present and voting). No provision for a Joint Sitting. If amending federal provisions, requires ratification by at least half of the State legislatures by simple majority. President *must* give assent (24th CAA 1971).

### 6.4 The Budgetary Cycle & Parliamentary Financial Control
- **Annual Financial Statement (Article 112)**: The formal constitutional term for the "Budget," presented by the Finance Minister.
- **Demands for Grants & Cut Motions**:
  - *Disapproval of Policy Cut*: Demand reduced to ₹1 (expresses disapproval of underlying policy).
  - *Economy Cut*: Demand reduced by a specified amount.
  - *Token Cut*: Demand reduced by ₹100 (ventilates a specific grievance).
- **Appropriation Bill (Article 114)**: No money can be withdrawn from the **Consolidated Fund of India** except under appropriation made by law.
- **Finance Bill**: Enacts the taxation and revenue proposals of the Government.
- **The Guillotine**: On the last allotted day of discussion on Demands for Grants, the Speaker puts all remaining undiscussed demands to vote immediately, terminating debate.

### 6.5 Parliamentary Committees: Watchdogs of the Executive
Parliament exercises continuous, non-partisan oversight through Specialized Standing Committees:
1. **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)**: Established in 1921 under GOI Act 1919. Comprises 22 members (15 Lok Sabha, 7 Rajya Sabha) elected by proportional representation. Examines the appropriation and financial audit reports of the CAG. By convention since 1967, the Chairman is drawn from the **Opposition**.
2. **Estimates Committee**: First constituted in 1950 on John Mathai's recommendation. Comprises **30 members, all drawn exclusively from Lok Sabha**. Examines estimates to suggest economies and administrative improvements ("Continuous Economy Committee").
3. **Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)**: Formed in 1964 on Krishna Menon's recommendation. 22 members (15 LS, 7 RS). Examines reports and accounts of public sector enterprises and CAG reports on PSEs.

## Unit 7: The Integrated Judicial System: Supreme Court, High Courts & Judicial Activism

### 7.1 The Supreme Court of India (Articles 124 to 147)
Unlike the dual court system in the United States, India maintains a **single integrated judicial system** administering both Union and State laws, with the Supreme Court at the apex:
- **Appointment of Judges & The Collegium System**:
  - *First Judges Case (1981)*: Ruled that "consultation" under Art 124 does not mean "concurrence"; executive primacy in judicial appointments upheld.
  - *Second Judges Case (1993)*: Overturned First Judges Case; ruled that "consultation" means **"concurrence."** Established the **Collegium System** (Chief Justice of India + 2 senior-most SC judges).
  - *Third Judges Case (1998)*: Expanded the Collegium to the **CJI and 4 senior-most Supreme Court judges**.
  - *Fourth Judges Case (2015)*: Struck down the **99th Constitutional Amendment Act 2014** and the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act**, declaring executive participation in judicial selection a violation of the **Independence of the Judiciary**, a Basic Feature of the Constitution.

### 7.2 Jurisdictional Spectrum of the Supreme Court
1. **Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)**: Exclusive federal dispute adjudication between Centre vs. One or More States, or State vs. State.
2. **Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32)**: Original but not exclusive (shared with High Courts under Art 226) for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
3. **Appellate Jurisdiction (Articles 132 to 134A)**: Constitutional, civil, and criminal appeals from High Courts via certificates of fitness.
4. **Special Leave Petition (SLP, Article 136)**: Extraordinary discretionary power of the Supreme Court to grant special leave to appeal against any judgment, decree, or order passed by any court or tribunal in India (except military tribunals).
5. **Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)**: The President can seek the opinion of the Supreme Court on questions of law or fact of public importance (SC may refuse to advise on post-constitutional matters, but must advise on pre-constitutional treaties). The opinion is advisory and not binding on the President.
6. **Curative Petition**: Evolved in *Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002)* to prevent abuse of process and gross miscarriage of justice after a review petition is dismissed.
7. **Court of Record (Article 129)**: Decisions have evidentiary value and cannot be questioned; power to punish for Contempt of Court (civil and criminal contempt under *Contempt of Courts Act 1971*).
8. **Complete Justice (Article 142)**: Extraordinary constitutional power empowering the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order necessary for doing **"complete justice"** in any cause or matter pending before it (used in Union Carbide, Ayodhya title suit, coal block cancellations).

## Unit 8: State Government, Special Provisions & Local Self-Government (73rd & 74th CAAs)

### 8.1 The State Executive: The Governor's Dual Constitutional Role
Articles 153 to 167 govern the State Executive, mirroring the Union system with a crucial difference regarding the **Governor**:
- The Governor is not elected; appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal (Article 155), holding office **during the pleasure of the President (Article 156)**.
- **The Dual Role**: 1. Constitutional Head of the State; 2. Agent of the Central Government.
- **Discretionary Powers (Article 163)**: While the President has virtually no explicit constitutional discretion, Article 163(1) explicitly recognizes the **discretionary powers of the Governor**. If any question arises whether a matter falls within the Governor's discretion, the Governor's decision is final and cannot be called into question.
- **Reservation of Bills (Article 200 & 201)**: The Governor can reserve a state legislative bill for the consideration of the President (mandatory if the bill endangers the constitutional position of the State High Court). The President can direct the Governor to return the bill; if re-passed, the President is *not* bound to give assent (unlike the Governor under Art 200).

### 8.2 Asymmetric Federalism & Special Provisions (Articles 371 to 371-J)
Part XXI provides tailored asymmetric governance guarantees to accommodate historical treaty obligations, tribal customs, and regional economic imbalances:
- **Articles 371 to 371-J**: Maharashtra and Gujarat (371), Nagaland (371A: religious/social practices, customary law, ownership of land/resources protected from Parliamentary acts unless state assembly consents), Assam (371B), Manipur (371C), Andhra Pradesh/Telangana (371D & 371E), Sikkim (371F), Mizoram (371G), Arunachal Pradesh (371H), Goa (371-I), Karnataka (371J: Hyderabad-Karnataka region development board).
- **Fifth Schedule**: Administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in 10 states; Governor submits annual reports and can direct that central/state laws do not apply to scheduled areas; establishes Tribes Advisory Councils.
- **Sixth Schedule**: Administration of Tribal Areas in four northeastern states (**AMTM**: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram) through autonomous **District Councils (ADCs)** possessing legislative, judicial, and financial taxation autonomy.

### 8.3 The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act (1992): Panchayati Raj
Democratizing local self-government operationalized Mahatma Gandhi's vision of *Gram Swaraj*:
- **Historical Committees**: Balwant Rai Mehta (1957 - 3-tier Panchayati Raj), Ashok Mehta (1977 - 2-tier system, Mandal Panchayats), G.V.K. Rao (1985 - "grass without roots"), L.M. Singhvi (1986 - recommended constitutional recognition of Panchayats).
- **The 73rd CAA 1992**: Added **Part IX** (Articles 243 to 243-O) and the **Eleventh Schedule** (29 functional items).
  - *Gram Sabha (Art 243A)*: The foundation of the system; assembly of all registered voters in a village.
  - *Three-Tier System (Art 243B)*: Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (intermediate), Zilla Parishad (district). (Intermediate tier optional for states with population below 2 million).
  - *Reservation of Seats (Art 243D)*: SCs and STs in proportion to population; mandatory **not less than one-third (33%) reservation for women** across all tiers and offices of chairpersons (many states have raised this to 50%).
  - *Fixed 5-Year Tenure (Art 243E)*: Fresh elections mandatory within 6 months of dissolution.
  - *State Election Commission (Art 243K)*: Independent superintendence, direction, and control of electoral rolls and elections.
  - *State Finance Commission (Art 243I)*: Constituted every 5 years by the Governor to review financial position and recommend tax devolution.
- **PESA Act of 1996 (Provisions of the Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas)**: Extended Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas, empowering Gram Sabhas with mandatory prior approval over land acquisition, mineral prospecting, prevention of land alienation, control over minor forest produce (MFP), and management of local village markets.

### 8.4 The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act (1992): Urban Local Bodies
Added **Part IX-A** (Articles 243-P to 243-ZG) and the **Twelfth Schedule** (18 functional items):
- Three types of Municipalities: **Nagar Panchayat** (transitional rural-to-urban area), **Municipal Council** (smaller urban area), and **Municipal Corporation** (larger urban metropolis).
- Wards Committees (Art 243S), District Planning Committees (Art 243ZD), and Metropolitan Planning Committees (Art 243ZE) to consolidate urban and rural developmental plans.

## Unit 9: Constitutional, Statutory & Regulatory Watchdogs

### 9.1 Constitutional Bodies
Bodies created directly by the text of the Constitution, possessing entrenched institutional independence:
1. **Election Commission of India (Article 324)**: Independent permanent constitutional body supervising elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and offices of President and Vice-President. Comprises Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and two Election Commissioners. CEC possesses security of tenure equal to a Supreme Court judge (removable only via presidential impeachment under Art 124(4)). Enforces the **Model Code of Conduct (MCC)**.
2. **Union Public Service Commission (UPSC, Articles 315 to 323)**: Independent central recruiting agency. Chairman and members appointed by President for 6 years / 65 years age; removable only on grounds of misbehavior after inquiry by Supreme Court under Article 317. Advisory role on disciplinary and promotion matters.
3. **Finance Commission (Article 280)**: Quasicjudicial body constituted by the President every five years. Recommends vertical devolution of tax divisible pool between Union and States, horizontal distribution among States, principles governing grants-in-aid (Article 275), and measures to augment Consolidated Funds of States for Panchayats and Municipalities.
4. **Goods and Services Tax Council (Article 279A)**: Federal constitutional body introduced by 101st CAA 2016. Chaired by Union Finance Minister, with Union Minister of State (Finance) and State Finance Ministers. Voting weight: Central Government has 1/3rd of total votes; State Governments collectively have 2/3rd of total votes; decisions require a **3/4th weighted majority**.
5. **Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG, Articles 148 to 151)**: Dr. Ambedkar described the CAG as *"the most important officer in the Constitution of India."* Guardian of the Public Purse; audits all expenditure from Consolidated Fund of India and States. Submits three audit reports to the President (Appropriation, Finance, and Public Undertakings), examined by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC).
6. **Attorney General for India (Article 76)**: Highest law officer of the country; appointed by President, qualified to be an SC judge; holds office during presidential pleasure. Has right of audience in all courts in India and right to speak in both Houses of Parliament without the right to vote (Article 88).

### 9.2 Statutory & Extra-Constitutional Bodies
1. **NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India)**: Established on January 1, 2015, via Cabinet Resolution, replacing the 65-year-old top-down Planning Commission. Operates as a policy think tank fostering **Cooperative Federalism** through its Governing Council (comprising the Prime Minister, all State Chief Ministers, and UT Lt. Governors).
2. **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)**: Statutory watchdog established under the *Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993*. Chaired by a former CJI or SC Judge; selection committee includes PM, Speaker, Home Minister, Leaders of Opposition in LS/RS, and RS Deputy Chairman.
3. **Central Vigilance Commission (CVC)**: Conceived on Santhanam Committee recommendations (1964); conferred statutory status in 2003. Apex integrity institution supervising anti-corruption probes under the Prevention of Corruption Act.
4. **Lokpal and Lokayuktas (Act of 2013)**: Statutory ombudsman investigating corruption allegations against public functionaries, including the Prime Minister (with specified procedural safeguards), Union Ministers, MPs, and Groups A, B, C, D civil servants.

## Unit 10: Political Dynamics, Electoral Jurisprudence & Working of the Constitution

### 10.1 The Anti-Defection Law (Tenth Schedule)
Added by the **52nd Constitutional Amendment Act (1985)** to eradicate the political culture of party-hopping (*"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram"*):
- **Disqualification Grounds**:
  1. *Voluntary Surrender*: If an elected member belonging to a political party voluntarily gives up membership (*Ravi Naik Case*: inferred conduct suffices; resignation not mandatory).
  2. *Whip Violation*: If a member votes or abstains contrary to party direction without prior permission.
  3. *Independent Member*: Disqualified if they join any political party after election.
  4. *Nominated Member*: Disqualified if they join any political party after expiry of 6 months.
- **The Split and Merger Exceptions**: The 52nd CAA exempted splits (1/3rd members). The **91st Amendment Act (2003)** abolished the split defense; a defection is protected only if there is a **Merger of at least two-thirds (2/3rd) of the party's members** in the legislature.
- **Adjudicating Authority**: Decided by the Presiding Officer (Speaker/Chairman).
- **Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)**: The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule, but struck down the clause ousting judicial review, ruling that the Speaker acts as a statutory **Tribunal** and decisions are subject to **Judicial Review** for mala fides, perversity, or constitutional violation.

### 10.2 Electoral Laws & Representation of the People Acts (RPA)
- **RPA 1950**: Governs allocation of seats, delimitation of constituencies, voter qualifications, and electoral roll preparation.
- **RPA 1951**: Governs conduct of elections, qualifications/disqualifications of MPs/MLAs, corrupt electoral practices, election disputes, and political party registration under Section 29A.
  - *Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013)*: The SC struck down Section 8(4) of RPA 1951 (which granted convicted lawmakers a 3-month protection window against disqualification pending appeal); conviction carrying 2+ years imprisonment triggers **immediate disqualification**.
  - *Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR 2002/2003)*: Ruled that voters possess a Fundamental Right to Information under Article 19(1)(a) regarding criminal antecedents, financial assets, and educational qualifications of election candidates.
  - *NOTA (None of the Above, 2013)*: Directed ECI to introduce NOTA button on EVMs.
  - *Electoral Bonds Scheme Struck Down (2024)*: A unanimous 5-judge bench held anonymous corporate donations through electoral bonds unconstitutional, violating Article 19(1)(a).

### 10.3 Assessment of the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC 2002)
In **2000**, the Central Government appointed the **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC)**, chaired by former CJI **Justice M.N. Venkatachaliah**:
- Concluded that the Constitution has not failed the people; rather, those entrusted with operating it had frequently failed the Constitution.
- **Key Recommendations**:
  - Incorporate explicit Fundamental Right to Rural and Urban Livelihood, Freedom of Press, and Healthcare into Part III.
  - Make the Inter-State Council a more active institutional forum under Article 263.
  - Mandate that a Chief Minister be appointed by the Governor only upon a vote of confidence on the floor of the Assembly (*Constructive Vote of No-Confidence*).
  - Rationalize the Governor’s appointment process in consultation with the Chief Minister.
  - Establish a National Judicial Commission for appointments to the higher judiciary.

## Pedagogical Self-Test Questions

1. **Constitutional Genesis**: How did the Government of India Act of 1935 establish the structural, federal, and institutional foundation of the 1950 Indian Constitution? Identify the five major institutional features borrowed directly from the 1935 Act.
2. **Preamble Jurisprudence**: Trace the judicial evolution of the Preamble across the *Berubari Union (1960)*, *Kesavananda Bharati (1973)*, and *LIC of India (1995)* cases. What was the constitutional significance of the 42nd Amendment Act 1976?
3. **Judicial Transformation of Liberty**: Contrast the "Procedure Established by Law" doctrine in *A.K. Gopalan (1950)* with the "Due Process of Law" doctrine established in *Maneka Gandhi (1978)*. How did this transformation culminate in the declaration of Privacy as a Fundamental Right in *K.S. Puttaswamy (2017)*?
4. **The FR-DPSP Dialectic & Basic Structure**: Analyze the conflict between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles from *Champakam Dorairajan* to *Minerva Mills*. Explain why the Basic Structure Doctrine represents an insurmountable barrier to unlimited parliamentary amending power under Article 368.
5. **Parliamentary Financial Governance**: Contrast a Money Bill (Article 110) with an Ordinary Bill regarding introducing chamber, Presidential recommendation, Rajya Sabha powers, and Joint Sitting (Article 108). How do the Public Accounts Committee and CAG enforce post-facto financial accountability?
6. **Asymmetric Federalism & Decentralization**: Differentiate between the Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas) and Sixth Schedule (Tribal Areas in AMTM) in their administrative autonomy. How did the 73rd and 74th Amendments institutionalize democratic decentralization, and how does the PESA Act of 1996 safeguard tribal self-governance?
