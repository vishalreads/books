Executive Epistemological Overview

Rajendra Prasad's *History of Modern India: Complete Study Notes with Practice MCQ for UPSC and State PSC Prelims & Mains Exam* provides a comprehensive, chronologically rigorous, and highly analytical synthesis of the modern Indian historical arc. Spanning from the decline of the Mughal Empire in the early eighteenth century through the consolidation of British colonial supremacy to the multifaceted freedom struggle and the climactic partition and independence in 1947, the work is engineered to provide civil services candidates with **complete conceptual mastery, statutory clarity, and empirical depth**.

The treatise deconstructs the transformation of India through three primary structural prisms: 1. **The Colonial State as an Extraction Machine**: How an armed commercial corporation (the East India Company) transformed into an imperial state using specialized annexation doctrines (Subsidiary Alliance, Doctrine of Lapse) and fiscal instruments (Zamindari, Ryotwari, Mahalwari), engineering the systematic deindustrialization of Indian handicrafts and the unilateral Drain of Wealth. 2. **The Dialectic of Indigenous Resistance**: Mapping the continuum of resistance from localized peasant and tribal insurrections (Santhal, Paika, Munda) and the cataclysmic watershed of the 1857 Revolt to the sophisticated intellectual socio-religious reform movements of the nineteenth century. 3. **The Organizational Dynamics of the National Movement**: Analyzing the ideological evolution from elite Moderate constitutionalism (1885–1905) to Extremist mass agitation (1905–1919), Revolutionary martyrdom (HSRA, INA), and the transcendent Gandhian mass satyagraha paradigm (1920–1947).

Unit 1: Eighteenth-Century Power Matrix: Mughal Decline, Regional Dynamics & Panipat

1.1 The Post-Aurangzeb Imperial Dissolution The death of Emperor **Aurangzeb in 1707** at Ahmednagar signaled the irreversible collapse of the centralized Mughal administrative state. The empire disintegrated rapidly under structural contradictions: - **The Mansabdari and Jagirdari Crisis**: The imperial policy of endless territorial expansion into the Deccan drained the imperial treasury and multiplied the number of imperial nobles (*mansabdars*) beyond the capacity of available productive crown lands (*paibaqi*). The resulting deficit of revenue assignments (*jagirs*) triggered fierce factional infighting among the Turani, Irani, Afghani, and Hindustani noble factions. - **The Agrarian Breakdown**: To satisfy escalating revenue demands, jagirdars squeezed the peasantry beyond subsistence levels, triggering widespread peasant rebellions under the leadership of the Jats, Sikhs, and Satnamis. - **The Foreign Incursions**: - *Nadir Shah of Persia (1739)*: Crushed Emperor Muhammad Shah's forces at the Battle of Karnal; sacked Delhi, massacred its population, looted the Peacock Throne, the Koh-i-Noor diamond, and ₹70 crore in bullion, destroying the aura of Mughal invincibility. - *Ahmad Shah Abdali (1748–1767)*: Raided Northern India eight times, neutralizing Mughal military remnants in the Punjab.

1.2 The Threefold Typology of Eighteenth-Century Indian States Historians categorize the successor regimes of the eighteenth century into three distinct political configurations: 1. **Successor States (Broke away from imperial center but retained Mughal administrative forms)**: - *Bengal*: Founded by **Murshid Quli Khan** (made Governor in 1717 by Farrukhsiyar); shifted capital from Dacca to Murshidabad; introduced revenue-farming (*ijaradari*); succeeded by Shuja-ud-din, Alivardi Khan (1740–1756), and Siraj-ud-Daulah. - *Awadh*: Founded by **Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk** in 1722; succeeded by Safdarjung and Shuja-ud-Daulah; developed Lucknow into a premier cultural metropolis. - *Hyderabad*: Founded by **Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah** in 1724 after defeating Mubariz Khan at the Battle of Shakr Kheda; preserved autonomous Mughal governance in the Deccan. 2. **Warrior States (Rose in rebellion against Mughal hegemony)**: - *Maratha Confederacy*: Reached paramount status under the Peshwas: Balaji Vishwanath (1713–1720), Baji Rao I (1720–1740, proponent of *Hindu Pad Padshahi* and expansion to Attock), and Balaji Baji Rao (Nana Saheb, 1740–1761). Governed through decentralized regional fiefdoms: Scindia (Gwalior), Holkar (Indore), Gaekwad (Baroda), and Bhonsle (Nagpur). - *Sikh Kingdom*: Emerged from 12 autonomous military fraternities (*Misls*), unified into a centralized sovereign empire by **Maharaja Ranjit Singh** (conquered Lahore 1799, Amritsar 1802; signed Treaty of Amritsar with British in 1809; built modern modernized army, the *Fauj-i-Khas*, trained by French officers Allard and Court). - *Mysore*: Transformed into an advanced military power by **Hyder Ali** (1761) and his son **Tipu Sultan** (*Tiger of Mysore*). Tipu introduced modern artillery, iron-cased war rockets, established a state commercial trading corporation, constructed a modern naval fleet at Bhatkal, and planted a Tree of Liberty at Seringapatam as an honorary Jacobin. 3. **Independent States**: Rajput principalities (Amber under Sawai Jai Singh, builder of astronomical observatories or *Jantar Mantars* at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Mathura, and Varanasi) and the Kingdom of Travancore under **Martanda Varma** (who famously routed the Dutch East India Company at the **Battle of Colachel in 1741**, permanently ending Dutch political ambitions in India).

1.3 Historiographical Debates: Dark Age vs. Regional Prosperity - **The "Dark Age" Thesis (Colonial View)**: Imperial historians (Vincent Smith, James Mill) characterized the 18th century as a period of absolute political anarchy, economic decline, and civil war, thereby legitimizing the British conquest as a "civilizing mission" and provider of order (*Pax Britannica*). - **The "Regional Reorientation" Thesis (Modern Revisionists)**: Revisionist historians (Muzaffar Alam, C.A. Bayly, Sanjay Subrahmanyam) demonstrate that while the imperial center at Delhi decayed, economic vitality, agrarian commercialization, and urban cultural vitality shifted dynamically to regional polities (Bengal, Awadh, Mysore, and Travancore). The British conquered not a desolate, dying civilization, but thriving commercialized regional economies.

1.4 The Cataclysm of Panipat (January 14, 1761) The **Third Battle of Panipat** was fought between the Maratha Confederacy (commanded by Sadashivrao Bhau and Vishwasrao) and the Afghan coalition of Ahmad Shah Abdali, supported by Najib-ud-Daulah (Rohilla) and Shuja-ud-Daulah (Nawab of Awadh): - **Outcome**: The Maratha army was encircled and annihilated; Sadashivrao Bhau, Vishwasrao, Tukoji Scindia, and 28,000 Maratha soldiers fell on the battlefield; Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao died of heartbreak upon receiving the news. - **Historiographical Consequence**: Panipat permanently arrested the northward expansion of the Marathas, shattered their internal unity into competing regional houses, and created an enormous geopolitical power vacuum. As historian J.N. Sarkar famously remarked, Panipat did not decide who was to rule India, but rather who was *not* to rule India—clearing the path directly for British territorial supremacy.

Unit 2: European Rivalries, The Battle for Bengal & Imperial Annexations

2.1 The Anglo-French Rivalry: The Carnatic Wars (1746–1763) The struggle between Britain and France for control of Indian trade and political supremacy unfolded in the Coromandel (Carnatic) region: - **First Carnatic War (1746–1748)**: Spurred by the War of the Austrian Succession in Europe. Dupleix captured Madras. In the **Battle of St. Thome (1746)** on the Adyar river, Captain Paradise’s tiny French force (230 Europeans, 700 Indian sepoys) routed Nawab Anwar-ud-din’s 10,000-strong army, proving the supreme tactical superiority of European infantry drill and rapid field artillery over traditional cavalry hordes. Ended with the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), restoring Madras to the British in exchange for Louisbourg in North America. - **Second Carnatic War (1749–1754)**: Dupleix intervened in the disputed successions of Hyderabad (Muzaffar Jung vs. Nasir Jung) and Carnatic (Chanda Sahib vs. Anwar-ud-din). **Robert Clive** executed the daring capture and 53-day defense of **Arcot (1751)** with just 500 men, turning the tide against the French. Ended with the Treaty of Pondicherry (1754); Dupleix was recalled to France in disgrace. - **Third Carnatic War (1758–1763)**: Triggered by the Seven Years' War in Europe. British forces under General **Sir Eyre Coote** decisively defeated the French under Count de Lally at the **Battle of Wandiwash (January 22, 1760)**. The Treaty of Paris (1763) permitted the French to retain Pondicherry, Chandernagore, and Mahe purely as unfortified commercial factories, permanently terminating French imperial ambitions in India.

2.2 The Subjugation of Bengal: Plassey to Buxar - **The Farman of 1717**: Emperor Farrukhsiyar granted the East India Company exemption from customs duties in Bengal in exchange for an annual payment of ₹3,000, issuing trade permits (*Dastaks*). Company servants corruptly misused Dastaks for private trade, cheating the Nawab’s treasury. - **The Black Hole Tragedy (June 1756)**: Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah seized Fort William, Calcutta, to stop unauthorized British military fortifications. British propagandist J.Z. Holwell claimed 146 British prisoners were locked in an 18x14 foot cell, suffocating 123 to death (largely an exaggerated pretext manufactured to justify aggressive military retaliation). - **The Battle of Plassey (June 23, 1757)**: Robert Clive marched on Plassey (Palashi). In a pre-arranged conspiracy, Mir Jafar (Mir Bakshi), Rai Durlabh, and financier Jagat Seth betrayed Siraj. Siraj fled, was captured, and murdered by Mir Jafar’s son, Miran. Clive installed Mir Jafar as puppet Nawab, extracting ₹2.25 crore in cash compensation and the zamindari of the 24 Parganas. - **The Battle of Buxar (October 22, 1764)**: Mir Qasim attempted to build an independent army at Munger, dismissed corrupt officials, and abolished all customs duties to eliminate British trade advantages. War broke out; Mir Qasim formed a tripartite alliance with Nawab Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. Major **Hector Munro** crushed the combined armies at Buxar. - *Significance*: Unlike Plassey (which was a skirmish decided by treason), Buxar was a pitched military contest against the sovereign Mughal Emperor and the Premier Nawab of Northern India. It confirmed the British Company as the de facto master of Northern India. - **The Treaty of Allahabad (August 1765)**: Clive secured the **Diwani Rights** (revenue collection rights) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa from Emperor Shah Alam II in return for an annual tribute of ₹26 lakh and the districts of Kora and Allahabad. - **The Dual Government in Bengal (1765–1772)**: Clive bifurcated governance into *Diwani* (revenue extraction, held directly by the Company) and *Nizamat* (civil administration, police, and justice, left to the impoverished Nawab). The Company enjoyed power without responsibility, while the Nawab bore responsibility without power or resources. This predatory system led to the catastrophic **Great Bengal Famine of 1770**, where one-third of Bengal's population (approx. 10 million people) perished of starvation while the Company maintained peak revenue extractions. Abolished by Warren Hastings in 1772.

2.3 Subjugation of Regional Powers: Mysore, Marathas, and Sikhs 1. **The Four Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–1799)**: - *First War (1767–69)*: Hyder Ali forced the British to sign the defensive Treaty of Madras. - *Second War (1780–84)*: British violated the treaty; Hyder Ali formed an alliance with Marathas and Nizam, routing Colonel Baillie; Hyder Ali died in 1782; Tipu continued the fight, concluding the Treaty of Mangalore (1784) on terms of mutual restitution. - *Third War (1790–92)*: Lord Cornwallis allied with Marathas and Nizam, besieged Seringapatam; forced Tipu into the humiliating **Treaty of Seringapatam (1792)**, ceding half his territory and surrendering two sons as hostages. - *Fourth War (1799)*: Lord Wellesley launched an all-out invasion alleging French collusion; Tipu Sultan died defending the ramparts of Seringapatam on May 4, 1799. Mysore was placed under the Wodeyar dynasty under a Subsidiary Alliance. 2. **The Three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818)**: - *First War (1775–82)*: Triggered by British support for pretender Raghunath Rao; Nana Fadnavis led Maratha resistance; concluded by the **Treaty of Salbai (1782)**, guaranteeing 20 years of peace. - *Second War (1803–05)*: Peshwa Baji Rao II fled to the British and signed the **Treaty of Bassein (1802)**, surrendering sovereignty. Scindia, Bhonsle, and Holkar resisted but were defeated by Arthur Wellesley and Lake, ceding vast territories. - *Third War (1817–18)*: Pindari campaign merged into a final Maratha desperate rebellion; Lord Hastings defeated the Peshwa, Scindia, Holkar, and Bhonsle; Peshwaship was abolished, Baji Rao II exiled to Bithoor on a British pension, and Maratha independence extinguished. 3. **The Two Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–1849)**: - *First War (1845–46)*: Post-Ranjit Singh court intrigues and Lal Singh/Tej Singh's betrayal led to Sikh defeat at Sobraon; **Treaty of Lahore (1846)** imposed ₹1.5 crore indemnity, ceded Jalandhar Doab, and sold Kashmir to Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh. - *Second War (1848–49)*: Rebellions by Mulraj (Multan) and Chattar Singh; battles of Chillianwala and Gujarat; Lord Dalhousie formally annexed Punjab on March 29, 1849.

2.4 Instruments of Colonial Expansion 1. **The Policy of Ring Fence (Warren Hastings, 1772–1785)**: Aimed at creating buffer states to defend Company frontiers from Maratha and Afghan invasions (e.g., defending Awadh to safeguard Bengal). 2. **The Subsidiary Alliance System (Lord Wellesley, 1798–1805)**: - An Indian state entering the alliance disbanded its indigenous army, stationed a permanent British force paid for by the state (in cash or territory), expelled all non-British Europeans, surrendered foreign relations to the British, and accepted a British Resident. - States trapped: **Hyderabad (1798 & 1800)**, Mysore (1799), Tanjore (1799), Awadh (1801), Peshwa Baji Rao II (Treaty of Bassein 1802), Bhonsle and Scindia (1803). 3. **The Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie, 1848–1856)**: - Prohibited dependent Hindu princely states from adopting male heirs without imperial permission; upon the death of an adoptive ruler without a natural heir, the state lapsed to the Company. - Annexed: **Satara (1848)**, Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), **Jhansi (1853)**, and **Nagpur (1854)**. - Annexed **Awadh (1856)** on the pretext of "maladministration" (Sleeman and Outram reports), deposing Nawab Wajid Ali Shah.

Unit 3: Colonial Fiscalism: Land Revenue Systems, Commercialization & Drain of Wealth

3.1 The Three Land Revenue Systems

  • **Permanent Settlement (1793)**: Formulated by Sir John Shore, implemented by Lord Cornwallis. Zamindars were recognized as hereditary proprietors of land. State revenue was permanently fixed at 10/11ths of the rent collected, with 1/11th retained by the Zamindar. The stringent **Sunset Law** mandated that if the revenue was not deposited by sunset on the designated date, the estate was auctioned off. It created a loyal class of absentee landlords who lived in Calcutta while intermediaries exploited the tillers through excessive sub-infeudation.
  • **Ryotwari System (1820)**: Devised by Alexander Read and popularized by Sir Thomas Munro in Madras and Mountstuart Elphinstone in Bombay. Bypassed zamindars to settle directly with individual cultivators (*ryots*). However, revenue assessments were exorbitant (45%–55% of gross produce) and revised upwards periodically. Cultivators were compelled to turn to village usurers (*Mahajans*), causing widespread mortgage and land dispossession.
  • **Mahalwari System (1822/1833)**: Conceived by Holt Mackenzie (Regulation VII of 1822) and reformed by R.M. Bird and James Thomason (1833). Applied to the North-Western Provinces, Central Provinces, and Punjab. Revenue was settled with the entire village community (*Mahal*) represented by the headman (*Lambardar*), with joint and several responsibility for tax default.

3.2 The Drain of Wealth & Deindustrialization - **Dadabhai Naoroji’s Drain Theory**: Published in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901), Naoroji proved that British rule extracted continuous, unrequited economic surplus from India to Britain through **Home Charges**, interest on public debt, military expenditures outside India, guaranteed 5% railway capital profits, and British corporate remittances. Naoroji estimated that India lost approximately £30 million annually without any material return. Supported by Romesh Chunder Dutt (*Economic History of India*) and Dinshaw Wacha. - **Deindustrialization**: Unilateral free trade imposed on India ruined the traditional textile artisans of Dacca, Surat, and Murshidabad. British manufactured cloth flooded India duty-free while Indian exports to Britain faced crushing tariffs (up to 80%), reducing India to an agrarian appendage of Manchester. - **Commercialization of Agriculture**: Coerced transition from subsistence food grain farming to cash crops for export (Indigo, Opium, Cotton, Jute, Tea). Left the peasant vulnerable to global market price crashes and triggered catastrophic recurring famines. - **The Railway Policy**: Dalhousie's 1853 Minute launched Indian railways under the "Guarantee System", guaranteeing private British investors a 5% net annual return funded entirely by the Indian exchequer. D.E. Wacha called it "private enterprise at public risk." Railways served colonial extraction—carrying raw materials from the hinterland to ports, and dispersing Manchester textiles inland—rather than catalyzing indigenous industrialization. - **Famine Commissions**: Devastating famines recurred with horrific frequency (1866 Orissa Famine, 1876–78 Great Famine). In response, the British established commissions: Sir Richard Strachey Commission (1880, proposed Famine Code and Famine Relief Fund), Sir James Lyall Commission (1897), and Sir Antony MacDonnell Commission (1901, emphasized official moral responsibility and agricultural banks).

Unit 4: Grassroots Defiance: Tribal Uprisings, Peasant Revolts & The 1857 Rebellion

4.1 Subaltern Rebellions Pre- and Post-1857 - **Sanyasi and Fakir Rebellion (1763–1800)**: In Bengal, ascetics and evicted peasants rose against British revenue exactions and restrictions on holy pilgrimages. Led by Majnu Shah, Chirag Ali, Bhawani Pathak, and Devi Chaudhurani. Immortalized in Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay’s patriotic novel *Anandamath* (which contained the national hymn *Vande Mataram*). - **Paika Rebellion (1817)**: The traditional landed militia (*Paikas*) of Khurda in Odisha, led by **Bakshi Jagabandhu Bidyadhara**, rebelled against the British confiscation of rent-free lands (*jagirs*), currency changes, and salt taxation. - **Kol Uprising (1831–1832)**: Tribals of Chhota Nagpur under Buddho Bhagat rebelled against the transfer of ancestral lands to outsider Sikh and Muslim farmers. - **Santhal Rebellion (Hool, 1855–1856)**: In the Rajmahal Hills (Damin-i-Koh), Santhal tribals under brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu** revolted against the nexus of British police, revenue officers, and moneylenders (*Dikus*). Over 15,000 Santhals were slaughtered before the rebellion was suppressed, leading to the creation of the Santhal Parganas district. - **Munda Ulgulan (The Great Tumult, 1899–1900)**: In Chhota Nagpur, **Birsa Munda** (*Dharti Aba*) launched an armed uprising against the destruction of the ancestral *Khuntkatti* (joint landholding) system, forced labor (*Bethbegari*), and Christian missionaries, demanding a sovereign Munda Raj. Led to the enactment of the **Chhota Nagpur Tenancy Act, 1908**. - **Indigo Revolt (Nil Bidroho, 1859–1860)**: In Bengal (Nadia and Jessore), peasants led by **Digambar Biswas and Bishnu Biswas** refused to grow indigo under European planters' oppressive contracts (*Tinkathia* coercion). Immortalized in Dinabandhu Mitra’s play *Nil Darpan*. Led to the Indigo Commission (1860) declaring that ryots could not be coerced. - **Deccan Riots (1875)**: In Pune and Ahmednagar, peasants attacked Gujarati and Marwari moneylenders, burning debt bonds (*khatas*). Led to the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879.

4.2 The Revolt of 1857: The Sepoy Mutiny & National War - **The Causes**: - *Political*: Doctrine of Lapse, annexation of Awadh (1856), humiliation of the Mughal Emperor (Dalhousie announced Bahadur Shah's successors would vacate the Red Fort). - *Economic*: Ruin of handicrafts, agrarian over-taxation, dispossession of Awadh taluqdars under the Summary Settlement of 1856. - *Socio-Religious*: Lex Loci Act 1850 (permitting Christian converts to inherit ancestral property), General Service Enlistment Act 1856 (compelling sepoys to serve overseas, which violated caste purity rules), active missionary proselytization. - *Immediate Cause*: Introduction of the Enfield rifle requiring biting greased cartridges rumored to be coated in cow and pig fat. - **Outbreak**: March 29, 1857: Mangal Pandey mutinied at Barrackpore, firing upon British officers. May 10, 1857: Sepoys mutinied at Meerut, marched to Delhi, and proclaimed **Bahadur Shah Zafar as Shahenshah-e-Hindustan**. - **Key Battles and Leaders**: - *Delhi*: General Bakht Khan (Bareilly brigade); siege broken by John Nicholson. - *Kanpur*: Nana Saheb (adoptive son of Baji Rao II), Tantia Tope, and Azimullah Khan; suppressed by Colin Campbell. - *Lucknow*: Begum Hazrat Mahal and prince Birjis Qadir; Henry Lawrence died defending the Residency; relieved by Outram, Havelock, and Campbell. - *Jhansi*: Rani Lakshmibai (*"I shall not surrender my Jhansi"*); fought heroically alongside Tantia Tope at Gwalior; fell fighting General Hugh Rose. Rose eulogized her: *"Here lay the woman who was the only man among the rebels."* - *Bihar (Arrah)*: Kunwar Singh (80-year-old zamindar of Jagdishpur); defeated British detachments across the Ganga. - *Faizabad*: Maulvi Ahmadullah. - **Suppression and Legacy**: Led to the **Government of India Act 1858**, abolishing the East India Company, transferring sovereignty directly to Queen Victoria, and creating the office of the Secretary of State for India. The **Queen’s Proclamation of 1858** announced non-interference in religious customs and abandonment of territorial annexation. The **Peel Commission (1859)** reorganized the British Indian army on the principle of "Division and Counterpoise," sharply increasing the ratio of European to Indian troops and organizing regiments on communal/martial-race lines to prevent future solidarity.

Unit 5: The Renaissance of Modern India: Socio-Religious Reform Movements

5.1 Reformist vs. Revivalist Ideological Currents The Indian renaissance of the nineteenth century operated along two distinct intellectual vectors: 1. **Reformist Movements**: Responded to the Western rationalist critique by advocating internal purification and structural modification of indigenous traditions without severing civilizational roots (Brahmo Samaj, Prarthana Samaj, Aligarh Movement). 2. **Revivalist Movements**: Sought to reconstruct a pristine golden past, arguing that indigenous scriptures contained universal truths superior to Western materialism (Arya Samaj, Deoband Movement, Ramakrishna-Vivekananda Movement).

5.2 Key Reformers and Organizations - **Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833)**: Father of Modern Indian Renaissance. Founded the *Atmiya Sabha* (1815) and *Brahmo Samaj* (1828). Monotheist; campaigned relentlessly against Sati, leading to William Bentinck’s Regulation XVII of 1829. Advocated Western scientific education and freedom of the press (*Mirat-ul-Akhbar* in Persian, *Sambad Kaumudi* in Bengali). Established Hindu College (1817) with David Hare and Vedanta College (1825). - **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891)**: Principal of Sanskrit College, opened Sanskrit education to non-Brahmins. Crusaded against child marriage and for widow remarriage, resulting in the **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (Act XV of 1856)** enacted under Lord Canning. Established Bethune School (1849) for female education. - **Young Bengal Movement (1820s–1830s)**: Led by Anglo-Indian teacher **Henry Louis Vivian Derozio** at Hindu College. Radical freethinkers inspired by the French Revolution; attacked orthodox rituals, advocated women’s rights and free speech. - **Prarthana Samaj (1867)**: Founded in Bombay by **Dr. Atmaram Pandurang**, later energized by **Mahadev Govind Ranade** and **R.G. Bhandarkar**. Emphasized monotheism, inter-caste dining, widow remarriage, and uplift of depressed classes. - **Swami Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883)**: Founded **Arya Samaj** (1875) in Bombay; authored *Satyarth Prakash*. Coined the clarion call **"Go Back to the Vedas"**, declaring the four Vedas infallible divine revelations. Rejected idol worship, polytheism, hereditary caste hierarchy, and priestly intermediaries; pioneered the *Shuddhi* reconversion movement and established DAV (Dayanand Anglo-Vedic) institutions. - **Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902)**: Chief disciple of Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa; founded the **Ramakrishna Mission** (1897) at Belur Math. Represented Hinduism at the World Parliament of Religions in Chicago (1893). Synthesized Advaita Vedanta with social service: *"Daridra Narayana"* (service to the poor is service to God). - **Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890)**: Radical anti-caste crusader of Maharashtra. Established **Satyashodhak Samaj** (1873); authored *Gulamgiri* (Slavery, dedicated to American abolitionists) and *Shetkaryacha Asud* (The Cultivator's Whipcord). With wife Savitribai Phule, opened India's first school for lower-caste girls at Bhide Wada, Pune (1848). - **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898)**: Launched the **Aligarh Movement**; founded the Scientific Society (1864) and the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College** (1875) at Aligarh (which became Aligarh Muslim University in 1920). Advocated modern scientific education and rational Quranic interpretation (*Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*), though politically advising Muslims to stay aloof from the Congress. - **Deoband School (1866)**: Founded by Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi at Saharanpur, UP. Orthodox Islamic revivalist school aiming to preserve Islamic religious doctrine; supported the Indian National Congress and issued a religious fatwa against Sir Syed’s pro-British stance. - **Temple Entry and Self-Respect Movements**: - *Self-Respect Movement (1925)*: Founded by **E.V. Ramasamy Naicker (Periyar)** in Tamil Nadu, rejecting Brahminical hegemony, Manusmriti, and advocating self-respect marriages without Brahmin priests. - *Vaikom Satyagraha (1924–1925)*: Led by K.P. Kesava Menon, T.K. Madhavan, and E.V. Ramasamy in Travancore, demanding the right of untouchables to walk on public roads surrounding the Vaikom Shiva temple. - *Guruvayur Satyagraha (1931–1932)*: Led by K. Kelappan (*Kerala Gandhi*) and A.K. Gopalan for temple entry in Malabar.

Unit 6: The Inception of Nationalism: Pre-Congress Forums & The Moderate-Extremist Dialectic

6.1 Early Political Formations & The Birth of Congress - **Pre-Congress Provincial Associations**: - *Landholders' Society (1838)*: Founded by Dwarkanath Tagore and Radhakanta Deb; first organized political forum in India to defend landlord interests. - *British Indian Association (1851)*: Formed in Calcutta, combining Landholders' Society and Bengal British India Society. - *Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870)*: Founded by M.G. Ranade, G.V. Joshi (*Chitnis*), and S.H. Chiplunkar to mediate between the government and people during famines. - *Indian Association of Calcutta (1876)*: Founded by **Surendranath Banerjea** and **Ananda Mohan Bose**; organized nationwide agitation against lowering the ICS examination age limit from 21 to 19 years (under Lord Lytton). - *Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884)*: Founded by M. Veeraraghavachariar, G. Subramania Iyer, and P. Anandacharlu. - *Bombay Presidency Association (1885)*: Founded by Pherozeshah Mehta, K.T. Telang, and Badruddin Tyabji (*"The Three Stars of Bombay"*). - **The Foundation of the Indian National Congress (December 1885)**: - Formed under the initiative of retired British civil servant **Allan Octavian Hume**. First session held at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay (December 28–31, 1885), presided over by **Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee** and attended by 72 delegates. - *The "Safety Valve" Controversy*: Colonial administrators and early critics (such as Lala Lajpat Rai in *Young India*) alleged that Hume created Congress as a "safety valve" to release growing popular discontent and avert a second 1857 mutiny. Modern nationalist historians (Bipan Chandra) argue the early leaders used Hume as a **"lightning conductor"** to neutralize colonial repression while establishing an all-India political platform.

Unit 7: Mass Agitation & Armed Resistance: Swadeshi, Revolutionary Phase & Home Rule

7.1 The Swadeshi Movement & The Surat Split - **The Partition of Bengal (1905)**: Announced by Viceroy Lord Curzon in July 1905, taking effect on October 16, 1905. Ostensibly justified for administrative convenience, the real imperial motive (documented in Home Secretary Risley’s confidential minutes) was to split the politically conscious Bengali intelligentsia along religious fault lines: Muslim-majority Eastern Bengal & Assam vs. Hindu-majority Western Bengal. - **The Swadeshi & Boycott Campaign**: Proclaimed on August 7, 1905, at Calcutta Town Hall: - *Boycott*: Rejection of Manchester textiles, Liverpool salt, foreign sugar, and British schools. - *Swadeshi & Self-Reliance (Atmashakti)*: Indigenous industries bloomed—Acharya P.C. Ray’s Bengal Chemicals, V.O. Chidambaram Pillai’s Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company at Tuticorin. - *National Education*: National Council of Education formed (1906); Bengal National College founded with Aurobindo Ghose as its first principal. - *Cultural Upsurge*: Rabindranath Tagore composed *Amar Sonar Bangla* and led mass rakhi-tying ceremonies on the partition day; Abanindranath Tagore painted the iconic *Bharat Mata* portraying the motherland as an ascetic four-armed goddess. - **The Surat Split (1907)**: Disagreement reached a breaking point at the Surat Congress session (December 1907) over extending the Swadeshi boycott nationwide and elevating Tilak/Lajpat Rai to the presidency. The session degenerated into physical brawl; Congress split; Moderates expelled Extremists. The colonial government unleashed brutal repression: Tilak was tried for sedition and imprisoned in Mandalay (Burma) for six years (1908–1914); Aurobindo retired to Pondicherry for spiritual yoga.

7.2 Phase I of Revolutionary Nationalism (1907–1917) - **Bengal**: Anushilan Samiti (founded 1902 by Pramathanath Mitra, Jatindranath Banerjee, Barindra Ghosh); *Yugantar* weekly (*"Force must be stopped by force"*). Alipore Bomb Conspiracy Case (1908) following Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki’s attempt on judge Kingsford at Muzaffarpur. Bagha Jatin (Jatindranath Mukherjee) died in the Battle of Burhi Balam (1915). - **Maharashtra**: Chapekar brothers (Damodar and Balkrishna) assassinated plague commissioner Rand in Pune (1897). V.D. Savarkar founded *Abhinav Bharat* (1904) and *Mitra Mela*. - **Delhi Conspiracy (1912)**: Rash Behari Bose and Sachin Sanyal hurled a bomb at Viceroy Lord Hardinge’s royal procession in Chandni Chowk, Delhi. - **Abroad**: - *India House (London)*: Established by Shyamji Krishna Varma; published *The Indian Sociologist*. Madan Lal Dhingra shot Curzon Wyllie dead in London (1909). - *Paris*: Madam Bhikaiji Cama unfurled the first Indian national tricolor at the International Socialist Conference in Stuttgart, Germany (1907). - *Ghadar Movement (1913)*: Founded in San Francisco, California, by Lala Hardayal, Sohan Singh Bhakna, and Kartar Singh Sarabha. Published the weekly *Ghadar* in Gurmukhi and Urdu. The **Komagata Maru incident (1914)** (where 376 passengers aboard a Japanese vessel were refused entry at Vancouver and returned to Calcutta, facing police firing at Budge Budge) inflamed diaspora fury. Planned an all-India armed insurrection on February 21, 1915, but was betrayed by internal spies and crushed by the colonial Defence of India Act 1915.

7.3 The Home Rule Movement & The Lucknow Pact (1916) - **Home Rule Leagues (1916)**: - *Tilak’s Home Rule League*: Launched in April 1916 at Belgaum; operated across Maharashtra (excluding Bombay), Karnataka, Central Provinces, and Berar. Coined the slogan: **"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!"** - *Annie Besant’s Home Rule League*: Launched in September 1916 at Madras; covered the rest of India with George Arundale as organizing secretary; published *New India* and *Commonweal*. - **Lucknow Session of INC (1916)**: Presided over by Ambica Charan Mazumdar: 1. Reunited the Moderate and Extremist factions of Congress after 9 years of alienation. 2. Concluded the historic **Lucknow Pact (1916)** between the Indian National Congress and the All-India Muslim League (negotiated by Tilak and Muhammad Ali Jinnah). Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims in provincial councils, while both bodies jointly presented a unified demand to the British Crown for self-government. - **Montagu Declaration (August 20, 1917)**: Secretary of State Edwin Montagu announced in British Parliament that the goal of British policy was "the gradual development of self-governing institutions, with a view to the progressive realization of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire."

Unit 8: The Gandhian Synthesis: Satyagraha, Non-Cooperation & Civil Disobedience

8.1 Gandhi’s Early Satyagraha Experiments (1917–1918) Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India on **January 9, 1915** (*Pravasi Bharatiya Divas*) after two decades in South Africa, where he perfected Satyagraha (Truth-Force and Non-Violent Civil Resistance). On Gokhale’s advice, he spent two years touring rural India: 1. **Champaran Satyagraha (1917, Bihar)**: First Civil Disobedience. Indigo peasants were coerced by European planters under the exploitative *Tinkathia* system (obligatory indigo cultivation on 3/20ths of land). Accompanied by Rajendra Prasad, J.B. Kripalani, and Mahadev Desai, Gandhi defied police eviction orders. The government formed the Champaran Agrarian Committee with Gandhi as a member; Tinkathia was abolished, and planters were compelled to refund 25% of illegal dues. 2. **Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918, Gujarat)**: First Hunger Strike. Dispute between mill owners and textile workers over the abolition of the plague bonus. Supported by Anasuya Sarabhai, Gandhi went on a fast unto death, securing a 35% wage increase for workers. 3. **Kheda Satyagraha (1918, Gujarat)**: First Non-Cooperation. Crops failed due to drought; under the land revenue code, if the yield was less than 25%, peasants were entitled to complete revenue remission, but the colonial authorities insisted on coercive tax collection. Gandhi and Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel organized peasants to withhold revenue; the government quietly issued orders to collect taxes only from those who could afford to pay.

8.2 Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh & Non-Cooperation (1919–1922) - **The Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act (Rowlatt Act, 1919)**: Enacted to extend wartime emergency measures, allowing indefinite detention of political suspects without trial, in camera trials, and no right of appeal (*"No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"*). Gandhi organized the nationwide Rowlatt Satyagraha. - **Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919)**: Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer marched British and Gurkha troops into the enclosed Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Punjab, where thousands had gathered peacefully on Baisakhi to protest the arrest of leaders Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal. Dyer sealed the single narrow exit and ordered continuous firing without warning for 10 minutes until ammunition was exhausted, slaughtering hundreds of men, women, and children. - *Repercussions*: Rabindranath Tagore renounced his British Knighthood; Gandhi surrendered his Kaiser-i-Hind medal, declaring British rule "satanic". The whitewashing Hunter Commission deepened Indian alienation. - **Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement (1920–1922)**: - Khilafat committee formed by the Ali Brothers (Shaukat Ali and Mohammad Ali), Maulana Azad, and Hasrat Mohani to protest the dismemberment of the Ottoman Caliphate by the Treaty of Sevres. Gandhi saw this as a historic opportunity to forge Hindu-Muslim unity (*"an opportunity such as will not occur once in a hundred years"*). - Special Calcutta Session (September 1920) and Nagpur Session (December 1920, presided by C. Vijayaraghavachariar) ratified the Non-Cooperation program: surrender of titles, boycott of civil service, courts, government schools, legislative councils, and foreign goods; promotion of Khadi, charkha, and national schools (Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith, Jamia Millia Islamia). - **Chauri Chaura Incident (February 5, 1922)**: At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur district, UP, police opened fire on a peaceful peasant procession; the enraged mob retaliated by burning down the police station, killing 22 policemen. Heartbroken that the masses had not fully mastered non-violence, Gandhi unilaterally suspended the entire Non-Cooperation movement on February 12, 1922 (Bardoli Resolution). Gandhi was arrested on March 10, 1922, and sentenced to 6 years imprisonment by Judge C.N. Broomfield.

8.3 The Interlude: Swarajists vs. No-Changers & The Simon Commission - **Swarajists vs. No-Changers (1922–1924)**: - *Swarajists (Pro-Changers)*: Led by **Chitta Ranjan Das** and **Motilal Nehru**, argued for entering the legislative councils to "wreck the constitution from within" and expose colonial sham democracy. Formed the Congress-Khilafat Swarajya Party (1923). In 1925, **Vithalbhai Patel** was elected first Indian Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly. - *No-Changers*: Led by Sardar Patel, Rajendra Prasad, C. Rajagopalachari, and M.A. Ansari, opposed council entry and focused on the Constructive Programme (Khadi, anti-untouchability, national education, Hindu-Muslim harmony). - **Simon Commission (1927)**: Formed by the British Government under Sir John Simon to review the Government of India Act 1919. Composed of 7 British MPs and zero Indian members ("All-White Commission"). Greeted across India with black flags and the slogan *"Simon Go Back"*. During a peaceful demonstration in Lahore, **Lala Lajpat Rai** was brutally assaulted by police lathis ordered by SSP Scott; Lajpat Rai died weeks later, declaring: *"Every blow aimed at me is a nail in the coffin of the British Empire."* - **The Nehru Report (1928)**: Prepared by a committee headed by Motilal Nehru in response to Birkenhead’s challenge to Indians to draft a consensus constitution. Recommended: Dominion Status; joint electorates with reservation for religious minorities; a charter of 19 Fundamental Rights; adult suffrage; and linguistic provinces. Jinnah rejected it, presenting his **14 Points** demanding one-third Muslim representation at the center and separate electorates.

8.4 Civil Disobedience & The Salt Satyagraha (1930–1934) - **Lahore Congress (December 1929)**: Under Jawaharlal Nehru’s presidency, Congress passed the historic **Purna Swaraj** (Complete Independence) resolution on the banks of river Ravi; unfurled the tricolor on midnight of December 31, 1929; and celebrated January 26, 1930, as the first Independence Day. - **The Dandi March (March 12 – April 6, 1930)**: Gandhi walked 240 miles from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi on the Gujarat coast with 78 chosen satyagrahis. On April 6, 1930, Gandhi picked up a handful of salt from the beach, defying the British salt monopoly and inaugurating the nationwide Civil Disobedience Movement. - **Pan-Indian Mobilization**: - *Tamil Nadu*: C. Rajagopalachari led the salt march from Tiruchirappalli to Vedaranyam. - *Kerala*: K. Kelappan marched from Calicut to Payyanur. - *North-West Frontier Province (NWFP)*: **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** (*Badshah Khan* / *Frontier Gandhi*) organized the volunteer brigade **Khudai Khidmatgars** (*Red Shirts*), practicing strict non-violence. The Garhwali soldiers under Chandra Singh Garhwali famously refused orders to fire on unarmed Pathan demonstrators at Peshawar. - *Dharasana Salt Works*: Sarojini Naidu, Imam Saheb, and Manilal Gandhi led peaceful marches into the salt depots; satyagrahis were ruthlessly bludgeoned by colonial police, documented worldwide by American journalist Webb Miller. - *Nagaland*: 13-year-old **Rani Gaidinliu** raised the banner of tribal revolt; captured in 1932 and sentenced to life imprisonment (released only in 1947). - **The Gandhi-Irwin Pact & Round Table Conferences**: - *First Round Table Conference (1930)*: Boycotted by Congress; failed to achieve constitutional settlement. - *Gandhi-Irwin Pact (March 5, 1931)*: Signed between Gandhi and Viceroy Irwin; government released political prisoners not convicted of violence and allowed peaceful salt manufacture; Congress suspended Civil Disobedience and agreed to participate in the Second Round Table Conference. - *Karachi Session of Congress (March 1931)*: Presided by Sardar Patel; ratified the Gandhi-Irwin Pact, and passed landmark resolutions on **Fundamental Rights** and the **National Economic Programme** (drafted by Jawaharlal Nehru). - *Second RTC (1931)*: Gandhi attended as sole representative of Congress; deadlocked over minority representation; British refused Dominion Status. - *Communal Award & Poona Pact (1932)*: British PM Ramsay MacDonald announced the Communal Award granting separate electorates to Depressed Classes. Gandhi undertook a fast unto death in Yerwada Jail, Pune. **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** and Gandhi signed the **Poona Pact (September 24, 1932)**, abandoning separate electorates in favor of joint electorates while nearly doubling reserved seats for Depressed Classes from 71 to 147 in provincial legislatures.

Unit 9: Left-Wing Radicalism, Subhas Bose & The Quit India Surge

9.1 Phase II of Revolutionary Socialism: HSRA and Surya Sen - **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA, September 1928)**: Reorganized at Feroz Shah Kotla, Delhi, by Chandrashekhar Azad, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru, explicitly adopting socialism as the ultimate objective of the liberation struggle: - *Saunders Assassination (December 17, 1928)*: Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, and Azad assassinated Assistant Superintendent of Police John Saunders in Lahore to avenge the martyrdom of Lala Lajpat Rai. - *Central Assembly Bombing (April 8, 1929)*: Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt hurled two non-lethal smoke bombs into the Central Legislative Assembly during debates on the repressive Public Safety Bill and Trade Disputes Bill. They showered leaflets quoting French anarchist Valliant: *"It takes a loud noise to make the deaf hear"*, courted arrest willingly to use the courtroom as an anti-imperial propaganda tribunal. - *Lahore Conspiracy Trial & Martyrdom*: In Lahore jail, revolutionaries undertook a historic hunger strike demanding rights as political prisoners. **Jatin Das** perished on the 63rd day of his fast (September 13, 1929). Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru were hanged at Lahore Jail on **March 23, 1931** (*Shaheed Diwas*). Chandrashekhar Azad died in a shootout with police at Alfred Park, Allahabad, on February 27, 1931, keeping his vow never to be captured alive. - **Chittagong Armoury Raid (April 18, 1930)**: Masterminded by schoolteacher **Surya Sen** (*Masterda*) and the Indian Republican Army (Chittagong branch). Revolutionaries including Kalpana Datta, Pritilata Waddedar, and Ambika Chakrabarty raided the imperial police armory, severed rail and telegraph communications, and hoisted the national flag, proclaiming a provisional revolutionary government. Surya Sen fought the Battle of Jalalabad Hills; was captured and executed in January 1934. - **Rise of the Left & Socialist Formations**: - *Communist Party of India (CPI)*: Founded in Tashkent (1920) by M.N. Roy, Abani Mukherji; established in Kanpur (1925) by Satyabhakta. Colonial government tried communist leaders in the Peshawar (1922), Kanpur (1924), and Meerut (1929–1933) Conspiracy Cases. - *Congress Socialist Party (CSP, 1934)*: Formed in Bombay by Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, Ram Manohar Lohia, and Minoo Masani to work within Congress and steer its policy toward democratic socialism. - *All India Kisan Sabha (1936)*: Established at Lucknow with Swami Sahajanand Saraswati as President and N.G. Ranga as General Secretary.

9.2 The Gandhi-Bose Dialectic & The Forward Bloc (1938–1939) - **Haripura Session (1938)**: Subhas Chandra Bose was elected Congress President unanimously. Emphasized planned industrialization, establishing the **National Planning Committee** under Jawaharlal Nehru. - **Tripuri Crisis (1939)**: Bose stood for re-election against the wishes of Gandhi, who fielded Dr. Pattabhi Sitaramayya. Bose won decisively (1580 votes to 1377). Gandhi declared: *"Pattabhi's defeat is my defeat."* Govind Ballabh Pant moved a resolution requiring the President to nominate the Working Committee in accordance with Gandhi's wishes. When Gandhi withheld cooperation, Bose resigned the presidency in April 1939, founded the **Forward Bloc** within Congress, and organized protests against colonial war participation.

9.3 The Quit India Surge (August 1942) - **Context**: Japan entered World War II, conquering Malaya, Singapore, and Burma, reaching India's eastern borders. The British despatched the **Cripps Mission (March 1942)** offering post-war Dominion status and the right of provinces to secede from the Indian Union; Gandhi dismissed it as *"a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank."* - **The Call for Struggle**: On **August 8, 1942**, the All-India Congress Committee met at **Gowalia Tank Maidan, Bombay**, passing the historic **Quit India Resolution**. Gandhi delivered his iconic mantra: **"DO OR DIE — We shall either free India or die in the attempt; we shall not live to see the perpetuation of our slavery."** - **Operation Zero Hour & Leaderless Rebellion**: - In the early hours of August 9, British police arrested all top Congress leaders (Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Azad) under Operation Zero Hour. - Denied central leadership, the movement erupted into a spontaneous, furious mass uprising. Peasants, workers, and youth attacked railway stations, cut telegraph lines, burned police stations, and destroyed imperial infrastructure. - *Underground Resistance Network*: Led by **Jayaprakash Narayan** (who made a daring escape from Hazaribagh Central Jail to organize the guerrilla *Azad Dasta* in Nepal), **Ram Manohar Lohia**, **Aruna Asaf Ali** (who heroically hoisted the tricolor at Gowalia Tank), and **Usha Mehta** (who operated the clandestine *Secret Congress Radio* broadcasting across Bombay). - *Parallel Governments (Prati Sarkars)*: 1. *Ballia (UP)*: Formed under Chittu Pandey for a week in August 1942. 2. *Tamluk (Bengal)*: Formed the *Jatiya Sarkar* (December 1942 – September 1944); established the armed volunteer corps *Bidyut Bahini* and organized disaster relief during the 1943 Bengal famine. Matangini Hazra (73-year-old) was shot dead holding the tricolor aloft. 3. *Satara (Maharashtra)*: Longest-running parallel government (1943–1946) led by Nana Patil and Y.B. Chavan; established *Nyayadan Mandals* (people’s courts) and village credit cooperatives.

Unit 10: Constitutional Progression, Decolonization & The Tragic Partition

10.1 The INA Exploits & The Royal Indian Navy Mutiny - **Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj)**: - First conceived by Mohan Singh with Indian POWs in Malaya; revived and commanded by **Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose** in Singapore (1943). Bose established the Provisional Government of Free India (*Arzi Hukumat-e-Azad Hind*), created the Rani of Jhansi Regiment under Captain Lakshmi Sahgal, and raised the battle cries *"Chalo Delhi"* and *"Jai Hind"*. - The INA fought alongside Japanese forces in Burma, advanced to the Indian border, and hoisted the tricolor at Moirang, Manipur (April 1944), before Japanese logistics collapsed at Imphal and Kohima. - **Red Fort Trials (November 1945)**: The British military court-martialed three INA officers together in the Red Fort: **Major General Shah Nawaz Khan (Muslim), Colonel Prem Kumar Sahgal (Hindu), and Colonel Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon (Sikh)**. The defense was led by Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, and Jawaharlal Nehru. Pan-Indian mass demonstrations erupted across communal lines, forcing Commander-in-Chief Claude Auchinleck to remit their prison sentences. - **Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (February 18–23, 1946)**: Ratings of HMIS Talwar in Bombay struck work protesting racial discrimination, unpalatable food, and the arrest of B.C. Dutt (who scrawled *"Quit India"* on the ship). The mutiny spread to 78 ships, 20 shore establishments, and 20,000 naval ratings across Bombay, Karachi, and Calcutta. Ratings hoisted the Congress, Muslim League, and Communist flags on ship masts. Sardar Patel and Jinnah intervened to negotiate their surrender, but the revolt decisively convinced British authorities that the Indian armed forces were no longer loyal instruments of colonial subjugation.

10.2 The Final Decolonization Sequence (1945–1947) 1. **Wavell Plan and Simla Conference (June 1945)**: Proposed an all-Indian Executive Council except the Viceroy and Commander-in-Chief. Collapsed because Jinnah insisted that only the Muslim League had the exclusive right to nominate Muslim members, which Congress rejected. 2. **Cabinet Mission Plan (May 1946)**: Sent by PM Clement Attlee, consisting of Lord Pethick-Lawrence, Sir Stafford Cripps, and A.V. Alexander: - Rejected a sovereign separate Pakistan; proposed an undivided Union of India with defense, foreign affairs, and communications under the center. - Divided provincial legislatures into three sections/groups: Section A (Hindu-majority: Madras, Bombay, UP, Bihar, CP, Orissa), Section B (Muslim-majority Northwest: Punjab, NWFP, Sindh), and Section C (Muslim-majority Northeast: Bengal, Assam). - Recommended the formation of a Constituent Assembly elected by provincial assemblies, and an Interim Government. 3. **Direct Action Day (August 16, 1946)**: Following disputes over the grouping clause and Nehru’s press conference stating the Constituent Assembly was sovereign, the Muslim League withdrew acceptance of the Cabinet Mission Plan. Jinnah proclaimed Direct Action Day: *"We shall have either a divided India or a destroyed India."* Horrific communal pogroms erupted in Calcutta (**"Great Calcutta Killings"**, leaving over 5,000 dead), spreading to Noakhali, Bihar, and Punjab. 4. **Interim Government (September 2, 1946)**: Formed with Jawaharlal Nehru as Vice-President of the Viceroy’s Executive Council. The Muslim League joined later (October 1946) with Liaquat Ali Khan becoming Finance Minister, deliberately obstructing the government's functioning from within. 5. **Attlee’s Announcement (February 20, 1947)**: British PM Clement Attlee declared that the British Government would transfer power into responsible Indian hands by **June 30, 1948**, and appointed Lord Mountbatten as the final Viceroy to oversee the transfer. 6. **Mountbatten Plan / 3rd June Plan (June 3, 1947)**: Recognizing civil war conditions in Punjab and Bengal, Mountbatten advanced the date of British departure by nearly a year to **August 15, 1947**. The plan accepted partition into two Dominions: India and Pakistan. Legislative Assemblies of Punjab and Bengal voted to partition their provinces; the Radcliffe Boundary Commission demarcated the borders. 7. **Indian Independence Act 1947**: Passed by the British Parliament on July 18, 1947: - Created the sovereign Dominions of India and Pakistan from August 15, 1947. - Terminated British suzerainty over 565 Princely States, releasing them to join either India or Pakistan, or remain independent. - Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (assisted by V.P. Menon) integrated all princely states through the Instrument of Accession, resolving the crises of Junagadh (plebiscite), Hyderabad (**Operation Polo**, September 1948), and Kashmir (signed October 26, 1947).
